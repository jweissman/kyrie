# The aesthetic doctrine

The owner's doctrine (14 November 2026), kept as given. It guides the decisions no ticket can fully specify, in the
playability and aesthetic work alike, and it is the test a design passes or fails when it is otherwise correct.

> Westernesse should feel like inhabiting a mysterious, responsive world. **It should not resign itself to operating
> an interface that describes one.**
>
> **The world must continually repay the player's attention.**

## 1. Begin with an invitation, not an inventory

On entering a place, the player should encounter an evocative, intelligible impression of it. The opening composition
should make clear where they are, establish an atmosphere, and offer something worth noticing or doing.

Don't try to report every available object, person, institution, fact, and affordance in the initial narration. The
three-sentence scene budget is an editorial constraint meant to force selection and composition, not merely brevity.
Prefer one particular, suggestive detail to five generic ones. A shrine with a clerk troubled by its accounts has more
dramatic potential than a shrine described through a list of architectural and institutional attributes.

## 2. Reward attention with actual discovery

Looking, inspecting, searching, listening, questioning, and exploring should reveal meaningfully different aspects of
the situation. The player should have the experience of noticing something, becoming curious, investigating it, and
discovering that the world has more to offer.

Sometimes the reward is knowledge, sometimes material, sometimes a relationship, a new possibility, a danger, or a
consequence. Not everything must be a quest. But repeated thoughtful attention should not usually lead to empty,
interchangeable responses. The encyclopedia, the Book, and the atlas should support these discoveries without replacing
them.

## 3. Make the interface quiet, stable, and intelligible

The painting and scene should dominate. The controls should feel like part of the game rather than a web application
laid on top. Complete the migration to a coherent game-native action language. Remove legacy links and buttons.
Maintain stable visual geometry, including during combat. Lenses should change attention without losing the player's
place.

Promote four meaningful choices when at least four exist; make the rest gracefully accessible. The choice hierarchy
should give the player confidence, not force them to read a long list on every turn.

Use atlas colors precisely and sparingly. They're promises about available opportunities, not decoration. Reveal
secondary information when relevant, requested, or supported by the character's knowledge. Don't continually repeat
the date, time, location, and other unchanged facts.

## 4. Let the world answer the player's actions

After an action, primarily communicate what happened, what changed, and what became possible or newly significant.
Avoid reprinting the whole scene. Avoid generic success messages disconnected from the fiction.

Consequences should persist, and characters should respond in ways consistent with their knowledge, intentions, and
circumstances. This principle applies equally to a small search, a difficult conversation, a tactical decision, and a
major undertaking.

**The player's actions should appear to matter to the world, not merely to a sequence of UI screens.**

## 5. Preserve continuity of place and intention

The player should rarely wonder where they are, how they got there, how to leave, or why an action they've just
selected led to a different interface. Navigation should be spatially credible. The compass should help with real
routes and exits. Undertaking guidance should remain grounded in actual reachable actions.

Combat should feel like a more dangerous state of the same world, not an unrelated application. Character creation
should lead naturally into play. We should not require the player to relearn how to inhabit Westernesse whenever the
activity changes.

## 6. Prefer dramatic density to informational density

A beautiful but empty scene is not enough. Nor is a dense scene filled with undifferentiated actions. Prefer situations
involving a few concrete things whose relationships create interesting possibilities:
- A person wants something.
- An object matters to someone.
- A place has a danger or secret.
- A choice carries a cost.
- An undertaking connects an immediate objective to a wider consequence.

The best scenes contain more potential action than their prose initially explains. This is particularly important for
ordinary adventures: preparation, exploration, discovery, confrontation, survival, return, and recovery should form an
enjoyable sequence.

## 7. Judge complete experiences rather than isolated components

Individual systems improving substantially while sitting down to play still feels disappointing is the failure to
avoid. Use the fast tests, terminal walkers, comparative character runs, and browser checks to identify and eliminate
recurrent defects, and supplement them with genuine editorial review of complete play sequences. Ask of each
representative sequence:
- Did the opening invite curiosity?
- Did the player understand where they were and what they could do?
- Were the promoted choices interesting and distinct?
- Did attending to something reveal worthwhile detail?
- Did the world respond coherently to the player's decisions?
- Did the presentation remain beautiful and unobtrusive?
- Did the experience create a reason to continue?

Test different characters and strategies. Do not satisfy this through one carefully scripted showcase.

## The guiding editorial question

When choosing between competing designs, ask:

> **Does this help the player forget the interface and become interested in what happens next?**

If the answer is no, a design may be technically correct yet aesthetically wrong.

The doctrine guides the finishing of existing objectives; it is not a new feature program. The goal is to make the
substantial world already built genuinely present to the player:

> **I want to sit down with Westernesse and find myself playing because something has caught my imagination, not
> because I'm conscientiously testing the application.**
