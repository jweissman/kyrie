connections proposed below are possibilities, not new canon. The reference game and its fifth expedition come first:
do not populate the opening with this outline or require the wider world for beta.

## A war seen from a doorway

The company begins poor in a small settlement, with a dangerous deep place close enough to return to on foot. At first
the dungeon promises equipment, coin and answers about its own inhabitants, not the destiny of the world. The company
comes back because the next descent might be possible and because people in the town have begun to know them.

Across several seasons a larger conflict reaches that doorway. A power claiming the old Aenean tradition of
*custodia* wants to restore safe roads, maintained defenses and reliable provisions. A rival known at first only as the
Dark Lord wants to break that power's control of the old works and establish rule of his own. Both can save lives;
both can spend them. His violence and appetite for dominion are real, not a misunderstanding for the player to explain
away. The restorationists are worth defending without being entitled to every obedience.

The company does not need to be chosen, royal or unusually virtuous. Its hero's journey is one of widening
responsibility: survive a descent, return to people who depend on the result, make a home worth defending, then decide
what kind of authority to help build or resist. The ending belongs as much to the next morning as to a victory.

### What the corpus gives us

The [history](../imbar/02-history.md) describes the Aeneans as keepers and administrators of older works, not their
makers; whether restoring their empire is desirable is a political question. The [series bible](../imbar/14-bible.md)
keeps the causes of the falls contested. Neither side here gets a final explanation of the empire's disappearance.
The Rì of Dun Rìerdan already keeps the Star-Readers' lamps at a cost that can be seen in
[The Calf in the Tail-Race](../adventures/tailrace/story.md). A proposed restorationist claimant might speak in the
Rì's name; the Rì's actual aims and the extent of that authority remain to be authored, not assumed.

[The Eel Bell](../adventures/the-eel-bell/story.md) and [Windfall](../adventures/windfall/story.md) give the campaign
Water-Wardens, failed gates and people who know how to live with them. [The Leaving Supper](../adventures/leaving/story.md)
gives it Hester Gannick's masterless company and a meal whose value has nothing to do with strategy. What Tessary Owes
gives it soldiers home from a long war with their oaths pledged; Three Nights at Sallowfield gives it a defense
prepared on the ground. These are starting points, not proof that their characters would accept the roles proposed
below.

The [repertoire survey](../adventures/repertoire-2026-11-03.md) identifies a particular absence: epic experienced
through duration, a camp or a family, rather than a summary of armies. It also asks for mutual dependence, practice,
productive failure, made joy and a rival who need not become an ally. This outline puts those experiences before its
strategic explanation.

## The two powers

**The keeping coalition.** A proposed coalition of local rulers, Wardens, traders and a claimant to the Rì's authority
can repair gates, provision a road and put trained people where they are needed. It also requisitions food, presses
workers and treats the upkeep of a tower as reason enough to take a whale. Its officers disagree: a settlement may
trust a Warden and reject an appointed collector. A small fair-minded act by the player should not make the whole
coalition fair.

**The adversary.** The Dark Lord is a distant title, not a monster encountered in every room. His observable program
is to deny the coalition the roads, workers and ancient protections it needs, then make dependent settlements accept
his garrisons and terms. He sends scouts to learn where provisions move, pays local intermediaries, raids a
storehouse, offers protection after a raid and captures a working gate if he can hold it. His troops must eat, carry
orders and defend their own lines; they can lose, bargain, desert or be outmaneuvered. He cannot conjure an army or
know a secret because a chapter needs him to.

His best argument is that inherited protections have become inherited rents. His worst deed is to make the people
who reject his rule pay for that argument. Keep his nature and history uncertain, but not his conduct: the player
should be able to trace a burned storehouse to a commander and learn what that commander was trying to gain. A
proposed field commander, **the Ferryman**, once shared a happy summer of work with a coalition quartermaster; each
remembers it differently. They have a history with each other, not a preassigned history with the protagonist.
Whether either relationship can survive the war follows from play.

The adversary must not turn every existing strange thing into his weapon. The walking orchard has its own reasons;
the calf is not a military omen. His agents may exploit a consequence without having caused it. Nor should the
coalition be the only source of safety: communities have their own skill, courage and interests.

## Form: four seasons, many possible orders

These are **episode proposals**, not a mandatory sequence or a quest list. Each has a local question, a playable
payoff and an aftermath; a larger connection may appear only if the company has grounds to notice it. An episode can
be missed, refused, failed or met after its principal danger. Characters recur only if their circumstances permit.
Where a source adventure is named, preserve its own place, cast, voice and outcomes; a later invitation must read
what actually happened rather than overwrite its ending. A portable casting or a visit elsewhere is a future design
choice, not a claim that the source already takes place in the first town.

Compactification governs the first playable proof: one settlement, old woods and a persistent dungeon with many
entries in one location. Wider places below are later horizons or news from beyond the playable slice, not early map
travel or new nearby provinces. Seasons describe dramatic accumulation, not a requirement to travel, attain a
particular level or complete every episode. Earlier adventures can remain entirely outside the war.

### Season I: The place to return to

*Feeling: curiosity, embarrassment, the warmth of being expected.* The first threat is not an army; it is the
question of whether the company can afford another descent.

1. **The low door.** A damaged entry and tracks in the woods reveal a dungeon without an elder's commission. Its
   first two serious levels have distinct inhabitants, a boss and a treasure cache each, routes around some fights,
   and a companion and basic gear near a secure entry. Deem rolls treasure; supplies carried out matter on the next
   trip. The first boss's absence changes what the second level does, not just who stands in a room.
2. **The supper that went wrong.** Help make a meal for people who have worked beside the company. Choose
   ingredients and revise the dish after tasting; a botched first attempt becomes a joke or a real disappointment,
   not a failed war objective. The Leaving Supper is a model of food as a particular memory, not a recipe to copy.
   The next morning the same people still have work to do.
3. **The difficult friend.** A proposed young rival wants the same salvage for a good, incompatible reason: to keep
   their household in fuel. They may outpace, obstruct or trade with the company and can remain a rival. A shared
   rope or an unpaid favor matters on a later descent; neither friendship nor a romance is a reward for selecting
   the right line.
4. **The ear at the door.** A stranger offers to buy a drawing of the dungeon entrances. The player can refuse,
   sell, mislead or ask what it is for. Evidence about the buyer is incomplete, but the terms and the drawing's
   possible use are legible. If passed on, it can later help a patrol approach; if withheld, scouts must learn
   another way. There is no compulsory betrayal.
5. **The night held in.** Weather or a blocked passage traps a small party together. Borrow the form, not the
   supernatural explanation, of The Light on Scarth Holm. Share heat, set a watch or sit in silence while an
   animal moves outside. The reward is what the player learns to notice and who will trust them with a task
   tomorrow.

### Season II: Work that keeps

*Feeling: competence, attachment, sensuous abundance, the shame of being wrong.* A place is not saved once; it has
to be kept.

1. **The second pour.** A local craftsperson teaches a repair with two independently important qualities, as
   Perpetua Sount's bell has a strike and a hum. Practice is material: observe, attempt, hear or see the result,
   revise where the material permits. A poor result stays in use. If Quillet and Perpetua are later visited, their
   own bell remains their own story, with its own deadline and outcomes.
2. **A day's brightness.** The player helps make a feast, race or small performance and chooses its form with a
   collaborator who has tastes of their own. Let the activity last; no attack interrupts to justify it. Borrow the
   generosity of Half the Hive or Wapshott's without turning either into a recruiting station. The work can be
   awkward and still be enjoyed.
3. **The wrong gate.** With evidence that supports more than one reading, the company may open a sluice, move a
   barrier or leave it alone. One reasonable action can harm a neighbor's field while protecting the road. Show
   the foreseeable risk before the choice and the specific damage afterward. There is no reload-shaped repair:
   apology, labor and altered trust are playable, and the next morning continues. Windfall's gate and pear suggest
   how a practical choice can have more than one human meaning; do not replace its ending with this one.
4. **The road takes its share.** A coalition collector needs grain for a genuine repair crew. A neighboring
   household needs the same grain. The company can escort, negotiate smaller contributions, hide supplies or
   refuse; each changes who eats and which work proceeds. The Rì's lamps and Waric Holle's account of their cost
   make the coalition's claim intelligible without making it absolute.
5. **The empty post.** A patrol is late; its trail leads back into a changed dungeon approach. The company can
   find the patrol, secure a route or return with partial news. In the wreckage are signs of an organized adversary,
   not a speech announcing him. A safe route established here can later serve either evacuation or a troop movement,
   depending on who holds it.

### Season III: The long road comes to us

*Feeling: impatience, fatigue, dependence and anger that has an object.* Soldiers and refugees arrive because
decisions elsewhere have material consequences here.

1. **A company at table.** A masterless band needs shelter and provisions. Hester Gannick's company is one
   possible later encounter if The Leaving Supper's outcome permits it; it does not automatically march here. Its
   captain's remembered meal remains her own need. A proposed local band can instead be cast independently. The
   company chooses how much it can feed, or whom to send elsewhere, then lives among the results.
2. **The drill.** A defense is made in daylight: set obstacles, find water, choose posts, teach frightened neighbors
   a signal and decide which approach must be left open. Sallowfield is the model for preparation rather than a
   promised reenactment. A rival may disagree about the placement and be right. The assault tests the actual
   preparations, including what was neglected.
3. **The ferryman's visit.** An intermediary offers protection on the Dark Lord's terms: a garrison and the use of
   the crossing in return for food and freedom from raids. The threat is credible because his force can carry it
   out, not because his offer controls the plot. A quartermaster recognizes a mannerism or a song from their shared
   summer; the player can listen, exploit it or leave it alone. Neither old affection nor a clever remark compels
   a change of sides.
4. **The wounded morning.** After a raid or a defense, carry water, treat a wound and decide who takes the next
   watch. A friend may misunderstand an earlier choice and refuse help; evidence may correct them, or it may not.
   An unsuccessful defense does not end the campaign: people relocate, supplies run short, and another route or
   ally becomes important. The dead do not return to populate a later scene.
5. **The road under the hill.** Contest a supply route through the persistent dungeon, not a new sequence of
   disposable battlefields. The adversary can scout, fortify, negotiate with inhabitants or cut the route; the
   company can do the same with appropriate means. A pitched fight is one option, not an assumption that all
   inhabitants exist to be killed. A damaged approach changes the next expedition.

### Season IV: The keeping

*Feeling: resolve, grief without pageantry, an earned but incomplete peace.* The three conflicts reach the same
place at once.

1. **Terms in a kitchen.** A coalition officer wants authority to requisition the last reserves; the rival wants
   their household exempt; the company needs supplies for a descent. Private negotiation, concealment, refusal or
   practical redistribution can change the coming defense. There is no final council, vote or hearing.
2. **The crossing.** The Ferryman moves to secure the route while the coalition brings a repair crew. The player
   can hold a bridge, evacuate people, raid provisions or pursue a separate objective. Each consumes time and
   leaves another task to allies or chance. The old friends may meet across the fight, but the encounter is not
   guaranteed and neither must die to make it moving.
3. **The lower work.** A dangerous descent reaches the contested gate's local mechanism. Its inhabitants have
   their own claims; the mechanism has a clear physical operation and limits that can be learned before it is
   touched. Hold it, damage it, give it up or make it inaccessible. No lost capital, world-explaining revelation
   or unique chosen hero is required. The opponent's commander has a plan for the same place and may arrive first.
4. **A place held or lost.** Resolve the fight from actual positions, supplies and prior acts. The coalition may
   hold the settlement and abuse its victory; the Dark Lord's forces may occupy it and still have trouble keeping
   their promises. A third outcome may be evacuation or an uneasy local settlement. Defeating a commander need not
   defeat a distant sovereign, and losing the field need not destroy all prospects of resistance.
5. **Tomorrow's work.** Revisit the meal, the damaged field and the dungeon entrance if they still exist. Share a
   task with the people who remain. A friend may be absent, angry or glad; a defense may have left an occupation
   rather than freedom. Let the player begin a repair or a celebration instead of reading an inventory of every
   person's fate. Further seasons can follow without declaring the war secretly meaningless.

## Timers that cross, rather than a calendar of cutscenes

These are proposed causal clocks, not existing engine behavior. Each advances only on a defined world event or
elapsed in-world time; when the company is elsewhere, actors still act according to their information and means.
Show a player the next observable pressure before it closes a reasonable option. Never start all local deadlines at
character creation or make an episode unwinnable merely because the player chose an unrelated adventure.

| Clock | Starts; visible signal | Advances and intersects | When it expires; player leverage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A relationship** | After the company accepts a shared job or makes a consequential promise; the rival names the next shift. | A missed shift or a kept one changes trust; a requisition or defense can make that same shift impossible. | The rival makes their own choice about the shared resource or post. The player can explain, help, refuse or accept the breach; no automatic reconciliation. |
| **Food and repairs** | A real supply disruption or arrival of a force; stores and the next delivery are visible. | Meals, refugees, raids and requisitions consume or add provisions. A repair crew needs both food and safe access. | A shortfall cancels or delays a specific repair, or forces a named rationing choice. The player can provision, divert, defend or evacuate; time spent doing so costs an opportunity elsewhere. |
| **The approach** | A scout is dispatched after an actual report or observation; signs include tracks or a missing sentry. | Travel takes time. A sold drawing, a discovered entrance or a defended road changes the scout's route and what they can report. | A commander can plan against known approaches, then moves forces through reachable routes. Patrol, deception or killing a messenger may delay or alter the plan, never erase knowledge already delivered. |
| **The gate** | A measured defect, flood or deliberate attack makes a particular mechanism unsafe; a keeper names the sign. | Weather and damage worsen it; people, materials and uninterrupted work improve it. Holding the approach permits repair, while using it for an army may interrupt the work. | It fails, is secured or is seized according to its physical state. A warning, temporary barrier or evacuation can limit harm without pretending to repair it. |

**One possible collision.** A scout reaches the lower entrance because the drawing was sold. The company spends a
day retrieving food rather than intercepting the messenger; that feeds the repair crew but gives the Ferryman time to
choose his route. Requisition for the crew takes the rival's fuel cart, so the rival leaves their defense post.
The company can cover that post, escort the crew or help evacuate, not do all three personally. If it withheld the
drawing, the scout instead spends time finding a route; the food shortage and the rival's needs still exist. A
different sequence can lead to a different intersection, and an absent or dead actor is never revived to fill a beat.

The game must expose concrete signs and consequences, not numerical clocks dressed as omniscience. A missed window
changes the situation; it does not silently invalidate unrelated stories. Outcomes follow from committed actions and
state, including when a faction succeeds offscreen. Simultaneous intentions require a deterministic resolution rule
and replay, not an author choosing a dramatic winner.

## Resistance worthy of the journey

**Every season must offer serious, intelligible danger.** The initial dungeon's two substantial levels each need a
boss, cache, multiple approaches and a way to retreat. Later revisits change occupiers, defenses or routes in response
to events; they do not simply scale every enemy to the party. Deeper resistance should include scouting enemies,
guarded approaches, reinforcements that take actual time to arrive, terrain, supplies and a reason foes might flee,
parley or pursue. A cache is rolled from the relevant Deem tables, not authored as a guaranteed solution.

Give the player information enough to judge danger: tracks, sound, spent supplies, the evidence of a prior failed
expedition. Time, light, wounds and equipment make repeated encounters costly. A prepared ambush should be deadly;
so should an avoidable frontal assault on a defended position. Retreat must remain a real choice, with routes that
can be blocked by causes the player can discover, and casualties or abandoned gear must persist. Victory through
preparation, negotiation, bypass or escape is as valid as killing every opponent.

War fights use the same principles at another scale. A defended crossing has cover, reserves, an escape route and
objectives other than eliminating every combatant. Preparations in the drill change actual positions and options.
Allies have limits and may act on their own objectives. The player can lose a position while saving people, or win
it at a cost that makes the following descent harder. There should be no level-appropriate promise that every
encounter can be won head-on and no compulsory fight against an unkillable adversary.

## Preserving the smaller books

- **A connection is an invitation, not a dependency.** A bell can sound in a later scene if it was made and is
  reachable; a failed bell may bring a different warning. A whale need not survive, nor die, to supply a war clock.
  If a character is absent, use another plausible actor or let the opportunity go.
- **Keep the authors' actual stakes.** Posy wants to name a bell; Tamsin Soames knows her orchard; Hester wants a
  taste of home. None exists to deliver a strategic clue. A source scene retains its humor and quiet even when the
  world beyond it is at war.
- **Let a story end.** Some episodes stop without a settlement, some recur as work, and some are private. Avoid the
  repertoire's moratoria: hearing climaxes, buried predecessor cities, an honest account-keeping companion, an
  ancient-machine explanation for every custom, and a comprehensive fate for every person.
- **Use evidence rather than universal flags.** Knowledge, injuries, people, supplies, access and prior actions are
  the bridges. An independent episode remains comprehensible without another episode's flag; later scenes must
  respect its actual outcomes.

## The turn and Act II: the Count

Act I ends in an act of mercy: lifting the hook from the water behind the measure (`long-mirror.kyr`). Act II begins
because of it. The standing water has seen Rill, and climbs out as three of her (`long-count.kyr`). At first this is a
comedy of manners. One Rill eats a loaf from the wrong end at Lowen's table; one drills ducks at the trough; one sits on
the bank writing sums in the mud. Asked whether there are four of her, each answers in character, and each answer is
also evidence. Their habits and the numbers on their brass discs are rolled by Deem in the cell's template
(`long_cell`, `longCountQuirks`), so no two Swallets meet the same three.

The comedy is a clock. The reflections are counting, and what they were sent to count is Merren, who has kept the
coalition's road ledger for a year. When the last one is unmasked, or after thirty-six watches, the count closes: the
quartermaster is gone and a left-handed reflection sits in her chair writing numbers that go east. This loss is not
avoidable once the count has begun. The player's choice is what Merren leaves behind, and what is done with the thing
in her chair.

### Unmasking, by calling

| Company | Way in | What it costs or risks |
|---|---|---|
| Warrior | watch the hands, then force a spear into a right hand, or spar | a fight with a level-3 gnoll copy; sparring gives it an opening blow |
| Thief | lift the disc from her coat | quiet; spends no fight, but hastens the close |
| Mage | conjure light, and watch her shadow fall the wrong way | needs the spell known and castable |
| Bard | sing Tavi's tune; she sings it back reversed | needs Rill's kin story, or that copy to have answered the "four" question |
| Monk | sit in stillness; she breathes out as you breathe in | thirty minutes off the count's clock |
| Cleric | show the holy sign; she makes it back mirrored | needs the calling |
| Ranger | sees the fourth line of wet prints at once | leads straight to Merren, before the close |
| Anyone | ask the bank copy, look at the floor, show Merren the prints | Merren gives her wheel and ledger; the count still closes |

A copy beaten in a fight drops its disc by a rule, so violence and finesse reach the same evidence by different costs.

### After the turn

- **The reflection in the chair** can be unmade ("Tell her she is not Merren"), stopping the leak; turned with the paper
  boat from Act I, which stops the leak and tells where Merren is held; or left, which feeds the Ash every march.
- **The panes** behind the measure open on the one pane whose number is the sum of the three discs. Each wrong pane
  bites (1d6 cold) and loses the count. The puzzle only opens to a company that has turned over a disc and seen the
  window on its back. Behind the right pane: the counting city, and Merren at a table with her boots off.
- **The Ferryman** comes back to the green. Told what happened, he can be asked to carry a boat east to her.
- **The war of the eight marches** (`long-war.kyr`) begins when Merren is counted. Five of eight marches are held.
  Each two days a march moves by comparing what Act I and Act II actually did: Merren lost, the leak, ledgers sold,
  the cell fed or defied, the bank cleared or occupied, the road guard alive or broken, the crew arrived, the terms
  refused, the reflection turned, the Ferryman's doubts, and whether the Ash's road scout got home or was bought off. Reading Merren's ledger shows the count.
- **Victory** is possible: with the true count read and Merren's wheel (or the turned reflection's word), and at
  least three things done for the West, the company can raise the wheel at the Calx Gate. The Rì's host arrives three (two, if Senn has freed the gate)
  days later, and if it holds eight marches the Ash breaks.
- **Defeat** is possible too: neglect lets the marches fall to none. Swallet is occupied; a garrison stands on the
  green; and the Ash takes a ration a day from the company while it stays there. The rally is no longer offered.

### The hero's journey, beat by beat

| Refusal | the Ferryman's terms: accepting means a garrison, refusing means a raid |

### The five colours in Act II

- **Struggle:** the copies are real level-3 bodies; the war is lost by neglect.
- **History:** Merren's ledger, the paper boats, Tavi's song reversed.
- **Wonders:** water that remembers a face; nine panes of a counting city.
- **Investigation:** hands, prints, habits and discs.
- **Treasure:** the wheel, the discs and the true count, each of which opens something later.

None of these colours is declared: each comes from what the acts teach or hand over.

### Setups for Act III

- Merren is alive behind the glass, at a table in the counting city.
- The Ferryman goes east with a boat, carrying doubt into the Ash.
- The reflection, if turned, is a double agent with a ledger.
- The panes show that the Ash's war is a count: the third act is entering what does the counting.

## What would have to be proved

This outline is not a claim that today's runtime can play the whole campaign. The
[strategy direction](directions-2026-11-19.md) already proposes a bounded deterministic multi-actor spike, with
factions using ordinary world actions and persisted history. The [kits study](adventure-kits-2026-11-26.md) distinguishes
portable roles and causes from each author's prose. Before commissioning a season, prove:

1. **The fifth return:** the compact reference dungeon supports several dangerous expeditions, durable changes,
   a companion and a reason to return to town. This is the prerequisite, not a new strategic layer added to a weak
   opening.
2. **A relationship across mornings:** one shared task, one consequential refusal or error, and a next playable
   morning that reads both without forcing reconciliation.
3. **A small contested route:** two actors with partial knowledge, real supplies and ordinary legal actions compete
   over one route; time, offscreen progress, save/resume and replay preserve the same result. Prove a player can
   encounter the consequence, including when they did not participate.
4. **A defense after preparation:** positions, reinforcements, retreat and survivors are represented as facts, not
   a narrated score. Test both a held and a lost position, then a later return.
5. **One faithful connection:** carry an existing adventure's different outcomes into a later local situation
   without requiring its preferred ending or replacing its voice. If a portable kit is needed, keep the source
   prose in its original casting.

These are research questions and candidate proof slices, not newly filed defects or promises that the language
already supports them. No page controls, Atlas semantics or second strategic simulation are proposed. A strategic
actor has only its actual knowledge, authority and resources; deterministic policies use the same world and rules
as everyone else.

## Reading and limits

This sketch draws on the history and bible, the repertoire and corpus review, the reference-game and
[compactification](direction-2026-11-26-compactification.md) directions, the adventure-kits study, and the cited
adventures' story drafts. The corpus review itself reports thinner readings of several works, including Sallowfield;
this is an outline based on texts and prior research, not a fresh playthrough or a balance assessment. Geography,
character availability after every ending, combat feasibility and the proposed clocks need verification before
implementation. No episode here establishes new canon or claims human acceptance.
