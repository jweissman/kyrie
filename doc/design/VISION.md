# WESTERNESSE
## A History of Emprise
### Creative Vision

*A Seventh Star game, set in the world of Imbar.*

## I. The world is wider than the adventure

A young magician arrives at an inn toward evening. Beyond the village, a road crosses the fields, climbs through a wood, and eventually disappears among the hills. A shepherd has seen lights in the ruined watchtower. The innkeeper knows the story, but has never visited the place. A travelling scholar claims that the tower is older than the kingdom. The scholar may be right.

The magician has a little money, a few spells, inadequate equipment, and perhaps no particular reason to trust anyone.

Tomorrow they might purchase a lantern and set out for the tower. They might find a companion, become involved in a local quarrel, earn money by other means, or follow the scholar to a distant city. Perhaps they never visit the tower at all.

If they do, they may find an abandoned stair leading beneath the earth. It descends past old fortifications, flooded crypts, inhabited caverns, strange machines, forgotten temples, and the works of civilizations so remote that their ordinary purposes have become mysteries.

Somewhere below, a passage may open into an immense subterranean world.

Somewhere beyond the hills are other kingdoms, seas and islands, imperial cities, hidden peoples, armies on the march, and exceptional doors leading out of the material world.

And all these places have their own histories and troubles.

**Westernesse should offer the player the astonishing conviction that an entire world lies beyond the edge of what they know—and the practical freedom to go out and discover it.**

It should be possible to spend one evening clearing vermin from a cellar and another negotiating with an immortal power. Neither should feel like an activity belonging to a different game.

The player enters Imbar as one particular person, but their story is not the world's organizing principle. Kings do not wait for them to arrive before making decisions. Merchants do not exist only to sell adventuring equipment. Strange animals have habitats, ruins have histories, religions have institutions, and ordinary people have obligations, grievances, pleasures, and hopes.

The world should be inexhaustible without being indifferent.

It should continually offer reasons to become curious, and possess enough independent reality to answer intelligently when the player acts.

## II. Emprise

*Emprise* is an old word for an undertaking, an enterprise, a venture of some difficulty or daring.

Westernesse is a history of such undertakings.

Some are modest: finding a lost child, recovering a stolen bell, securing passage over a flooded river, learning a forbidden spell, settling a debt, or investigating a troublesome animal.

Some are extravagant: descending forty levels beneath a ruined fortress, sailing beyond the known archipelagos, discovering a hidden elven city, pursuing an impossible thief through the Infinite Library, or rebuilding an artifact capable of opening the upper House of Doors.

Some are political or intellectual: founding a school, uncovering a falsified royal genealogy, understanding a machine from the Mathematical Age, converting an enemy into an ally, or trying to preserve a civilization whose inhabitants disagree about what preservation means.

Others are absurd. A circus impresario may attempt to pass off a basilisk as an unusually temperamental lizard. A martial clown may discover that the technique of making an enemy ridiculous has genuine magical consequences. A royal menagerie may suffer the escape of animals that nobody living has ever seen.

There should be room for chivalry, scholarship, espionage, comedy, commerce, crime, artistic creation, romance, religious devotion, military adventure, and grand philosophical folly.

Not every undertaking must become heroic. Not every mystery conceals a cosmic conspiracy. A character may acquire private ambitions that no adventure author explicitly prescribed.

The particular pleasure of Westernesse should be that **an undertaking can grow beyond its original meaning**.

A stolen object leads to a village dispute. The dispute exposes an older law. The law sends the player to an archive. An archival discovery transforms the understanding of a dynasty. A decision that once concerned a single household becomes relevant to the politics of a kingdom.

Or perhaps the stolen object is recovered, everyone goes home, and a friendship begins.

The world must be capable of both outcomes.

## III. The old-school art of adventuring

The practical adventure is sacred to the design.

There should be real pleasure in buying provisions, studying a map, choosing equipment, recruiting companions, preparing spells, and deciding whether an expedition is worth the risk.

The world's dangers should be substantial. Monsters need not be balanced to the party's level. An inexperienced magician who wanders alone into an owlbear's territory may die. An ancient red dragon should be an object of terror long before it becomes a plausible opponent.

But old-school difficulty should reward intelligence rather than punish ignorance of the interface.

The player should be able to recognize warnings, make plans, learn from evidence, seek information, discover alternate entrances, recruit help, bargain, deceive, flee, and return better prepared.

A rope, a crowbar, a spyglass, a pack animal, a water-breathing spell, or a knowledge of ancient languages may be more valuable in the right circumstances than an improved sword.

The game should make such usefulness real.

Consider a dungeon containing a broken lift, a flooded passage, a hostile faction, an underground river, and a sealed archive. There should not be one officially designated solution to each obstacle. The player ought to be able to experiment with tools, spells, mechanisms, geography, negotiations, and alliances.

Some experiments should fail. Some should be dangerous. Some should have unanticipated consequences.

The environment should remain coherent enough that a successful plan feels earned.

The great dungeon is not merely a succession of encounters. It is a place to learn.

Its passages form a geography. Its inhabitants possess territories and relationships. Its treasures may be difficult to extract. Its lower levels become increasingly strange, but their strangeness should have some intelligible relation to the history of the place.

An expedition may reveal a shortcut, change the allegiance of a faction, repair a mechanism, open a new region, or make an old route dangerous.

Returning to town is part of the adventure. Treasure can finance further expeditions. Discoveries may lead to new abilities. A bookseller might exchange a rare spell for an ancient manuscript. A companion may develop an opinion about what happened. The town itself may change.

**One expedition should create the desire and means for another.**

A player should be able to enter Westernesse with very little and gradually acquire an increasingly astonishing repertoire of possible actions.

This is not simply advancement through levels. It is the acquisition of capacities, resources, knowledge, relationships, and freedom.

## IV. The material world should resist—and yield

Westernesse is not intended to be a sequence of elaborate fictional scenes with occasional mechanical interruptions.

It is a simulated world in which people, places, objects, and institutions have properties and relationships that remain meaningful independently of the adventures in which they appear.

Wood burns. Stone resists ordinary fire. Rope bears weight and can be severed. Water can be carried, diverted, contaminated, frozen, or used in processes that require it. A cask is both a container and a physical object. A building can be damaged, repaired, occupied, abandoned, or destroyed.

These properties should permit interactions that are not individually scripted for every adventure.

A player who wants to barricade a doorway, cut a bridge, flood a cellar, improvise a crossing, or use a spell in an unexpected way should encounter a world capable of reasoning about the attempt.

The aim is not to reproduce every process of nature at arbitrary resolution. Simulation should be selective, comprehensible, and compositional.

A small number of well-designed causal relationships may produce thousands of interesting circumstances.

And physical consequences should enter the social world.

A burned warehouse means lost goods, endangered inhabitants, interrupted commerce, possible legal proceedings, and perhaps a chance for somebody else to prosper. A bridge repaired for one expedition may later be used by merchants, refugees, or an advancing army.

The same logic applies to people.

A character has knowledge, commitments, beliefs, resources, relationships, temperament, and a present situation. Their actions should follow from these circumstances without reducing their personality to a handful of repeated mannerisms.

A merchant may change prices because a road is blocked. A priest may refuse an apparently generous offer because of a prior obligation. A companion may disagree with the protagonist's methods while remaining loyal to them.

The most important characters need far more than a psychology model. They deserve distinctive literary authorship: particular voices, histories, intellectual commitments, humor, blind spots, desires, contradictions, and the possibility of genuinely surprising change.

**Simulate the circumstances in which people act; write the people with the care of a novelist or dramatist.**

This division should not be absolute. Authored drama and simulation must continually inform one another.

A powerful adventure may begin with a carefully designed political or personal conflict. Its subsequent developments should nevertheless be able to respond to the material world and to actions the author did not anticipate.

The aspiration is not a world that merely appears responsive.

It is a world capable of producing meaningful events that surprise even its creators.

## V. The romance of deep time

Imbar is of immemorial age.

Its contemporary kingdoms are late arrivals in a world repeatedly transformed by empires, migrations, wars, catastrophes, religions, technological accomplishments, and changes in the very understanding of nature.

The Aenean civilization survives in languages, roads, laws, buildings, and ancient machinery. Beneath that historical layer may lie the Mathematical Age, whose works contemporary people experience as magic, though their makers may have understood them quite differently.

The past should be materially present.

A modern road follows the foundations of an imperial causeway. An apparently sacred prohibition once protected a dangerous mechanism. A noble family's title depends on a mistranslated inscription. A river has changed its course because of a machine still operating in a buried chamber.

A scholar may reconstruct the history of a place through architecture, documents, language, tools, ritual, and the testimony of people who have inherited incompatible traditions.

Historical inquiry should be an adventure in its own right.

The pleasure lies not only in discovering that an account was wrong, but in understanding why people believed it, what the belief made possible, and what may change when its foundations are disturbed.

An especially ancient discovery might transform the player's understanding of magic, civilization, or the relationship between the planes.

But no single revelation should exhaust the mystery of Imbar.

There should always be another age beneath the familiar one, another interpretation of an old monument, another tradition whose significance has been forgotten.

**History should be something the player can investigate, misunderstand, exploit, preserve, and sometimes alter—not merely an encyclopedia to read.**

## VI. Philosophy and the strangeness of being

Westernesse should aspire to unusual philosophical depth, but philosophy must not be reduced to clever dialogue or a set of ideological factions.

Different civilizations and orders of existence may embody different understandings of personhood, obligation, time, knowledge, beauty, freedom, and the good.

These understandings should have consequences in their institutions and practices.

A society's conception of memory may shape inheritance, education, justice, and the persistence of personal identity. A religious order's understanding of sacred music may shape its architecture and political life. An elven city's conception of historical continuity may make its inhabitants disagree profoundly about what it means to survive.

A discovery should sometimes challenge not merely the player's opinion of an individual, but the categories through which they understood the situation at all.

The game should not insist that every philosophical problem be resolved through a dialogue contest, or that every conflict eventually yields a comfortable synthesis.

An adventure may produce a genuinely new form of association, a transformation of an institution, an unprecedented practice, or a possibility that none of the original participants could have conceived.

The world should permit invention, not only the selection of alternatives prepared in advance.

The planes offer an especially great opportunity.

The elemental inner planes are not simply combat environments with corresponding damage types. Nor are the proper outer planes merely distant countries populated by unusual monsters.

The Plane of Fire is distinct from Gehenna. The Plane of Air is distinct from Pandemonium. Water, Earth, and the energy planes possess their own orders of existence. Arcadia, Nirvana, the Abyss, Cthell, and the other outer realms should be treated as substantive metaphysical places, with their own histories, inhabitants, structures, and principles.

The House of Doors may provide passage into this greater cosmos. The Infinite Library may contain more geography, scholarship, intrigue, and peril than a mortal traveler could exhaust in a lifetime.

Some planes may challenge familiar assumptions about distance, matter, duration, individuality, causality, or language.

That challenge should be playable.

It may alter navigation, communication, institutions, magic, memory, and the consequences of particular acts.

The inherited cosmology of Imbar—its accounts of the prima materium, the Virina, the Varakiiri, and the Kiritarun—should supply extraordinary possibilities without becoming a single universally reliable explanation.

Priests, scholars, magicians, and travelers may possess incompatible accounts of the same realities. Even accurate traditions may be incomplete.

The player should be able to explore, test, and occasionally overturn what the inhabitants of the world think they know.

**Philosophy should sometimes become geography, magic, political struggle, danger, and practical action.**

And the universe should remain stranger than its inhabitants' explanations.

## VII. The magnificent, the grotesque, and the ridiculous

Westernesse must not become an impeccably tasteful fantasy museum.

It should inherit something of the exuberance of old romances, weird fiction, pulp fantasy, extravagant adventure serials, and tabletop campaigns whose most memorable moments arose from improbable combinations of danger and ingenuity.

There should be enormous monsters and terrible machines, haunted warships, dangerous magical bargains, eccentric sorcerers, magnificent ruins, secret societies, lost kingdoms, grotesque cults, strange transformations, ancient treasures, astonishing escapes, and expeditions that go wonderfully wrong.

A circus may acquire an animal it cannot safely exhibit. An alchemist may invent something they cannot reverse. An imperial observatory may have spent centuries attending to a star that no longer exists.

A foolish undertaking may become heroic. A heroic undertaking may become ridiculous.

The game should be capable of spectacle without becoming monotonously spectacular.

Not every marvel needs to symbolize a philosophical doctrine. Not every dragon needs a tragic interior life. Sometimes the pleasure is simply that a dragon exists, is very large, is exceptionally dangerous, and has amassed a fortune that several generations of reckless adventurers have failed to steal.

There is room for outrageous adventure and for the sober beauty of ordinary life.

A difficult political conversation may follow an absurd theatrical performance. A terrible discovery may occur beneath a cheerful market. An old soldier may be capable of both moving testimony and execrable jokes.

The greatest moments of wonder need quieter surroundings in which to become wonderful.

**Intellectual seriousness and imaginative extravagance should strengthen one another, not cancel each other out.**

An astonishing premise gives thought something strange to work upon. Causal rigor makes an outrageous exploit more satisfying. Literary intelligence gives an absurd event particular human significance.

The game should sometimes make the player laugh aloud, sometimes feel frightened, sometimes experience the pleasure of solving an ingenious problem, and sometimes remain haunted by a person or discovery long after closing it.

## VIII. Civilizations as living accomplishments

Westernesse should contain genuinely great cities and societies.

The City of Seven Towers should be more than a collection of adventure locations. It should have neighborhoods, markets, palaces, temples, theaters, libraries, magical colleges, courts, workshops, households, political factions, and histories.

Its inhabitants should be occupied with lives that cannot all be understood through the protagonist's immediate interests.

A bookseller may possess a private collection with a complicated provenance. A theater company may be entangled in a succession dispute. A scholar's theory may offend a religious institution for reasons that are not wholly foolish. A guild may preserve a craft that depends upon a magical resource imported from a distant plane.

The royal palace should become a great campaign setting in its own right.

Members of the royal family possess different obligations, ambitions, loyalties, and understandings of the kingdom. Their personal arcs may lead the protagonist through the capital's institutions and onward into the wider world.

A disputed succession may arise from genuine historical and constitutional conflict, not simply from the discovery that one claimant is secretly wicked.

The player might become a royal protector, support a cadet branch, serve a rival kingdom, acquire a military command, or refuse the court's authority altogether.

Other civilizations should be as particular.

A hidden elven city might possess extraordinary artistic and intellectual traditions, ancient houses, rival conceptions of duty, and institutions built upon an understanding of memory and collective life unlike that of surrounding peoples.

The discovery of such a city should itself be an immense undertaking. A prolonged stay should reward familiarity with its neighborhoods, inhabitants, arts, politics, and histories.

If catastrophe threatens it, the danger should have emotional weight because the player has encountered something worth preserving.

Its destruction might be possible without the destruction of its people. Its survival might require transformations that some inhabitants find intolerable.

The game need not guarantee a happy ending. It should permit meaningful acts of rescue, preservation, resistance, compromise, invention, and remembrance.

Elsewhere are maritime imperia, island kingdoms, mercantile republics, great forests, deserts, dwarven fastnesses, subterranean societies, and civilizations formed by histories the player has not yet learned.

These should not be interchangeable cultural costumes.

They should differ in geography, institutions, material practices, historical inheritance, conflicts, ways of life, and possibilities for adventure.

## IX. The world at war

The strategic dimension of Westernesse should be real.

Kingdoms possess territories, roads, rivers, ports, fortifications, resources, armies, fleets, institutions, and political interests. They can pursue plans independently of the protagonist.

War is not simply a sequence of authored battles.

Armies march. Supply lines stretch and fail. Fortresses hold out. Roads become unsafe. Refugees move. Prices change. Agents gather intelligence. Commanders misjudge terrain or one another. Political factions disagree about what should be sacrificed.

The player should encounter these consequences long before they possess any military authority.

A bridge may first matter because it provides a route to a dungeon. Later it may become the means by which a besieged city receives supplies. Still later, the player may need to decide whether destroying it would save one province at the expense of another.

The same geographical world should support all three decisions.

The larger campaign may concern a formidable adversary threatening the successor states of an ancient imperial alliance. Its armies should be capable of actual strategic movement. Its commanders should possess objectives, resources, information, and disagreements.

As the protagonist grows in power and influence, they may acquire the ability to intervene at increasing scales.

They might protect a caravan, organize an expedition, recruit a company, secure a pass, command a regiment, direct an army, negotiate an alliance, or plan an entire campaign.

At the highest levels, the world map becomes a strategic instrument as well as a travel map.

There should be genuine pleasures of maneuver, logistics, reconnaissance, supply, fortification, concentration of force, and the exploitation of terrain—something of the intellectual engagement of chess and historical strategy games, without severing the connection to individual lives and adventures.

A siege must remain a siege of a real city, inhabited by people with their own histories and possible futures.

The player may command its armies, infiltrate its defenses, negotiate its surrender, rescue someone trapped inside, or sail away to attend to an entirely different concern.

**Grand strategy should enlarge the possible scale of action without replacing the RPG.**

The world is not obliged to make the player a general, or even a hero. But such futures should exist, and the path from an ordinary traveler to a figure of political or military consequence should feel earned.

Nor should victory in a great war exhaust the political story.

What happens to the soldiers afterward? How are devastated provinces rebuilt? Which new institutions emerge from the campaign? What does peace mean to people whose livelihoods, identities, or ambitions were formed by war?

The formation and transformation of civilization should be as interesting as its defense.

## X. Literature that can be inhabited

The most important authored adventures should possess genuine dramatic form.

We want distinct scenes, memorable places, particular voices, sustained relationships, meaningful discoveries, reversals, foreshadowing, and endings that transform what came before.

A small adventure need not be long, but it should have substance. A major adventure should not consist of accepting a task, traveling to a location, defeating an enemy, and returning for a reward with only increased prose length distinguishing it from a minor one.

Characters should act because of their motives and circumstances. Their beliefs should sometimes be intelligent and persuasive even when the player disagrees. They should change for reasons beyond having been presented with a successful argument.

There should be room for romance, philosophical difficulty, comedy, grief, tenderness, obsession, courage, vanity, cruelty, and the pleasures of ordinary conversation.

A companion may become important through years of shared events. An old enemy may reappear in a changed position. A sentence spoken casually in an early adventure may acquire significance much later.

A persistent world makes a special literary possibility available: **later events can transform the meaning of experiences the player genuinely remembers having**.

This ambition should influence how adventures are authored.

Authors ought to design situations with meaningful histories and possible developments, not only scripts that advance when the protagonist selects the expected action.

At the same time, the simulation must not dissolve every authored scene into anonymous procedural language.

An exceptional character should sound like themselves. A carefully written revelation should retain its force. A comic exchange should have timing and specificity.

Procedural composition can enrich and vary the circumstances of a literary work. It should not be mistaken for literary authorship itself.

The goal is a medium in which independently authored adventures can coexist in one continuous world, share institutions and geography, recognize one another's consequences, and sometimes create unexpected relationships between stories.

## XI. One life among innumerable possibilities

The player begins as one person.

They should not be required to assemble a party of abstractions before entering the fiction. Other people may become companions through encounters, affinities, practical arrangements, old obligations, and shared experience.

Different protagonists should encounter different opportunities.

A thief may recognize an entrance that a warrior overlooks. A scholar may discover significance in a passage of ancient language. A magician may recognize an environmental transformation that others cannot explain. A fighter may make a dangerous journey possible through their ability to protect the company.

Over time the protagonist may acquire many other capabilities.

They might learn languages, master schools of magic, obtain extraordinary tools, develop friendships, purchase property, command a ship, join a philosophical or religious institution, become famous as an explorer, establish a trading concern, serve a court, acquire military authority, or undertake voyages through the planes.

Such advancement should not merely unlock a larger set of numerical encounters.

It should open different relationships to the world.

The scholar who began by copying inscriptions may eventually direct an archaeological expedition. The poor magician may become a formidable metaphysical investigator. The obscure mercenary may lead legions. A performer may become entangled in the politics of a royal court.

Yet none of these lives should become compulsory.

A powerful protagonist should still be able to visit an old friend, attend a festival, investigate a local mystery, return to a familiar dungeon, or spend an evening studying an unusual book.

The accumulated circumstances of a character's life should provide their own motives.

And the world should possess more possibilities than any one character can exhaust.

## XII. The experience must live up to the world

Westernesse's ambitions will mean little if the player cannot readily experience them.

The game must be beautiful, comprehensible, responsive, and inviting.

The isometric world map should make geography a pleasure to explore. Towns should contain recognizable people, useful services, interesting circumstances, and clear ways into adventure. Interiors should have intelligible spatial structure. Danger should be recognizable; consequences should be accurately reported.

The interface should not resemble an administrative dashboard for the simulation.

It should bring forward what matters in the current situation.

When traveling, the player attends to geography. When speaking, to a person. When investigating, to a thing and its properties. When fighting, to danger and tactical possibility. When studying an old book, to its language, meaning, and mysteries.

There should be an attractive economy of attention: clarity without impoverishment, detail without clutter, mystery without accidental obscurity.

Actions should possess understandable costs. Routes should have meaningful durations and resource requirements. Searching should sometimes discover things beyond the explicit needs of an authored quest. Equipment should permit useful actions. Companions should be available through the fiction and matter in practice.

Above all, the game must provide a reliable rhythm of desire and fulfillment.

The player hears of something worth investigating, forms a plan, acts, discovers, encounters resistance, returns with consequences, and acquires new possibilities.

That is not the whole artistic ambition. It is the means by which the larger world becomes accessible.

Our first great achievement should be a genuinely enjoyable continuing adventure: a town worth returning to, a wilderness worth exploring, a deep site worth understanding, useful companions and equipment, meaningful danger, and discoveries that lead naturally to further undertakings.

A game that promises innumerable planes and civilizations must first make it enjoyable to walk out of an inn.

Once that experience is reliable, the same underlying world can sustain ever greater adventures without losing its identity.

## XIII. The promise of Westernesse

We should be able to imagine a character beginning with a lantern, a few coins, and an uncertain rumor.

They descend beneath an abandoned tower, discover something extraordinary, and return to a town where the right person knows enough to be alarmed by it.

The discovery leads to another expedition. The expedition changes a relationship. A relationship opens a city. In the city, the protagonist learns of old wars, strange religions, distant kingdoms, and arguments about the nature of the universe.

Years later they may stand in a royal council, command an army at a mountain pass, sail among unfamiliar islands, or pass through the House of Doors into a reality where their earlier assumptions no longer hold.

Or they may never do any of those things.

They may become a scholar, join a circus, pursue a romance, acquire a ship, devote their life to a deep dungeon, or simply continue taking the roads that interest them.

The world should be spacious enough for all of these lives, and coherent enough for their consequences to persist.

It should be dangerous enough to reward ingenuity, strange enough to sustain wonder, intellectually ambitious enough to challenge understanding, and extravagantly imaginative enough to astonish.

It should possess the practical pleasures of the old-school expedition, the dramatic pleasures of literature, the freedom of a causal simulation, and the vast perspective of history, philosophy, and strategy.

And it should never forget the ordinary human and inhuman lives amid all this grandeur: the miller, the soldier, the child, the scholar, the clown, the priest, the old friend, the failed magician, the creature making its home in the ruins.

**Westernesse is a world to venture into, a world to understand, a world to act upon, and a world that acts back.**

Its beginning is a road out of town.

There should be no telling where that road might lead.
