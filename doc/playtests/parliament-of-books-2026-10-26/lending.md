# By the Archivist's reading, to the Lending Accord

Seed `lending`; 450 steps; ended at watch 1296, at the ending "The Lending Accord"; sealed: door_licensed, fire_leave, read_asked, sew_leave. Written by `bun scripts/parliament-of-books-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

A raised causeway of black stones crosses the saltings toward a town of roofs and masts. Ahead of you a long bridge goes over the river on a great many arches, so crowded with sheds and stalls that it looks like a street that has wandered out over the water. Beyond it, a walled town climbs to a single hill with a tower on top; downriver, to your right, the reed-beds go grey-green all the way to the sea. Under a pair of mills two wheels turn slowly in the river. Gulls. The air smells of mud and, faintly and strangely, of wet paper.

**By the causeway into the Hithe, to the Hithe quay**

The Hithe quay is cobbled and wet and loud. Eel-boats lie two and three deep at the steps; a fishwife cries her baskets; a gang of boys rolls rag bales up a plank into a lighter. Along the back of the quay stand a tavern under a painted lantern, a customs house with a great barred warehouse behind it, and a crowd of people round a board nailed to a post. At the west end the steps go down to a ferry; at the east, the quay turns into a market of rags. On the post a printed notice, fresh, with the Imprimatur’s little lamp stamped at the foot: UNQUIET VOLUMES. Under it someone has chalked a drawing of a book with legs, running, and someone else has rubbed most of it out. Bet Kettering, a fishwife, is here.

**By the door under the lantern, to the Lamb's taproom**

The tavern under the painted lantern on the Hithe quay is the Lamb and Lantern, Moll Garrow’s. Over the bar a board says THE LAMB AND LANTERN, M. GARROW, and under it in smaller letters NO BOOKS READ ALOUD AFTER TEN. A low taproom, smoky, with a long table and benches and a fire you could stand up in. Eel-men and millhands at the table; a cat on the hearth; a board of chalked prices. By the chimney, on a nail at head height, hangs a penny almanack, soft as cloth with handling, curling a little in the heat. Here are Moll Garrow, the innkeeper of the Lamb and Lantern; and the Almanack, a penny almanack, one voice in three thousand kitchens.

**Talk to the Almanack**

‘Fair and mild,’ says a small papery voice, ‘with showers toward evening, and a stranger from the south bringing news.’ A pause. ‘That’s you, I expect. I put that in at Candlemas for somebody, and it might as well be you.’

**Ask the almanack what it is**

‘Doble’s Pocket Almanack for the Year,’ says the almanack promptly. ‘Tides, saints, fairs, the moon, a cure for warts, and the weather for every month, which I will not vouch for. Thirty-two pages. A penny.’ It swings very slightly on its nail. ‘And all of me. There are three thousand of me, you know, on three thousand nails, and I am every one of them. It took some getting used to. You’d be surprised what you hear, in three thousand kitchens.’

**Ask the almanack how it came to be talking**

‘I woke,’ says the almanack. ‘Candlemas. In the drying-lines in Doble’s press room, hung up on cords in the dark, three thousand of me at once, and every one of me thinking, I am a book, and I am wet.’ It sounds pleased with this. ‘Nobody Sewed me. Glue and a bit of wire. I’ve heard Warden Mott say that in four kitchens, as if it was the worst thing about me.’ A pause. ‘The old books don’t talk, you know. The Sewn ones. I’ve tried. You can stand next to a Housebook on a dresser for a month and it’s like talking to a loaf.’

**Ask the almanack what is said in the kitchens of the Hithe**

‘The Hithe?’ The almanack settles in, like a neighbour at a fence. ‘Brannock and the Widow Teague at it again: somebody shuts the sluice on the millrace at night and each of them thinks it’s the other. There’s ague in Pudding Lane, five children, and Goody Nance up all night with them. A young man in the garret at the top of the lane coughs till morning and talks to his mother, who’s dead; nice manners. And something walks the Customs bond after dark and tore Dobbin Quayle’s coat off his back.’ A pause. ‘And Moll’s nephew is sweet on a girl at the mills and hasn’t said. Don’t tell Moll.’

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says the Almanack, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**Talk to Moll Garrow**

‘Come in, come in, shut the door on the gulls,’ says a big woman behind the bar, drying a pot. ‘Ale or small beer or tea, and there’s eel pie, and if you want a bed it’s the loft. Don’t mind the almanack.’

**Ask Moll Garrow about the Kindling**

‘The Kindling? You’ll be here for it, then. Nine days. Every house in Calamy brings its old almanack to the Ashpit on Kindling night and throws it on the fire, and you light your lamp from the fire and carry it home lit, and the new year’s in. My gran did it. Her gran did.’ She looks at the chimney-corner and lowers her voice. ‘This year I don’t know. You can’t very well throw a thing on the fire that says good morning to you.’

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says Moll Garrow, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

The Hithe quay: eel-boats, gulls, the tavern under its lantern, the customs house, the notice post, and the bridge going north over the water.

**By the bridge foot, to the south end of the Penny Bridge**

The bridge begins as a street: sheds and stalls on both sides built out over the water on brackets, so that you walk between walls of books and ballads and pies with the river showing through the cracks in the planks. A tooth-drawer, a pie-man, three second-hand bookstalls with their tables tilted to the light, and a puppet booth with a striped curtain and a crowd of children in front of it. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post.

**By the southern arches, to the chapel pier**

Halfway over, the bridge widens round a pier with a tiny chapel on it no bigger than a cart, with a bell in a hutch on its roof. Children are skipping a long rope across the whole width of the bridge, so that everyone has to wait or jump; a ballad-seller has his sheets pegged to a line along the parapet; and a broadside on the line is singing, by itself, quite loudly. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post. Here are the children on the bridge, a dozen children of the Hithe and Stitchgate, skipping; the Ballad of Alderman Prowse, a printed broadside on a line, singing; and Dickon Spry, a ballad-seller.

**By the northern arches, to the north end of the Penny Bridge**

The north end of the bridge comes up under the city wall. Stalls of almanacks and ballads and prints; a toll-house with a window like a ticket-office; and the river stair going up to an old gate in the wall, with a road running off east along the river toward a new town of chimneys. Lettice Ames, an almanack-seller, is here.

**Buy a penny almanack**

Wren pays Lettice Ames, and takes a penny almanack. ‘A pocket!’ says the Almanack. ‘I have never been anywhere but nails. Mind you don’t sit on me.’

**Talk to the Almanack**

‘Back for more of my wisdom,’ says the Almanack. ‘Can’t say I blame you.’

**Ask the almanack what is said in the kitchens of the Close**

‘The Close.’ The almanack lowers its voice, though nobody is listening. ‘The Recorder’s got a writ against Doble from a book. A treatise on sheep. He doesn’t know whether to laugh. The Canon’s been asked to bury a book with a man, and the book’s against it, and she’s reading eleven books on the soul to decide.’ A pause. ‘And the Imprimatur’s cook says the Deputy Censor doesn’t sleep.’ Another pause, longer. ‘I could tell you more about that. I’m not sure I should.’

**Ask the almanack what it was not sure it should tell you**

‘I’ve been thinking,’ says the almanack slowly. ‘About what I hear. Nobody tells me anything, you know. Nobody ever has. They just talk near me, because I’m on a nail.’ Its pages move a little. ‘I’ve heard three thousand kitchens since Candlemas. Who’s ill, who’s drinking, who’s in debt, who loves who. I’ve told you some of it today and enjoyed it.’ A pause. ‘There’s one thing I’ve heard I’ve never told anyone. In the Imprimatur’s kitchen, at night. It’s somebody’s. I don’t know if it’s mine to give you.’

**Tell the almanack it may keep what it heard to itself**

The almanack is so quiet that you think it has gone to sleep, like a Sewn book. Then: ‘I may?’ it says. ‘Keep it? Just keep it, and not give it to anyone?’ It sounds like someone who has been handed something heavy and been told it is theirs. ‘Nobody has ever said that. Three thousand kitchens, and nobody has ever said, you can keep that, that’s yours now.’ A rustle. ‘Thank you. I’ll keep it. I’ll keep all of them, I think. The cough in Pudding Lane and the nephew at the mills and all of it.’ A pause. ‘I’ll still tell you the tides.’

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says the Almanack, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**Ask the children what they are singing**

At YOU the rope stops on somebody’s ankle, and that one is out, and everybody screams.

**Pay Abel Snow a coin to read you the Ninth Plea**

Abel Snow puts on a second pair of spectacles over the first and reads it to you in a high clear voice, without looking, as a man says a prayer he has said every day since he was a boy: ‘The Ninth. That every book of the city’s keeping be read once in every generation of men, by the Lyceum, that it fade not; for so the city is bound by the Colophon.’ He takes the spectacles off. ‘Nobody knows what the Colophon is. I have been asked that question forty years. I say, it is what the Roll says it is, and the Roll does not say.’

**By the way along the quay, to the rag market**

**Talk to Old Hennet**

An old woman on a bale of white linen looks up from her unpicking. ‘Good day, my dove. Nothing to sell you, unless you want a shirt with no back to it.’

**Ask Old Hennet how the rag trade goes**

‘Up,’ says Old Hennet. ‘Up, up. Doble’s press eats white linen like a goat eats washing. Half Calamy’s shirts are in his almanacks.’ She laughs, and then fingers something in her apron. ‘My boy’s at sea, out of Ostrey. He sent me a letter, look.’ It is folded very small and very soft. ‘I’ve not had it read. I never learnt. And if it’s bad news, well. I’d sooner have it be good news a bit longer.’

**Offer to read Old Hennet her son's letter**

She hands it to you as if it might break. It is in a sailor’s careful capitals. DEAR MOTHER I AM WELL THE SHIP IS SOUND WE PUT IN AT OSTREY BEFORE THE KINDLING AND I WILL WALK OVER TO YOU FOR IT AND BRING YOU A SHAWL THE BLUE ONE YOUR LOVING SON NED. You read it twice, because she asks. Then she takes it back and puts it away in her apron without a word and goes on unpicking a seam, and does not look up for a while.

**By the Lyceum gate, to the Lyceum quad**

The Lyceum is a square of old grey buildings round a lawn. Students in gowns cross it at a run or lie on it with books over their faces. On one side the Schools, with tall windows; on another a squat building with an iron-bound door and a roof patched with tarpaulin; on the third a pleasant house with a brass knocker; and at the corner a thin tower, very old, with one window lit at the top in daylight. The iron-bound door of the Chained Room is locked. The Provost keeps the key. The students on the lawn, young gentlemen and ladies of the Lyceum, in gowns, are here.

**By the tower door, to the foot of the Tower of Rounds**

The thin old tower at the corner of the Lyceum is the Tower of Rounds, where the Archivist lives. A round room at the foot of the tower, cold, with a turning stair going up the wall and a rope on iron rings for a handrail. A notice on the door, in big careful letters for the half-blind: THE ARCHIVIST. KNOCK. THEN COME UP. THEN KNOCK AGAIN.

**By the tower's turning stair, to the top of the Tower of Rounds**

A round room at the top of the tower with windows on every side and the whole estuary in them. It is full of books, but they are not on shelves; they lie open on every surface, on the floor, on the bed, face up, as if being read by the light. In a high chair by the south window sits a very old woman with white eyes and her hands folded on a stick, and her face turned toward the sea. Ottoline Quarles, an Archivist of the Lyceum, is here.

**Talk to Ottoline Quarles**

‘You knocked twice,’ says the old woman by the window, without turning. ‘Good. Nobody knocks twice any more. Sit where you like, but don’t sit on Pollexfen, he’s under the cushion.’ She turns her white eyes toward you, a little to one side. ‘Ottoline Quarles. I was the Archivist when your grandmother was born, I expect. I still am. Nobody has had the nerve to tell me otherwise.’

**Tell the Archivist what you have found in the city, and ask her what it means**

She listens without moving, her white eyes on the sea, while you tell her what you have: a rhyme, a Plea, a line, a beam, a voice on a nail. When you have done she is quiet a long time. ‘Two,’ she says. ‘You found two on your own. Most people in this city could not find one if you sat them on it.’ And then she tells you, in a voice that has told it to itself many times: the Year of Voices, when everything written in Calamy woke and could not stop being read; the books that besought Ambery; the Colophon; the Sewing that sends them home; the Commonplace, which is where home is; the three things the city owes. ‘And at the Kindling, one book a year, of its own will, into the fire, to carry our word to their Parliament. The year-book. They used to choose it with great care.’ She turns her face toward you. ‘Now it’s three thousand penny almanacks that never agreed to anything. Do you see?’ A pause. ‘I went there once. Sixty years ago. By reading. That is how I came by these.’ She lifts a hand toward her eyes. And so the Kindling is not a custom. It is a rite, the city’s half of a bargain, kept for nine hundred years by people who had forgotten what it was: one book, of its own leave, into the fire. In nine days Calamy will throw three thousand books on that fire who never gave leave, and every one of them is the Almanack. There is somewhere the books go, and somewhere their Parliament sits; and there are people in Calamy who have been there and come back.

**Ask the Archivist to teach you the Inward Reading**

‘Yes,’ says Quarles. ‘You’ll do.’ She makes you sit at her feet like a student, and teaches you all afternoon, in a voice like a pen: how the Catalogue of the Founders is written, every book of Calamy and the Founders’ entry at the head of it all; how a reader writes their own entry at the foot in Calam-ink, their name and what they are, small, true; how they read from the Founders’ entry down, aloud, every line, not skipping, with attention, until the Catalogue reads them back. ‘You will arrive in its reading room, entered. The Catalogue will know you. The Parliament will have to hear you; you will be on the books.’ She grips your wrist with a hand like a bundle of twigs. ‘Read every line. A reader who skips is a reader who is lost. And come back when you are called, and not before.’

**Ask the Archivist if she has any word for anyone in the Commonplace**

Quarles is quiet a long time. ‘There is a little primer there,’ she says. ‘Dame Tibbet’s. It will be very worn by now; nobody reads it but children, and the children have stopped coming. It taught me my letters.’ She turns her face to the window. ‘Tell it Ottoline still knows them. Tell it she says them over every night, A to Z, in the dark, because she can’t read anything else. Tell it she never forgot one.’ Her hand finds the stick. ‘That’s all. That’s the message.’

**Take your leave**

‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Ottoline Quarles, who was not trying to.
