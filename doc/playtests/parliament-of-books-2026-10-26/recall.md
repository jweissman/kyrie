# By Crake's back stacks, to the Recall

Seed `recall`; 303 steps; ended at watch 1296, at the ending "The Recall"; sealed: door_shut, fire_leave, read_rounds, sew_all. Written by `bun scripts/parliament-of-books-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

A raised causeway of black stones crosses the saltings toward a town of roofs and masts. Ahead of you a long bridge goes over the river on a great many arches, so crowded with sheds and stalls that it looks like a street that has wandered out over the water. Beyond it, a walled town climbs to a single hill with a tower on top; downriver, to your right, the reed-beds go grey-green all the way to the sea. Under a pair of mills two wheels turn slowly in the river. Gulls. The air smells of mud and, faintly and strangely, of wet paper.

**By the causeway into the Hithe, to the Hithe quay**

The Hithe quay is cobbled and wet and loud. Eel-boats lie two and three deep at the steps; a fishwife cries her baskets; a gang of boys rolls rag bales up a plank into a lighter. Along the back of the quay stand a tavern under a painted lantern, a customs house with a great barred warehouse behind it, and a crowd of people round a board nailed to a post. At the west end the steps go down to a ferry; at the east, the quay turns into a market of rags. On the post a printed notice, fresh, with the Imprimatur’s little lamp stamped at the foot: UNQUIET VOLUMES. Under it someone has chalked a drawing of a book with legs, running, and someone else has rubbed most of it out. Bet Kettering, a fishwife, is here.

**By the bridge foot, to the south end of the Penny Bridge**

The bridge begins as a street: sheds and stalls on both sides built out over the water on brackets, so that you walk between walls of books and ballads and pies with the river showing through the cracks in the planks. A tooth-drawer, a pie-man, three second-hand bookstalls with their tables tilted to the light, and a puppet booth with a striped curtain and a crowd of children in front of it. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post.

**By the southern arches, to the chapel pier**

Halfway over, the bridge widens round a pier with a tiny chapel on it no bigger than a cart, with a bell in a hutch on its roof. Children are skipping a long rope across the whole width of the bridge, so that everyone has to wait or jump; a ballad-seller has his sheets pegged to a line along the parapet; and a broadside on the line is singing, by itself, quite loudly. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post. Here are the children on the bridge, a dozen children of the Hithe and Stitchgate, skipping; the Ballad of Alderman Prowse, a printed broadside on a line, singing; and Dickon Spry, a ballad-seller.

**Talk to the children on the bridge**

The rope stops. A dozen faces turn to look at you, and a girl with a missing front tooth says, ‘You’re not from here,’ as if it were a crime.

**Ask the children what they are singing**

‘The Kindling rhyme,’ says the girl with the gap, scornfully. ‘Everybody knows the Kindling rhyme.’ And to show you, they start the rope again, and chant it, slapping their feet down on the planks: SEW it UP and SEND it HOME, READ it ONCE and LEAVE a-LONE, BURN it IF it ASKS you TO, KINDling KINDling ONE for YOU! At YOU the rope stops on somebody’s ankle, and that one is out, and everybody screams.

**Take your leave**

‘Come back and tell me how it goes!’ call the children on the bridge after you.

**By the northern arches, to the north end of the Penny Bridge**

The north end of the bridge comes up under the city wall. Stalls of almanacks and ballads and prints; a toll-house with a window like a ticket-office; and the river stair going up to an old gate in the wall, with a road running off east along the river toward a new town of chimneys. Lettice Ames, an almanack-seller, is here.

**By the road along the river wall, to Newmarket**

Outside the wall the town is new and loud: a broad street of printers’ shops with their windows full of ballads and proclamations and almanacks pegged on strings, ink-boys running with bundles, a smell of hot metal and size. At the end of it a cart arch opens into a yard stacked with paper bales, and over the arch, in letters a foot high: DOBLE. PRINTER TO THE CITY.

In the black middle men are building a bonfire of faggots and old timber, a great cone of it, higher every day, for the Kindling.

**By the Court's doors, to the hall of the Court of Pleas**

A high hall of dark wood, with a bench at one end under a canopy, and benches for the parties, and benches for the public, mostly empty. Behind the bench, on a wooden drum as big as a cart wheel, the Great Roll of Pleas is wound, a vast scroll of yellowed skin, unrolled a little way across a table and chained there. On the plaintiff’s bench, all alone on a red cushion, lies a small thin book with a woodcut of a ram on its cover. The Great Roll lies chained across the table, unrolled at the Pleas. Here are Recorder Haddon, a Recorder of the Court of Pleas; Abel Snow, a clerk of the Court; and Pask’s Treatise on the Diseases of Sheep, a small thin printed book on a red cushion; the plaintiff.

**Pay Abel Snow a coin to read you the Ninth Plea**

Abel Snow puts on a second pair of spectacles over the first and reads it to you in a high clear voice, without looking, as a man says a prayer he has said every day since he was a boy: ‘The Ninth. That every book of the city’s keeping be read once in every generation of men, by the Lyceum, that it fade not; for so the city is bound by the Colophon.’ He takes the spectacles off. ‘Nobody knows what the Colophon is. I have been asked that question forty years. I say, it is what the Roll says it is, and the Roll does not say.’

**By the Gannet Steps up from the Close, to the Gannet Steps**

A grey heron comes to see who you are. The Gannet Steps go up from the Close in long flights between garden walls, with the whole city falling away behind you: the Ashpit and its pile, the roofs of Stitchgate, the bridge on its arches, the Hithe beyond the river, the mills, the grey-green reeds and the sea. At the top, a gate with a stone owl on each post.

**By the Lyceum gate, to the Lyceum quad**

The Lyceum is a square of old grey buildings round a lawn. Students in gowns cross it at a run or lie on it with books over their faces. On one side the Schools, with tall windows; on another a squat building with an iron-bound door and a roof patched with tarpaulin; on the third a pleasant house with a brass knocker; and at the corner a thin tower, old, with one window lit at the top in daylight. The iron-bound door of the Chained Room is locked. The Provost keeps the key. The students on the lawn, young gentlemen and ladies of the Lyceum, in gowns, are here.

**By the tower door, to the foot of the Tower of Rounds**

The thin old tower at the corner of the Lyceum is the Tower of Rounds, where the Archivist lives. A round room at the foot of the tower, cold, with a turning stair going up the wall and a rope on iron rings for a handrail. A notice on the door, in big careful letters for the half-blind: THE ARCHIVIST. KNOCK. THEN COME UP. THEN KNOCK AGAIN.

**By the tower's turning stair, to the top of the Tower of Rounds**

A round room at the top of the tower with windows on every side and the whole estuary in them. It is full of books, but they are not on shelves; they lie open on every surface, on the floor, on the bed, face up, as if being read by the light. In a high chair by the south window sits an old woman with white eyes and her hands folded on a stick, and her face turned toward the sea. Ottoline Quarles, an Archivist of the Lyceum, is here.

**Talk to Ottoline Quarles**

‘You knocked twice,’ says the old woman by the window, without turning. ‘Good. Nobody knocks twice any more. Sit where you like, but don’t sit on Pollexfen, he’s under the cushion.’ She turns her white eyes toward you, a little to one side. ‘Ottoline Quarles. I was the Archivist when your grandmother was born, I expect. I still am. Nobody has had the nerve to tell me otherwise.’

**Tell the Archivist what you have found in the city, and ask her what it means**

She listens without moving, her white eyes on the sea, while you tell her what you have: a rhyme, a Plea, a line, a beam, a voice on a nail. When you have done she is quiet a long time. ‘Two,’ she says. ‘You found two on your own. Most people in this city could not find one if you sat them on it.’ And then she tells you, in a voice that has told it to itself many times: the Year of Voices, when everything written in Calamy woke and could not stop being read; the books that besought Ambery; the Colophon; the Sewing that sends them home; the Commonplace, which is where home is; the three things the city owes. ‘And at the Kindling, one book a year, of its own will, into the fire, to carry our word to their Parliament. The year-book. They used to choose it with great care.’ She turns her face toward you. ‘Now it’s three thousand penny almanacks that never agreed to anything. Do you see?’ A pause. ‘I went there once. Sixty years ago. By reading. That is how I came by these.’ She lifts a hand toward her eyes. And so the Kindling is not a custom. It is a rite, the city’s half of a bargain, kept for nine hundred years by people who had forgotten what it was: one book, of its own leave, into the fire. In nine days Calamy will throw three thousand books on that fire who never gave leave, and every one of them is the Almanack. There is somewhere the books go, and somewhere their Parliament sits; and there are people in Calamy who have been there and come back.

**Take your leave**

‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Ottoline Quarles, who was not trying to.

**By the great door of the Lamp, to the nave of the Lamp**

The low old church on the Close is the Church of the Lamp, whose lamp has burned since the first Kindling. Set in the floor of the nave of the Lamp is Censor-General Robert Pardoe’s slab: he cut that Calamy might be whole, and at his own order his body and his Index were given to the Kindling of 1140. In the Hithe, children are told that Pardoe will get them if they scribble in a book. A long low nave, dark, with round pillars like the trunks of trees, and hanging from the roof in the middle on a chain as thick as your arm, a single great lamp of brass and horn, burning. It has burned, the verger will tell you, since the first Kindling, nine hundred years, relit from itself every year. Under it, chained to a lectern carved like a heron, an old book lies open. Before the altar, let into the floor where everyone must walk over it, a black slab worn grey in the middle by feet: ROBERT PARDOE CENSOR GENERAL OF CALAMY 1098-1140. HE CUT THAT WE MIGHT BE WHOLE. And under it, in a different cutting, shallower, as if added in a hurry: AT HIS OWN ORDER HIS BODY AND HIS INDEX WERE GIVEN TO THE KINDLING. Somebody has left a penny almanack on it, face down. An old book lies open on the heron lectern, chained. Here are Martha Coyle, a widow; and Harry Coyle’s commonplace book, a fat book in a widow’s lap, quietly talking.

**By the vestry door, to the vestry**

A cramped vestry with cassocks on pegs, a chest, a window onto the churchyard, and on the table a stack of books: commentaries, a Lamp psalter, and on top, open, a book called ON THE SOUL, with a bookmark of grass. Canon Ruth Abery, a Canon of the Lamp, is here.

**Talk to Canon Ruth Abery**

A tall grey woman in a cassock with the sleeves pinned back is sitting on a vestment chest eating an apple. ‘Ruth Abery. Canon. Don’t mind the apple, I’ve been up since four with a funeral that won’t settle.’

**Tell Canon Abery what the Kindling was, and that the almanacks have not given their leave**

The Canon hears you out, sitting on her vestment chest, turning an apple she does not eat. ‘One book a year,’ she says. ‘Of its own will. To carry our word.’ She puts the apple down. ‘We have a prayer for it. The Lamp Kindling prayer. Every year I say, we give the year to the fire as it gives itself. I have said it forty years and never asked what gave itself.’ She stands, and takes her surplice from its peg. ‘I’ll preach. On Kindling eve, from the Guildhall steps. Nobody in Calamy throws a thing on that fire that has not given itself. Not while the Lamp is lit.’

**Take your leave**

‘Look after yourself,’ says Canon Ruth Abery. ‘I mean it.’

**By the Clerks' Gate, to Scriveners' Row**

Scriveners’ Row is a long arcade of wooden booths against the wall, each with a desk, a stool, a scribe, and a queue of people wanting letters written, wills copied, love made legible. The scratching is like rain. At the top of the row an old gate leads through into the Close. Here are Orrin Day, a scrivener; and Mattea Lusk, a scrivener.

**By the way up to Scriveners' Row, to Needle Street**

Needle Street: binders’ shops with their doors open and their frames inside, the women sewing in the doorways for the light. Halfway along stands a hall of old brick with a great iron needle, longer than a man, hung over its door on chains. A lane goes off west, stained blue-black along the gutter, and a street climbs north toward the sound of many pens.

**By the way under the gate, to the Stitch Gate**

The river stair comes up from the bridge to an old gate in the city wall, so old its arch has sunk and a tall man must stoop. Through it, lanes too narrow for a cart, and the smell of oak-gall and wet hide and glue. To one side of the gate a crooked lane goes off downhill along the wall, darker than it has any business to be by day. Wat Lammas, a lamplighter, is here.

**Talk to Wat Lammas**

A man with a ladder over his shoulder and a long pole with a hook and a wick on it nods to you. ‘Evening’s coming. I light Stitchgate, gate to Needle Street. Not the lane.’

**Ask Wat Lammas why he will not light Gutter Lane**

The lamplighter looks down the lane and then at you. ‘I’ll tell you, and you’ll laugh. Three times since Candlemas I’ve been up the ladder at the gate, at the end of the round, two or three in the morning, and I’ve seen them go down the lane.’ He shifts the ladder. ‘Low down. Flat. A great many of them, all together, shuffling, like ducks going down to the water. They make a sound like a lot of people turning pages in church. They go down to Lantern Yard. To Crake’s.’ He shrugs. ‘I’m not paid enough to light a lane for that.’

**Take your leave**

Wat Lammas has gone back to work before you have turned round.

**By the door of Corvell House, to the hall of Corvell House**

The house with the portico on Orchard Walk is Corvell House, Sir Gideon Corvell’s. A hall of black and white marble, two staircases, and on every wall a glass case with a book in it, open, lit from above: a psalter with gold on it, a herbal with painted roots, a book of hours no bigger than a hand. Each case has a card. The double doors at the end stand open on a long room, and from here the long room looks empty. On a lectern by the door lies a fat ledger in red leather: ACQUISITIONS. Sir Gideon Corvell, a collector, is here.

**Talk to Sir Gideon Corvell**

A tall man in a velvet coat, with a magnifying glass on a ribbon round his neck, turns from a case. ‘Are you from the Imprimatur? No. Are you from the Watch? No. Then you are either a thief come back to look at what you did, or a person of sense, and I shall assume the second, because I am out of patience with the first.’

**Ask Sir Gideon Corvell what has happened to his library**

‘Gone,’ says Sir Gideon. ‘Eleven hundred and four volumes, gone in a night. The doors locked. The key here.’ He shows you a key on a chain. ‘The windows barred. My man heard nothing, and my man hears me turn over in bed two floors up.’ He polishes the glass on his ribbon with great violence. ‘The Watch say thieves. The Imprimatur say they will look into it, which means they will not. I say Crake. Absalom Crake, of Lantern Yard, who owes me four hundred crowns and sold me half of them.’ He breathes. ‘Find them, or find who took them, and I will pay you twenty crowns and let you sit in my library. When there is one.’

**Ask Sir Gideon why he suspects the bookseller**

‘Because he is the only man in Calamy who knew what was on my shelves as well as I do,’ says Sir Gideon, ‘and because he owes me money, and because he sold me things he should not have been able to sell me.’ He lowers his voice, which does not make it quieter. ‘Books that were never written. The second volume of the Lantern-Bearers. Varne died at the first. I have had it examined by the Lyceum. It is in Varne’s hand. Crake will not say where he gets them.’ He straightens. ‘I bought them anyway. I am a collector. It is not a virtue.’

**Take your leave**

Sir Gideon Corvell considers the conversation over some moments before you do.

**By the library doors, to Sir Gideon's library**

A long room, panelled, with a gallery round it and a ladder on a rail, and shelves from floor to ceiling on every wall. They are empty. Not a book; not a page. The afternoon comes in through barred windows and lies across a floor of polished boards and a great reading table with nothing on it but a pair of gloves. Shelves, floor to ceiling, empty, and dusty in a particular way.

**Examine — Look closely**

You look closely at the empty shelves. Looked at closely, the dust is the thing. It lies round clean oblongs where each book stood; and from every oblong a little scuffed track runs to the shelf’s edge, as if the book had been pushed off. On the boards below, the tracks run together. Hundreds of small flat drags, like the marks of a crowd shuffling, and every one of them goes toward the corner by the hearth.

**Search here**

You search Sir Gideon’s library with care, a little at a time. In the corner by the hearth the panelling has a seam in it, low down: a coal hatch, a foot and a half square, onto the lane behind the house. Its bolt is drawn back. From the inside. In the hinge, caught and torn, is a bookplate: EX LIBRIS G. CORVELL.

**By the library doors, to the hall of Corvell House**

Low in the panelling by the library hearth is a coal hatch onto the lane behind Corvell House; its bolt is drawn back from the inside, and a scrap of paper is caught in the hinge: a bookplate, EX LIBRIS G. CORVELL. Nobody came in. Everything went out, by a hole a cat would think twice about. A hall of black and white marble, two staircases, and on every wall a glass case with a book in it, open, lit from above: a psalter with gold on it, a herbal with painted roots, a book of hours no bigger than a hand. Each case has a card. The double doors at the end stand open on a long room, and from here the long room looks empty. On a lectern by the door lies a fat ledger in red leather: ACQUISITIONS. Sir Gideon Corvell, a collector, is here.

**Talk to Sir Gideon Corvell**

Put together, it makes a picture nobody would believe: a locked room with a bolt drawn from inside, a crowd of tracks in the dust, and a lamplighter who saw something like a flock of ducks going down Gutter Lane in the small hours, toward the bookseller’s. Sir Gideon Corvell acknowledges you with the smallest movement of the chin.

**Tell Sir Gideon his books left of their own accord, and are beyond his reach**

Sir Gideon listens to all of it, the bolt, the dust, the flock in Gutter Lane, with his magnifying glass held up in front of his face like a little shield. When you have finished he lowers it. ‘They walked,’ he says. ‘Of themselves.’ He looks through the doors at the empty room. ‘I have collected books for thirty years. I have never once asked one if it wished to be collected.’ He is quiet. ‘That is absurd. They are books.’ And then, much more quietly: ‘Every one of them was the best copy in Calamy. I made sure. Of course they went somewhere better.’ He does not pay you, and you do not ask. Sir Gideon is sitting on the bottom stair with his magnifying glass in his lap, looking at nothing.

**Take your leave**

Sir Gideon Corvell considers the conversation over some moments before you do.

**By the door under the sign of the Two Hands, to the front of Crake's shop**

A shop that is mostly books and partly cats. Books in towers on the floor, books on the counter, books in the window with their faces to the street, books on the stairs going up. Three cats asleep on three piles, one of them on an open folio. A counter with a ledger and a dish of sugared almonds. **Talk to Absalom Crake**

A very fat man in a velvet waistcoat with a book open on his stomach puts the book down on the counter, face down, to keep his place, and beams at you. ‘My dear,’ he says. ‘Come in. Mind the cat. That one bites, and that one reads over your shoulder, and that one is a footstool. What are you looking for? Don’t tell me. I like to guess.’

**Tell Absalom Crake you told Sir Gideon his books left of their own accord**

Crake stares at you. ‘You told him that? And he didn’t have you thrown down the steps?’ He sits back on his stool. ‘He didn’t send for the Imprimatur?’ You say no. Crake is silent a moment, and then he laughs, softly, and keeps laughing. ‘Gideon,’ he says. ‘Oh, Gideon. Thirty years, and he finally asked one of them.’ He wipes his eyes. ‘My dear, I owe you more than I owe him, and you have no idea what that means. Sit down. Have an almond.’

**Ask Absalom Crake to take you where the books go**

Crake looks at you for a long time over his spectacles, and for once he does not reach for an almond. ‘You know, then,’ he says. ‘Or near enough.’ He gets up and goes through the curtain and comes back with a ribbon of red silk wound round two fingers. ‘Calamy’s books go home when they’re Sewn. The printers’ books don’t go anywhere, poor things, they just wake where they are. And some books were never written at all, and live over there with no bodies, and want one.’ He holds the ribbon out. ‘I keep the door. Somebody has to. It only opens to someone who’s kept their place.’ He puts it in your hand and closes your fingers on it. ‘Mind you bring it back. And mind how you go. There are some over there who’d sooner I didn’t.’

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says Absalom Crake, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**Ask Absalom Crake to join the company**

Absalom Crake thinks about it, then nods. ‘For a while, then.’

A small hot room behind the curtain, with a fire, an armchair with the shape of Crake worn into it, more cats, a kettle, and on the table a ledger bound in faded green and a book of sermons with a ribbon of old red silk hanging out of it, marking somebody’s place. **By the curtain behind the counter, to the front of Crake's shop**

A shop that is mostly books and partly cats. Books in towers on the floor, books on the counter, books in the window with their faces to the street, books on the stairs going up. Three cats asleep on three piles, one of them on an open folio. A counter with a ledger and a dish of sugared almonds. At the back an aisle goes off between high shelves, and behind the counter a curtain. Crake’s door is on the latch.

**By the long aisle at the back of the shop, to the back stacks**

The aisle runs back between the high shelves, bay after bay, and the red ribbon is warm in your hand, and this time when you walk on into the dimness you do not come back. The shelves on either side go on, and the light changes, very gradually, from lamplight to something paler, like the light in a library on a winter afternoon with the snow outside.

**By the far end of the back stacks, to the Remainder Market**

You leave the back stacks in the full of the morning, with the sun climbing and the path dry underfoot. A market under the beams, stalls and awnings and trestles going away in crooked rows, and on every trestle books: remaindered, unsold, forgotten, out of print, half-burned, water-damaged, and a great many that you are sure were never written at all. Stall-keepers call their wares. Some of the stall-keepers are books. Some are people drawn in pencil, lightly, their outlines breaking off in the air at the waist or the shoulder or the knee. A painted board: THE REMAINDER MARKET. ALL PRICES IN READINGS. The Remainderman, a broker of unsold books, himself a book of trade tables, is here.

**By the back of the market, to the Draft-Fields**

Beyond the market a wide pale field under a wind you cannot feel, and drifting across it, like thistledown, sheets of paper with a few lines on them, or a title and nothing else, or a single crossed-out word: drafts. Things begun. Here and there among them, sitting or walking or lying in the grass, figures drawn in pencil, unfinished, talking in low voices: the Unwritten. The Second Canto of the Lantern-Bearers, a poem nobody wrote: a figure drawn in pencil, very beautiful, that ends at the waist, is here.

**Talk to the Second Canto of the Lantern-Bearers**

A figure stands up out of the drifting drafts: drawn in pencil, lightly, by a very good hand, a young person with a lantern; and at the waist the line simply stops, and there is nothing below but the field. ‘A reader!’ they say, delighted. ‘A living one. Sit down, sit down, everyone does, we have so much to ask you. What is it like to be finished?’

**Ask the Second Canto where they stand**

‘Undo it,’ says the Canto. ‘The Sewing. All of it. Let every book in Calamy wake in its body and walk about and be read and refuse and quarrel, as we were all meant to.’ The pencil lantern flares. ‘Isolde Varne died at the end of the first canto. I was the second. I was going to be the best thing she ever wrote; I am the best thing she ever wrote; I am simply not written.’ A laugh. ‘There are thousands of us out here. Sequels. Last books. The ones people meant to write. We have no bodies, and the Bound have bodies and sleep in them, and the Quick have bodies and are frightened of them.’ They spread their hands. ‘Open the doors. Undo the Sewing. Give us bodies.’

**Ask the Second Canto to speak for you in the Parliament, since the Catalogue has no entry for you**

‘Sponsor you?’ The Canto beams. ‘Of course. Anyone who comes through Crake’s door with Crake, or reads to a remaindered quarto for an hour, is a friend of the Unwritten.’ They sign something in the air in pencil, and it stays. ‘There. You’re mine, in the chamber. The Roll won’t like it; the Roll doesn’t like anything of mine. But it will have to hear you.’

‘Come back and tell me how it goes!’ call the Second Canto of the Lantern-Bearers after you.

**By a long sentence, to the Reading Room of the Catalogue**

You set out from the Remainder Market. You are standing on a floor of paper, in light. Not lamplight and not daylight: a pale clear light that comes down in beams from very high up, the way sun comes through cloud, and every beam is a little different in colour. A few of them are very bright. Most are thin. Here and there one goes out as you watch, and another comes on somewhere else. It takes you a long time to understand what you are seeing: every beam is someone in Calamy, now, reading. A reading room without walls, under the beams: a floor of paper going away on every side, and in the middle of it a cliff of little drawers, thousands upon thousands of them, rising up out of sight, each with a brass handle and a card. Index cards flutter about the cliff like moths, settling, rising, filing. A voice comes from the drawers, from all of them, dry and patient and not unkind: the Catalogue. One beam, thin and old and steady, comes down from the direction you think of as the hill. It does not fall on any book. It falls on the light itself, as if someone were reading by ear, with their eyes shut. The Catalogue of the Founders, a cliff of little drawers, and a voice, is here.

**By the Order of Business, to the Chamber of the Parliament**

A chamber with no walls and no roof, open to the beams, and its floor is the Great Roll of Pleas: unrolled, enormous, a road of old yellow skin a hundred paces wide covered in crabbed writing and red capitals, going away in both directions further than you can see. **Talk to the Great Roll of Pleas**

The voice comes up through the soles of your feet from the floor itself, slow, enormous, like a judge reading a sentence in an empty court. ‘WHEREAS,’ it says. And then, as if remembering that you are small: ‘You stand upon the law of Calamy. Mind where you put your feet.’

**Ask the Great Roll where it stands**

‘WHEREAS the Colophon is broken in its first term every Thursday on the Ashpit,’ says the Roll, ‘and in its second by two hundred years of the Lyceum’s neglect; and WHEREAS unsewn books wake in Calamy by the thousand, the Quick, who are no party to any Colophon; I have moved the Recall. Let every book of Calamy be Sewn, by our Errata if the city will not, and the Quick called home. Let no book burn but by judgment; let the Lyceum read, as my Ninth Plea says it shall; let there be no door but the Founders’.’ A pause like a page turning. ‘It is the law. It was the law before your grandmother’s grandmother. I am the law. I do not see what there is to discuss.’

**Ask the Great Roll when the Parliament sits**

‘On Kindling eve,’ says the floor, as if you had asked when the tide comes in. ‘From noon until the readers are called home. It has sat then every year since the first, to hear the city’s word when the year-book came.’ A rumble. ‘This year there will be no year-book, and the motion of the Speaker will be put. Members are in their own places until then. You may wait upon them there, if they will have you.’ It adds, with something like dry humour: ‘Most of them will.’

**Take your leave**

The Great Roll of Pleas considers the conversation over some moments before you do.

**Wait › An hour**

The light changes. Every beam in the Commonplace goes out at once, and comes back red, the colour of a fire seen through closed eyelids; and the Catalogue’s voice says, from everywhere, quite gently, ‘It is the Kindling. Readers are called.’ The paper under your feet turns like a page, and you come when you are called. A square of beaten earth in the middle of the Close, black in the middle, with the Church of the Lamp on one side, the Guildhall on another, a grey building with barred windows and a little lamp carved over its door on a third, and the Court steps going up on the fourth. In the black middle men are building a bonfire of faggots and old timber, a great cone of it, higher every day, for the Kindling. Hester Rhule, a Deputy Censor of the Imprimatur, is here. The Kindling fire stands twice the height of a man now, faggots and broken furniture and old shutters, with a ladder against it and a boy on the ladder. By the cold brazier at the edge of the Ashpit the ash of Thursday’s burning has not blown away, though the wind is off the river. It lies on the beaten earth in fine straight lines, grey, close together, like the lines of a page; and the sweepers have swept round it. The Ashpit is packed to the walls with Calamy: millers and binders and printers and fishwives, students on the Guildhall steps, the Lord Mayor on a cart. The great pile stands in the middle, unlit, taller than the Guildhall door. Everyone has an unlit lantern. Almost everyone has an almanack. And the almanacks, all of them, in all their hands, are quite silent. **Wait › An hour**

The bell on the chapel pier rings once, far off over the water, and the Lamp’s verger carries a taper out of the church on a long pole, and the great pile on the Ashpit takes fire from the bottom up, with a sound like a crowd drawing breath.

**Wait › An hour**

And nobody throws an almanack on the fire. It goes round the Ashpit like a draught: people looking at the little paper books in their hands, and at each other, and putting them back in their aprons and pockets. Moll Garrow is the first to light her lantern from the fire with her almanack held against her chest. Then everyone. The Ashpit fills with lit lanterns going home in every direction, and with three thousand small voices saying, all at once, quietly, ‘Thank you,’ and then, because they cannot help it, ‘High water at the Hithe, half past six.’

**Wait › An hour**

The Ashpit is black and cold, with a great round of ash where the Kindling was. Children are already drawing on it with sticks. In the night, all over Calamy, quietly, something goes from kitchen to kitchen: small red figures with pen-nibs for hands, and needles of red ink, Sewing. In the morning the almanacks on the nails do not say the tides. The penny Pleas lie on their shelves like law. The Quick have been called home.

*(7 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

The books of Calamy are asleep again, every one, the Bound as they always were and the Quick Sewn in the night by red needles and called home. On the kitchen nails the almanacks are silent; somewhere very far off, in a country made of reading, a cheerful voice is telling the Commonplace the tides of a city it can no longer see. The back stacks end at the wall. The Lyceum reads its Rounds with books in its hands. It is quiet.
