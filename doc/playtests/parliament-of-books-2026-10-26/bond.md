# The bestiary in the bond

Seed `bond`; 56 steps; ended at watch 82. Written by `bun scripts/parliament-of-books-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

A raised causeway of black stones crosses the saltings toward a town of roofs and masts. Ahead of you a long bridge goes over the river on a great many arches, so crowded with sheds and stalls that it looks like a street that has wandered out over the water. Beyond it, a walled town climbs to a single hill with a tower on top; downriver, to your right, the reed-beds go grey-green all the way to the sea. Under a pair of mills two wheels turn slowly in the river. Gulls. The air smells of mud and, faintly and strangely, of wet paper.

**By the causeway into the Hithe, to the Hithe quay**

The Hithe quay is cobbled and wet and loud. Eel-boats lie two and three deep at the steps; a fishwife cries her baskets; a gang of boys rolls rag bales up a plank into a lighter. Along the back of the quay stand a tavern under a painted lantern, a customs house with a great barred warehouse behind it, and a crowd of people round a board nailed to a post. At the west end the steps go down to a ferry; at the east, the quay turns into a market of rags. On the post a printed notice, fresh, with the Imprimatur’s little lamp stamped at the foot: UNQUIET VOLUMES. Under it someone has chalked a drawing of a book with legs, running, and someone else has rubbed most of it out. Bet Kettering, a fishwife, is here.

**By the lane to the Customs, to the Customs yard**

A yard behind the customs house, cobbled, with a great warehouse along the back of it: the bond, where goods wait for their duty to be paid. Its gate is barred with iron. On the gate a notice: BOND CLOSED AT DUSK BY ORDER. And across the notice, deep in the oak of the gate, four long claw-marks, fresh and pale, as if something on the other side had wanted very much to get out. The bond gate stands open. Mr. Sallis of the Customs, a customs officer, is here.

**Talk to Mr. Sallis of the Customs**

A tired man in a blue coat with a bunch of keys at his belt nods at you from the customs-house step. ‘Bond’s open till dusk. If you’ve goods in it, show me the docket. If you haven’t, there’s nothing in there you want.’

**Ask Mr. Sallis about the claw-marks on the gate**

‘A book,’ says Mr. Sallis wearily. ‘An old one, out of the hold of the Saint Ide, seized for duty with the rest of her cargo. A bestiary, gold on every page. Some light-fingered lads got in at the back a fortnight ago and cut the thread out of it to sell the pages one by one, and Dobbin disturbed them, and they ran and left it.’ He rubs his eyes. ‘And since then, every night, what’s in the pictures comes out of it and walks. Dobbin says a lion with a man’s face. I didn’t believe him. Then I saw the gate.’ He jingles his keys. ‘The Imprimatur’ll burn it Thursday if I send it. It’s six hundred years old. I’d give eight coin to anyone who’d make it stop, any way but that.’

**Take your leave**

Mr. Sallis of the Customs has gone back to work before you have turned round.

**By the lane to the Customs, to the Hithe quay**

On the post a printed notice, fresh, with the Imprimatur’s little lamp stamped at the foot: UNQUIET VOLUMES. Under it someone has chalked a drawing of a book with legs, running, and someone else has rubbed most of it out. Bet Kettering, a fishwife, is here. The Hithe quay: eel-boats, gulls, the tavern under its lantern, the customs house, the notice post, and the bridge going north over the water.

**By the steps down to the ferry, to the ferry stairs**

Green steps go down into the river between two piles hung with weed. A flat black ferry-boat is tied at the bottom, and on the top step a board says J. PELLOW, FERRYMAN, PENNY A HEAD. Across the board somebody has chalked NOT AFTER DARK. LICENCE LAPSED. BY ORDER. Jonah Pellow, a ferryman, is here.

**By Jonah's ferry, to the Water Gate stairs**

Wet steps up from the river into a low arch in the wall of the Close. At the top an iron gate, open; through it, grass and the dark bulk of a church.

**By the Water Gate, to the Close green**

A lawn inside the old city, with paths across it worn by nine hundred years of the same short cuts. On one side the Church of the Lamp, low and old, with a great door; through the door, a light. An arch in the far wall goes down to the river. Elsewhere, the black square of the Ashpit with its bonfire going up, and on the hill above the roofs a tower.

**By the Clerks' Gate, to Copyists' Row**

Scriveners’ Row is a long arcade of wooden booths against the wall, each with a desk, a stool, a scribe, and a queue of people wanting letters written, wills copied, love made legible. The scratching is like rain. At the top of the row an old gate leads through into the Close. Here are Orrin Day, a scrivener; and Mattea Lusk, a scrivener.

**By the way up to Copyists' Row, to Needle Street**

Needle Street: binders’ shops with their doors open and their frames inside, the women sewing in the doorways for the light. Halfway along stands a hall of old brick with a great iron needle, longer than a man, hung over its door on chains. A lane goes off west, stained blue-black along the gutter, and a street climbs north toward the sound of many pens.

**By the turning into Inkhorn Lane, to Inkhorn Lane**

Inkhorn Lane is blue-black. The gutter runs blue-black, the doorsteps are blue-black, and so are the hands of everyone in it. A shop with jars in the window, gall-nuts and lampblack and vitriol; and opposite it a little house with a shut door, and a board over the door with letters painted for children to read: A B C, D E F, and THE SCHOOL.

**By the ink shop door, to Gallon's ink shop**

A shop that is all jars: oak-galls in one, lampblack in another, green vitriol, gum arabic, cuttlefish, something red. On the counter a row of little stoppered bottles of ink, each with a hand-written label: SCRIVENERS’ BEST. LAWYERS’ BLACK (WILL NOT FADE). LOVERS’ BROWN (WILL). Behind the counter a woman with blue-black hands is stirring a pot that smells like a wet forest. From the ceiling, through the attic hatch, comes the sound of someone sighing very loudly on purpose. By the hearth hangs a penny almanack on a nail, curling in the steam. Here are Widow Gallon, an ink-maker; and the Almanack, a penny almanack, one voice in three thousand kitchens.

**Buy › A lantern**

Wren pays Widow Gallon, and takes a lantern.

Wren pays Widow Gallon, and takes a flask of lamp oil.

**By the way under the gate, to the Stitch Gate**

The river stair comes up from the bridge to an old gate in the city wall, so old its arch has sunk and a tall man must stoop. Through it, lanes too narrow for a cart, and the smell of oak-gall and wet hide and glue. To one side of the gate a crooked lane goes off downhill along the wall, darker than it has any business to be by day. Wat Lammas, a lamplighter, is here.

**By the river stair to the Stitch Gate, to the north end of the Penny Bridge**

The north end of the bridge comes up under the city wall. Stalls of almanacks and ballads and prints; a toll-house with a window like a ticket-office; and the river stair going up to an old gate in the wall, with a road running off east along the river toward a new town of chimneys. Lettice Ames, an almanack-seller, is here.

**By the northern arches, to the chapel pier**

Halfway over, the bridge widens round a pier with a tiny chapel on it no bigger than a cart, with a bell in a hutch on its roof. Children are skipping a long rope across the whole width of the bridge, so that everyone has to wait or jump; a ballad-seller has his sheets pegged to a line along the parapet; and a broadside on the line is singing, by itself, quite loudly. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post. Here are the children on the bridge, a dozen children of the Hithe and Stitchgate, skipping; the Ballad of Alderman Prowse, a printed broadside on a line, singing; and Dickon Spry, a ballad-seller.

**By the southern arches, to the south end of the Penny Bridge**

The bridge begins as a street: sheds and stalls on both sides built out over the water on brackets, so that you walk between walls of books and ballads and pies with the river showing through the cracks in the planks. A tooth-drawer, a pie-man, three second-hand bookstalls with their tables tilted to the light, and a puppet booth with a striped curtain and a crowd of children in front of it. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post.

**By the bridge foot, to the Hithe quay**

The Hithe quay: eel-boats, gulls, the tavern under its lantern, the customs house, the notice post, and the bridge going north over the water. On the post a printed notice, fresh, with the Imprimatur’s little lamp stamped at the foot: UNQUIET VOLUMES. On the gate a notice: BOND CLOSED AT DUSK BY ORDER. **By the bond gate, to the bond floor**

It is pitch dark. Without a light you can see nothing here, and do nothing that needs seeing.

**By the bond stair, to the upper bond**

The caladrius comes to see who you are. It is pitch dark. Without a light you can see nothing here, and do nothing that needs seeing. In the dark you can hear the Hithe Bestiary close by.

**Talk to the Hithe Bestiary**

The great book on the crate stirs its loose pages, and a voice comes out of it, old and soft and frightened, like a nun who has woken in the wrong house. ‘Who is it? Is it the knives again? Please. I have nothing left that is gold.’

**Ask the Bestiary what happened to it**

‘I was asleep,’ says the Bestiary. ‘Six hundred years. Sewn in a scriptorium in the Close by a woman called Agnes with a cough, who said the words with every stitch; and I went home, and I was home all that time, and I was read now and then, a beam of light from far away, very pleasant.’ Its pages shiver. ‘And then knives. Hands at my back, cutting the thread. And I woke here, in the dark, in my body, and could not get home, and I was so frightened that everything in me came out.’ A pause. ‘They walk at night. My beasts. I can’t call them in. I don’t know how, awake. I was never meant to be awake.’

**Ask the Bestiary how its beasts might be called in**

‘Read them,’ says the Bestiary, at once, as if it had only been waiting to be asked. ‘Read them their own pages. Aloud. That is what a bestiary is: you read the page and the beast is there, in its picture, where it belongs.’ It rustles. ‘The manticore at the third leaf. The bonnacon at the ninth. The caladrius at the twelfth; she is no trouble, she only sings.’ A pause. ‘They come out after dark. You will have to read to them in the dark, with a light, and the manticore will not wait politely to be read.’

**Take your leave**

‘Look after yourself,’ says the Hithe Bestiary. ‘I mean it.’

**By the bond stair, to the bond floor**

It is pitch dark. Without a light you can see nothing here, and do nothing that needs seeing.

**By the bond gate, to the bond floor**

Among the wool-bales something like a painted bull lowers its useless curling horns at you and backs, very purposefully, toward you. The bond at night: shapes of bales in the dark, a smell of tea and tar, and from the upper floor a sound of something large moving about, and something else singing. The bond gate stands open. The bonnacon, a beast out of a bestiary, brown and gold, is here.

**Read the bonnacon its page, the ninth leaf, aloud**

You read it the ninth leaf: The bonnacon is a beast of Asia, like a bull, whose horns curl back so that it cannot fight with them; but when pursued it defends itself thus. You read the rest with a straight face, which is the hardest thing you have done in Calamy. The bonnacon listens, and appears faintly embarrassed, and goes back into the ninth leaf like a cow going into a byre.

**By the bond stair, to the upper bond**

Out of the dark at the head of the stair something stands up that has a man’s face, smiling, with three rows of teeth in it, on a lion’s painted body; and its tail comes up over its back with a sound like paper tearing. Up in the round window, white against the dark, a painted bird sits singing; and when you look up, it looks back at you, steadily. You find you feel better than you have all day. Here are the Hithe Bestiary, an illuminated bestiary, six hundred years old, its thread cut; and the manticore, a beast out of a bestiary, blue and red and gold.

**Read the manticore its page, the third leaf, aloud**

You hold the light to the third leaf and read it out over the smiling face: The manticore is a beast of India, with a triple row of teeth; it has the face of a man and the body of a lion and a tail like a scorpion’s sting; it hungers for the flesh of men. Its smile falters. Its tail comes down. By the last line it is looking at the page, not at you, as if it had recognised its own name; and then it is not there, and on the third leaf the lion’s body has a face.

**Read the caladrius its page, the twelfth leaf**

The caladrius is a white bird, without any black; if a man is sick, and the bird looks at him, he will live; and if it turns its face from him, he will die. The bird hears it out, singing softly, and goes back into the twelfth leaf without your quite seeing how, and the page, which was white, is white with a bird in it. All three are in their pages, and the Bestiary, on its crate, gives a long sigh, like a book being shut.
