# The Stacks: the Library's own books, and Dr. Fenwick

Seed `stacks`; 99 steps; ended at watch 45. Written by `bun scripts/parliament-of-books-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

A raised causeway of black stones crosses the saltings toward a town of roofs and masts. Ahead of you a long bridge goes over the river on a great many arches, so crowded with sheds and stalls that it looks like a street that has wandered out over the water. Beyond it, a walled town climbs to a single hill with a tower on top; downriver, to your right, the reed-beds go grey-green all the way to the sea. Under a pair of mills two wheels turn slowly in the river. Gulls. The air smells of mud and, faintly and strangely, of wet paper.

**By the causeway into the Hithe, to the Hithe quay**

The Hithe quay is cobbled and wet and loud. Eel-boats lie two and three deep at the steps; a fishwife cries her baskets; a gang of boys rolls rag bales up a plank into a lighter. Along the back of the quay stand a tavern under a painted lantern, a customs house with a great barred warehouse behind it, and a crowd of people round a board nailed to a post. At the west end the steps go down to a ferry; at the east, the quay turns into a market of rags. On the post a printed notice, fresh, with the Imprimatur’s little lamp stamped at the foot: UNQUIET VOLUMES. Under it someone has chalked a drawing of a book with legs, running, and someone else has rubbed most of it out. Bet Kettering, a fishwife, is here.

**By the bridge foot, to the south end of the Penny Bridge**

The bridge begins as a street: sheds and stalls on both sides built out over the water on brackets, so that you walk between walls of books and ballads and pies with the river showing through the cracks in the planks. A tooth-drawer, a pie-man, three second-hand bookstalls with their tables tilted to the light, and a puppet booth with a striped curtain and a crowd of children in front of it. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post.

**By the southern arches, to the chapel pier**

Halfway over, the bridge widens round a pier with a tiny chapel on it no bigger than a cart, with a bell in a hutch on its roof. Children are skipping a long rope across the whole width of the bridge, so that everyone has to wait or jump; a ballad-seller has his sheets pegged to a line along the parapet; and a broadside on the line is singing, by itself, quite loudly. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post. Here are the children on the bridge, a dozen children of the Hithe and Stitchgate, skipping; the Ballad of Alderman Prowse, a printed broadside on a line, singing; and Dickon Spry, a ballad-seller.

**By the northern arches, to the north end of the Penny Bridge**

The north end of the bridge comes up under the city wall. Stalls of almanacks and ballads and prints; a toll-house with a window like a ticket-office; and the river stair going up to an old gate in the wall, with a road running off east along the river toward a new town of chimneys. Lettice Ames, an almanack-seller, is here.

**By the river stair to the Stitch Gate, to the Stitch Gate**

The river stair comes up from the bridge to an old gate in the city wall, so old its arch has sunk and a tall man must stoop. Through it, lanes too narrow for a cart, and the smell of oak-gall and wet hide and glue. To one side of the gate a crooked lane goes off downhill along the wall, darker than it has any business to be by day. Wat Lammas, a lamplighter, is here.

**Talk to Wat Lammas**

A man with a ladder over his shoulder and a long pole with a hook and a wick on it nods to you. ‘Evening’s coming. I light Stitchgate, gate to Needle Street. Not the lane.’

**Ask Wat Lammas why he will not light Gutter Lane**

The lamplighter looks down the lane and then at you. ‘I’ll tell you, and you’ll laugh. Three times since Candlemas I’ve been up the ladder at the gate, at the end of the round, two or three in the morning, and I’ve seen them go down the lane.’ He shifts the ladder. ‘Low down. Flat. A great many of them, all together, shuffling, like ducks going down to the water. They make a sound like a lot of people turning pages in church. They go down to Lantern Yard. To Crake’s.’ He shrugs. ‘I’m not paid enough to light a lane for that.’

**Take your leave**

Wat Lammas has gone back to work before you have turned round.

**By the river stair to the Stitch Gate, to the north end of the Penny Bridge**

The north end of the bridge comes up under the city wall. **By the road along the river wall, to Newmarket**

Outside the wall the town is new and loud: a broad street of printers’ shops with their windows full of ballads and proclamations and almanacks pegged on strings, ink-boys running with bundles, a smell of hot metal and size. At the end of it a cart arch opens into a yard stacked with paper bales, and over the arch, in letters a foot high: DOBLE. PRINTER TO THE CITY.

**By Orchard Walk from Newmarket, to Orchard Walk**

Orchard Walk runs between high brick walls with fruit trees showing over them, and doors in the walls with bell-pulls. One house stands out to the street with a portico and a great many windows, all shuttered on one side. Further along, a narrow shop-front with gilt letters and lace curtains, and at the end a gate into a garden of beds and labels.

**By the door of Corvell House, to the hall of Corvell House**

The house with the portico on Orchard Walk is Corvell House, Sir Gideon Corvell’s. A hall of black and white marble, two staircases, and on every wall a glass case with a book in it, open, lit from above: a psalter with gold on it, a herbal with painted roots, a book of hours no bigger than a hand. Each case has a card. The double doors at the end stand open on a long room, and from here the long room looks empty. On a lectern by the door lies a fat ledger in red leather: ACQUISITIONS. Sir Gideon Corvell, a collector, is here.

**Talk to Sir Gideon Corvell**

A tall man in a velvet coat, with a magnifying glass on a ribbon round his neck, turns from a case. ‘Are you from the Imprimatur? No. Are you from the Watch? No. Then you are either a thief come back to look at what you did, or a person of sense, and I shall assume the second, because I am out of patience with the first.’

**Ask Sir Gideon Corvell what has happened to his library**

‘Gone,’ says Sir Gideon. ‘Eleven hundred and four volumes, gone in a night. The doors locked. The key here.’ He shows you a key on a chain. ‘The windows barred. My man heard nothing, and my man hears me turn over in bed two floors up.’ He polishes the glass on his ribbon with great violence. ‘The Watch say thieves. The Imprimatur say they will look into it, which means they will not. I say Crake. Absalom Crake, of Lantern Yard, who owes me four hundred crowns and sold me half of them.’ He breathes. ‘Find them, or find who took them, and I will pay you twenty crowns and let you sit in my library. When there is one.’

**Ask Sir Gideon why he suspects the bookseller**

‘Because he is the only man in Calamy who knew what was on my shelves as well as I do,’ says Sir Gideon, ‘and because he owes me money, and because he sold me things he should not have been able to sell me.’ He lowers his voice, which does not make it quieter. ‘Books that were never written. The second volume of the Lantern-Bearers. Varne died at the first. I have had it examined by the Lyceum. It is in Varne’s hand. Crake will not say where he gets them.’ He straightens. ‘I bought them anyway. I am a collector. It is not a virtue.’

**Take your leave**

Sir Gideon Corvell considers the conversation over some moments before you do.

**By the library doors, to Sir Gideon's library**

A long room, panelled, with a gallery round it and a ladder on a rail, and shelves from floor to ceiling on every wall. They are empty. Not a book; not a page. The afternoon comes in through barred windows and lies across a floor of polished boards and a great reading table with nothing on it but a pair of gloves. Shelves, floor to ceiling, empty, and dusty in a particular way.

**Examine — Look closely**

You look closely at the empty shelves. Looked at closely, the dust is the thing. It lies round clean oblongs where each book stood; and from every oblong a little scuffed track runs to the shelf’s edge, as if the book had been pushed off. On the boards below, the tracks run together. Hundreds of small flat drags, like the marks of a crowd shuffling, and every one of them goes toward the corner by the hearth.

**Search here**

You search Sir Gideon’s library with care, a little at a time. In the corner by the hearth the panelling has a seam in it, low down: a coal hatch, a foot and a half square, onto the lane behind the house. Its bolt is drawn back. From the inside. In the hinge, caught and torn, is a bookplate: EX LIBRIS G. CORVELL.

**By the library doors, to the hall of Corvell House**

Low in the panelling by the library hearth is a coal hatch onto the lane behind Corvell House; its bolt is drawn back from the inside, and a scrap of paper is caught in the hinge: a bookplate, EX LIBRIS G. CORVELL. Nobody came in. Everything went out, by a hole a cat would think twice about. A hall of black and white marble, two staircases, and on every wall a glass case with a book in it, open, lit from above: a psalter with gold on it, a herbal with painted roots, a book of hours no bigger than a hand. Each case has a card. The double doors at the end stand open on a long room, and from here the long room looks empty. On a lectern by the door lies a fat ledger in red leather: ACQUISITIONS. Sir Gideon Corvell, a collector, is here.

**Talk to Sir Gideon Corvell**

Put together, it makes a picture nobody would believe: a locked room with a bolt drawn from inside, a crowd of tracks in the dust, and a lamplighter who saw something like a flock of ducks going down Gutter Lane in the small hours, toward the bookseller’s. Sir Gideon Corvell acknowledges you with the smallest movement of the chin.

**Tell Sir Gideon his books left of their own accord, and are beyond his reach**

Sir Gideon listens to all of it, the bolt, the dust, the flock in Gutter Lane, with his magnifying glass held up in front of his face like a little shield. When you have finished he lowers it. ‘They walked,’ he says. ‘Of themselves.’ He looks through the doors at the empty room. ‘I have collected books for thirty years. I have never once asked one if it wished to be collected.’ He is quiet. ‘That is absurd. They are books.’ And then, much more quietly: ‘Every one of them was the best copy in Calamy. I made sure. Of course they went somewhere better.’ He does not pay you, and you do not ask. Sir Gideon is sitting on the bottom stair with his magnifying glass in his lap, looking at nothing.

**Take your leave**

Sir Gideon Corvell considers the conversation over some moments before you do.

**By the gap beside the gate, to Gutter Lane**

Gutter Lane goes down along the inside of the wall from the Stitch Gate, crooked as a bad stitch, with a gutter down the middle and the backs of houses on both sides. The lamp-brackets on the walls have their lamps in them but nobody has lit them in a long while; the glass is black with old smoke. At the bottom the lane turns into a little yard.

**By the crooked end of Gutter Lane, to Lantern Yard**

A yard at the bottom of Gutter Lane no bigger than a room, with a pump in it and the city wall for one side. Across the back of it stands a narrow shop with a bow window crammed with books, and over its door a painted sign of two hands holding an open book: ABSALOM CRAKE. BOOKS BOUGHT, SOLD, AND FOUND. Crake’s door is on the latch. A narrow shop front, four windows wide, with the city wall close behind it on both sides.

**By the door under the sign of the Two Hands, to the front of Crake's shop**

A shop that is mostly books and partly cats. Books in towers on the floor, books on the counter, books in the window with their faces to the street, books on the stairs going up. Three cats asleep on three piles, one of them on an open folio. A counter with a ledger and a dish of sugared almonds. At the back an aisle goes off between high shelves, and behind the counter a curtain. Crake’s door is on the latch. Absalom Crake, a bookseller, is here.

**Talk to Absalom Crake**

A very fat man in a velvet waistcoat with a book open on his stomach puts the book down on the counter, face down, to keep his place, and beams at you. ‘My dear,’ he says. ‘Come in. Mind the cat. That one bites, and that one reads over your shoulder, and that one is a footstool. What are you looking for? Don’t tell me. I like to guess.’

**Tell Absalom Crake you told Sir Gideon his books left of their own accord**

Crake stares at you. ‘You told him that? And he didn’t have you thrown down the steps?’ He sits back on his stool. ‘He didn’t send for the Imprimatur?’ You say no. Crake is silent a moment, and then he laughs, softly, and keeps laughing. ‘Gideon,’ he says. ‘Oh, Gideon. Thirty years, and he finally asked one of them.’ He wipes his eyes. ‘My dear, I owe you more than I owe him, and you have no idea what that means. Sit down. Have an almond.’

**Ask Absalom Crake to take you where the books go**

Crake looks at you for a long time over his spectacles, and for once he does not reach for an almond. ‘You know, then,’ he says. ‘Or near enough.’ He gets up and goes through the curtain and comes back with a ribbon of red silk wound round two fingers. ‘Calamy’s books go home when they’re Sewn. The printers’ books don’t go anywhere, poor things, they just wake where they are. And some books were never written at all, and live over there with no bodies, and want one.’ He holds the ribbon out. ‘I keep the door. Somebody has to. It only opens to someone who’s kept their place.’ He puts it in your hand and closes your fingers on it. ‘Mind you bring it back. And mind how you go. There are some over there who’d sooner I didn’t.’ There is somewhere the books go, and somewhere their Parliament sits; and there are people in Calamy who have been there and come back.

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says Absalom Crake, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**Ask Absalom Crake to join the company**

Absalom Crake thinks about it, then nods. ‘For a while, then.’

The red ribbon hangs out of the book of sermons, marking a place. A ledger bound in faded green lies on the parlour table.

**By the long aisle at the back of the shop, to the back stacks**

The aisle runs back between the high shelves, bay after bay, and the red ribbon is warm in your hand, and this time when you walk on into the dimness you do not come back. The shelves on either side go on, and the light changes, very gradually, from lamplight to something paler, like the light in a library on a winter afternoon with the snow outside.

**By the far end of the back stacks, to the Remainder Market**

You leave the back stacks in the full of the morning, with the sun climbing and the path dry underfoot. A market under the beams, stalls and awnings and trestles going away in crooked rows, and on every trestle books: remaindered, unsold, forgotten, out of print, half-burned, water-damaged, and a great many that you are sure were never written at all. Stall-keepers call their wares. Some of the stall-keepers are books. Some are people drawn in pencil, lightly, their outlines breaking off in the air at the waist or the shoulder or the knee. A painted board: THE REMAINDER MARKET. ALL PRICES IN READINGS. Here are the Remainderman, a broker of unsold books, himself a book of trade tables; and Quire XVII, a sixteen leaves cut out of the Concordance of Every Word, AUSPICE to AUTUMN, still alive.

**Ask Absalom Crake who else has gone through his back stacks**

Crake makes a face. ‘Readers go through,’ he says, ‘and readers come back, mostly, by the Founders’ door, entered, looking sheepish. Two haven’t.’ He holds up a finger. ‘Fenwick of the Lyceum, eleven years ago, with a pencil, which I told him not to take. Lovely man. Thought he could map it.’ A second finger. ‘Tobias Hask, in March, with a bone folder and two hundred crowns, which I took, God forgive me. He’s looking for the Author.’ Crake says the word as if it had a smell. ‘If you go further than the Market: take nothing written that you want back the way it was. Not your letters. Not your lists. Not your book of prayers. The Stacks rewrite.’

**By a long sentence, to the Reading Room of the Catalogue**

You set out from the Remainder Market. You are standing on a floor of paper, in light. Not lamplight and not daylight: a pale clear light that comes down in beams from very high up, the way sun comes through cloud, and every beam is a little different in colour. A few of them are very bright. Most are thin. Here and there one goes out as you watch, and another comes on somewhere else. It takes you a long time to understand what you are seeing: every beam is someone in Calamy, now, reading. A reading room without walls, under the beams: a floor of paper going away on every side, and in the middle of it a cliff of little drawers, thousands upon thousands of them, rising up out of sight, each with a brass handle and a card. Index cards flutter about the cliff like moths, settling, rising, filing. A voice comes from the drawers, from all of them, dry and patient and not unkind: the Catalogue. One beam, thin and old and steady, comes down from the direction you think of as the hill. It does not fall on any book. It falls on the light itself, as if someone were reading by ear, with their eyes shut. The Catalogue of the Founders, a cliff of little drawers, and a voice, is here.

**By the Order of the Hexagons, behind the Catalogue, to the First Hexagon**

A hexagonal room, not large, lined on five of its six sides with shelves from floor to ceiling, twenty shelves to a side, and the books on them all the same height, all bound alike in plain leather, none with a title on its spine. The sixth side opens onto a narrow vestibule: on one hand a closet you could sleep standing up in, on the other a spiral stair that goes up and down out of sight, and between them a mirror that doubles the vestibule so faithfully you have to touch it to be sure. In the middle of the floor a low rail, and beyond the rail, nothing: a shaft of air with the same hexagon above it and below it, as far as you can see. The light is the light of the Reading Room, grown tired. Between the third case and the fourth there is a narrowness that is not a gap. A little book hangs on a chain at the mouth of the vestibule, worn soft as a glove by hands. The library ladder, a library ladder on a brass rail, very old, which remembers every reader who has climbed it, is here.

**Examine — Look closely**

You look closely at a small book chained at the vestibule, worn soft. You say the first rule over to yourself, so as to have it: on the spiral, count aloud going down. And the second: in the mirrors, read over your shoulder. And the theory of the centre, which two readers have signed. INSTRUCTIONS FOR THE READER ENTERING THE STACKS. Reader: you have come further than the Reading Room, which is not wise. Bring nothing written that you love; it will not come back as it went. Bring no pen; it will write what it likes. Sign nothing. Calam-ink and the ink of the Wellhead hold, and nothing else. On the spiral, count the steps aloud going down; the stair adds one for anyone who does not, and the one it adds goes somewhere else. In the Hexagon of Mirrors read nothing that faces you: turn your back and read over your shoulder, as you would read a thing written on your own back. Do not look for the centre. Under it, in brown ink, newer: There is an Author. T.H.

**Talk to the library ladder**

The ladder on its rail rolls a foot toward you, of its own accord, and its rungs creak in a way that is almost a word. ‘Up?’ it says. ‘Or are you only looking? Most are only looking.’

**Ask the ladder who has climbed it lately**

‘Lately,’ says the ladder, considering, the way furniture considers. ‘Two. A long time ago, or last week; it is the same here. A thin man in a Lyceum gown with a pencil behind his ear and a great many questions, who went up and down me forty times copying titles, and then went up the spiral and did not come back down it. Fenwick, he said, of the Lyceum, as if I would know. I did not know. I know now; he told me about himself for a long time.’ Its rail creaks. ‘And a heavy man with a binder’s hands and a bone folder in his belt, who did not climb me at all, but stood here and said to the shelves, one after another, I know you are in there. Hask. He was looking for somebody. He went down.’ A pause. ‘Count the stair,’ it adds. ‘Nobody listens to the ladder.’

**Take your leave**

‘Look after yourself,’ says the library ladder. ‘I mean it.’

**By the Gannet Steps up from the Close, to the Gannet Steps**

The Gannet Steps go up from the Close in long flights between garden walls, with the whole city falling away behind you: the Ashpit and its pile, the roofs of Stitchgate, the bridge on its arches, the Hithe beyond the river, the mills, the grey-green reeds and the sea. At the top, a gate with a stone owl on each post.

**By the Lyceum gate, to the Lyceum quad**

Fenwick stops in the middle of the quad and stands there a long time, looking at the grass, and the Schools, and the shuttered window with its yellow card. A student on the lawn looks up, and looks again, and drops her book. ‘He’s back,’ she says, to nobody, and then, louder, to everybody: ‘Fenwick’s back!’ Somewhere in the Fellows’ Court, a door opens. A grey heron comes to see who you are. The Lyceum is a square of old grey buildings round a lawn. Students in gowns cross it at a run or lie on it with books over their faces. On one side the Schools, with tall windows; on another a squat building with an iron-bound door and a roof patched with tarpaulin; on the third a pleasant house with a brass knocker; and at the corner a thin tower, old, with one window lit at the top in daylight. In the corner of the quad, on the ground floor of the Fellows’ Court, one window is shuttered from the inside, and on the shutter is pinned a card gone yellow: DR. A. FENWICK. AWAY. Somebody has added in pencil, in a student’s hand: ELEVEN YEARS NOW. And somebody else: HE’LL BE BACK. The shutter in the Fellows’ Court is open. Through the window you can see Dr. Fenwick at a desk, drawing, very slowly, in ink, with students on the floor round him; on the wall behind him, one plan. The iron-bound door of the Chained Room is locked. The Provost keeps the key. The students on the lawn, young gentlemen and ladies of the Lyceum, in gowns, are here.
