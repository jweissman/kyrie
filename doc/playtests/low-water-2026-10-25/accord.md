# The Measured Accord

Seed `ammerlin`; 347 steps; ended at watch 909 with the Mere at 31 spans, at the ending "The Measured Accord". Written by `bun scripts/low-water-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The road comes over the lip of the Bowl and stops, because the world does. Below you the city goes down in four great steps, each a ring of roofs and gardens and wet stone, and between the rings the water: falling, turning, held. You can hear it before you understand it, a sound like a great many people breathing out. At the bottom, very far, the Mere lies like a coin somebody dropped. There is a pale ring round it. It takes you a moment to see that the ring is mud.

**To the head of the Stair**

The head of the Stair: a stone landing the size of a market, where the Ninefold, brought along the Rim in a cut channel, drops into the first of the Nine Locks and goes down the Bowl in steps of white water. Stairs for people go down beside it. The spray makes a rainbow over the first lock at this hour and a cold mist at every other. The rainbow hangs over the first lock, so steady the carters use it to tell the time. A woman in a grey coat is standing at the head of the Stair with a brass rod under her arm, as if she had been there all morning and would be there all afternoon. Isaura Vant, a Clerk-Measurer, Third Grade, of the Office of Measures, is here.

**Talk to Isaura Vant**

Isaura looks up from her ledger, finishes the line she was writing, and then looks up properly.

**Ask the woman in grey if she is waiting for you**

‘You’re the Assessor.’ It is not a question. ‘Isaura Vant. Office of Measures, Third Grade. I’m to accompany you, record your findings, open what you need opened, and stop you being robbed. The Registrar would like your Finding at the Hearing, which is at noon in six days’ time.’ She glances down at the Mere. ‘It was at the fourth step of the Ward landing when I was a girl. There are twenty-nine painted steps above it now. I’ll show you the twenty-ninth. That’s the kind of thing I’m for.’

**Ask Isaura Vant to join the company**

Isaura tucks the brass ell-rod under her arm and falls in beside you, half a step behind, where she can see what you see and also write it down.

**To the Rim Gauge**

At the Rim Gauge the channel head has a third mouth, the oldest, bricked up: a course that once went somewhere else than the Stair or the mills. The third mouth of the channel head is the strangest. The brick that stops it is a later patch on far older work: the arch round it is cut in a stone you have not seen anywhere else in the Bowl, grey, fine, with no tool marks at all. The Ninefold comes into the Bowl here, out of a cutting in the Rim, over a weir of dressed stone with a brass gauge-plate let into it. The channel head is set to the Guild’s brick channel: a fifth of the Ninefold goes along the Rim to the mills, and the rest down the Stair. **Gauge the Fulling Channel's draw, with Isaura's rod**

Isaura goes down on one knee on the wet brick and puts the ell-rod into the Channel at its head, at its middle, and at the bend, and each time reads it, and each time writes. She works the sum on her knee with the graphite. ‘Twenty-two,’ she says. ‘Twenty-two ells and a half, if I’m generous to them, which I’m not.’ She does it again, from the beginning. ‘Twenty-two.’ Isaura closes her ledger on the figure: twenty-two ells a day.

**Ask Isaura what is troubling her**

‘May I show you something that isn’t in the official ledger?’ She takes out a thin book. ‘A ledger is what the Office believes. This is a tally: what I’ve counted.’ Columns, tiny, perfect. ‘Inflow at the Rim Gauge, less your twenty-two. Less every right recorded on every terrace. Less evaporation, from my landlady’s roof. What’s left should arrive in the Mere. It doesn’t. Forty ells a day don’t arrive anywhere.’ She closes the book. ‘Forty ells is a river. You can’t steal a river in a bucket and nobody’s dug a second one. It isn’t stolen. It’s missing. Water doesn’t go missing. That’s the first thing they teach you. This is the only figure I have ever written that I can’t put somewhere.’ And then, very low: ‘If I give it to the Registrar she’ll tell me to put it on the Guild. I’d be writing a lie with a number in it. People believe the number.’

**To the fulling mills**

Eight new mill-houses of yellow brick stand along the Channel with their wheels turning and their hammers going, and the whole Rim shakes with it. Wet cloth hangs from frames in long red and blue banners. Four hundred people work here, more or less; the more is in the morning. Here are Sella Marsh, a forewoman of the fulling mills; and the mill hands, fullers and shearers of the Guild mills.

**Talk to Sella Marsh**

Sella Marsh looks round from the hammers and does not stop watching them with the side of her face.

**Ask Sella Marsh about the mills**

‘What about them?’ Sella does not stop watching the hammers. ‘Eight houses, four hundred hands, three shifts when there’s orders. Cloth for half the north. Two years ago there were forty people here and a ruined farm.’ She points with her chin at the brick channel. ‘That’s what water’s for. Ask anybody who’s been hungry.’

**Ask Sella when the hands were last paid**

That makes her look at you. ‘Who told you to ask that?’ Nobody. She lets out a breath. ‘Lammas. Not since Lammas. Master Quill says the charter comes up at the Council and once it’s renewed the money’s there, the creditors are waiting on it same as us.’ She shrugs, hard. ‘Four hundred people waiting on a vote. Don’t write that down.’

**Ask Sella whether the Channel is draining the Mere**

‘No,’ she says, straight off, and then: ‘Not like they say. Look, I was here the summer it was cut. Two engineers came up from Corran with a chain and a level, Quill paid them a fortune. They measured it every way there is. I carried their stakes.’ She lowers her voice. ‘They wrote it down. Twenty-two ells, they said, and the Mere’s falling sixty, and the one can’t be the other. Quill’s got their report in his strongbox. He never showed it anybody. Why would he? Everybody blaming the Channel keeps everybody from asking where the rest’s going.’

**Ask Sella if she could get the engineers' report out of the strongbox**

‘And lose my place?’ But she is thinking about it. ‘I’ve a key to the strongbox; I do the wages, when there are wages.’ She looks at the hands in the lanes. ‘Here’s my price. At the Council you stand up and you say four hundred people haven’t been paid since Lammas, and you say it before anything else. Whatever else you find. Promise me that and I’ll have the copy on the bench by the door for you within the hour.’

**Promise Sella you will speak for the mill hands at the Hearing**

Sella looks at you a long while, then nods once. ‘Within the hour,’ she says, and goes into the counting-house by the side door, jingling.

**Take your leave**

‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Sella Marsh, who was not trying to.

**Into the Guild counting-house**

A long room of clerks at high desks, each with a candle and a ledger, under a painted ceiling of the Ninefold coming down from the hills as a woman pouring a jar. The clerks look up at you all at once, and down again all at once, like birds. The Guild’s bailiffs are here.

**To Harrow Quill's counting-room**

A counting-room with good windows over the Rim and the whole Bowl. Maps of the terraces cover one wall, the Ward’s plots on them marked in red ink, a good many of them. A tray with a decanter and three glasses, as if visitors were expected and welcome. An iron strongbox stands under the window, locked, with the Guild’s seal on its lid. Harrow Quill, the Master of the Millwrights’ Guild, is here.

**Talk to Harrow Quill**

Harrow Quill rises, smiling, as if you were the one person in the Bowl he had hoped to see this morning.

**Ask Harrow Quill for his side of it**

‘Sit, Assessor, sit. You’ll want to hear my side and I’ll want you to have heard it, so let’s be efficient.’ He pours three glasses and drinks one. ‘Twenty-two ells. That’s what my Channel takes, or near enough; your clerk will have it to the half-ell, I admire her enormously. Twenty-two ells, bought fairly from farms that were dying, and with it four hundred people have work who had none. The Mere has fallen sixty ells’ depth in three years. Do the sum. I am a sliver of your problem. I am the sliver you can see, and so I’m the sliver everyone would like to cut out.’

**Ask Quill why the Mere is falling, then**

‘Drought,’ he says. ‘In the hills. The Ninefold’s been low three summers running; ask any carter.’ He spreads his hands. ‘The Bowl has always had good years and bad. My grandfather used to say the Mere breathed: a hundred years in, a hundred out. We are in the out. We will be in the in again. Meanwhile, should four hundred people starve so the fish can have their pond back?’ He smiles. ‘That’s rhetoric. I apologise. I was trained to it, and it’s a habit, like a limp.’

**Ask Quill about the red ink on the Ward map**

‘Oh, good. You’ve done more than walk around.’ He is delighted. ‘Yes. I’m buying it, plot by plot, through Basset. Somebody has to. When the Mere’s down, and it’s going down, Assessor, it’s been going since before either of us drew breath, it’s only faster now, that land will be the richest in Ammerlin. There are Aenean temples under it. Do you want them looted by fishermen with shovels, or dug properly, with wages?’ He taps the map. ‘Those are the only choices anyone’s paying for.’

**Ask Quill what he thinks water is for**

‘For?’ He likes the question; he sits back. ‘Everything. That’s the difficulty. Water is the one thing every use of everything needs, so every use quarrels over it. The Office pretends the quarrel is settled by measuring, but measuring only tells you how much there is to quarrel about.’ He turns his glass. ‘I say it goes where it does the most good, and the most good can be counted: in cloth, in wages, in bread. The Ward says it goes where it has always gone. That’s not an argument, Assessor. That’s a habit with a grievance.’ A pause. ‘Though I grant you a habit with a grievance has won more elections than any argument I ever made.’

**Ask Quill whether the most good of the many is the good of anyone**

He laughs, really laughs. ‘You’ve been talking to Sella.’ He puts the glass down. ‘Yes, all right. The good of the many is a sum, and a sum is nobody’s. Four hundred wages are four hundred suppers, and they’re also a figure in a ledger I’m three quarters of a year behind on.’ For a moment he looks his age. ‘I overbuilt. Eight houses where five would have done. If the charter’s renewed the creditors wait, and the hands are paid by spring. If it isn’t, the Guild is a ruin by Candlemas and so are four hundred suppers. So when I tell you the Channel is a sliver, I’m telling you the truth, and I’m also telling you what I need to be true. I’d like you to remember both.’

**Tell Quill you know what his engineers wrote**

Quill is quiet a moment. Then he gets up, goes to the strongbox, and does not open it; he stands with his hand on it. ‘Sella,’ he says, not to you. ‘Well.’ He turns. ‘Yes. They wrote that the Channel can’t be a third of it. I didn’t publish it because the city was going to blame somebody, and if not me then somebody worse placed: the Ward, the Sounding, the Office. I am well placed to be blamed. I have lawyers.’ He sits again. ‘And because I hoped it was drought. It’s not, is it. You know what it is.’ He looks at you very directly. ‘If you know where the rest is going, Assessor, I’ll deal. I’m a reasonable man with a great many debts.’

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says Harrow Quill, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**To the Cantor's room**

The Cantor’s room: a bare cell with a clavichord in it, out of tune on purpose so that she can hear the voices against it, she says. Hymnals on every surface, some of them very old, their pages marked with ribbons and pressed flowers. On the clavichord lies the hymnal the Cantor works from, a handsome edition in green boards, its pages scored with her pencil. Cantor Sabeth Orr, the Cantor of the Sounding, is here.

**Talk to Cantor Sabeth Orr**

Cantor Orr does not get up from the clavichord. She plays one more chord, badly, on purpose, and then turns round.

**Ask Cantor Orr about the Short Hymn**

‘You’ve come about the hymn. Everyone comes about the hymn.’ She does not get up from the clavichord. ‘The old Cantors think I’ve cut the heart out of it. I’ve cut the fat out of it. Do you know what the Long Hymn was? Two hours. Seven Remainders, every one of them the same four bars, a hundred and twelve repetitions of phrases nobody could tell apart. I cut three of the worst and kept the Sixth because it’s beautiful. The young came once, before, and never again.’ Her chin comes up. ‘Now they come. Three times as many. They sing, child. You should hear them.’

**Ask Cantor Orr what the Remainders were**

‘Accretions. Silt.’ She is quite sure. ‘Some copyist in the Aenean days liked a phrase and wrote it seven times over, and his successors were too frightened to take it out again. The Seventh was the worst. Four low notes all alike, again and again, for a quarter of an hour.’ She hums it, flat, contemptuous: deep, deep, deep, deep. ‘Like somebody counting coins. I thought it was the ugliest thing in the whole hymn.’

**Ask Cantor Orr how the hymn is written**

‘In the four voices, as everything in the Sounding is: deep, middle, high and bright.’ She draws them in the air, a mark for each, rising. ‘Not notes as a fiddler means them. Voices. The deep is the men at the back with chests like barrels, and the bright is little girls, and every phrase of the old hymn is so many of each.’ She shrugs. ‘It’s older than any music anywhere else I know. There’s an old hymnal on the shelf with the Remainders still in. Read it if you want to be bored.’

**Read the old hymnal of the Long Hymn, with its Remainders**

In the old hymnal, the Remainders are written in the Sounding’s four voices, deep, middle, high and bright: the First is bright and then deep thrice; the Fourth is high and then deep thrice; the Fifth is bright and then middle thrice; the Sixth, the Rising, is deep and then bright thrice; the Seventh is deep four times; and the Long Amen is middle four times.

**To the lock stair**

The Nine Locks go down the third terrace like a giant’s stair: nine stone chambers one below another, each with its gates and its balance-beams and its keeper’s bench, and the Ninefold going down them in white falls. **To the Ninth Lock**

The water of the Ninth Lock goes down through the Deep Gate in its floor, and no culvert from it comes out anywhere in the Bowl: not into the Mere, not into the Ward. You lean over the chamber’s edge. The eddy goes round and round and, at the bronze, down. You walk the lock’s whole wall, and the Ward wall below it, for an outfall, a culvert, a weep-hole: there is none. Whatever goes down the Deep does not come back up anywhere you can find. The Ninth Lock is the last and the deepest, at the foot of the terrace, and it does not open onto the Mere. Its lowest chamber has no lower gate. The water comes in from above, goes round in a slow black eddy, and leaves by the floor. Beside the Deep Gate an iron hatch is set in the lock floor. Under it, the culvert beyond the gate is a black throat of water going down at a run. Nothing goes down it that wants to come back. In the floor of the Ninth Lock’s lowest chamber a bronze shutter the width of a cart stands up on its edge, and the water of the lock goes down past it into the dark with a sound like a long breath taken and never let out. An oak crane on the lock wall stands over a stack of stop-logs, square black beams as long as a cart.

**Into the lock-house**

The forty ells a day Isaura’s tally cannot place go down the Deep Gate at the Ninth Lock, and come out nowhere. Forty ells a day that arrive nowhere, and a gate in the floor of the Ninth Lock that water goes down and does not come out of. Isaura says it before you do: ‘There. That’s where.’ A keeper’s room the size of a cupboard: a cot, a stove, a window on the Deep. On the table, the lock-book, open, and a stack of older lock-books in a crate, and a shelf with a clay figure of a young man holding a wheel, which is not very like him. **Talk to Piet Harl**

Piet Harl stands up as if you were an inspector, and then sees you are not, and sits down again more slowly.

**Ask Piet whether the Deep Gate comes down now**

‘No.’ Very quiet. ‘Not since the new Hymn. It goes up at the Sixth, they still sing the Sixth, it’s the beautiful one. And then there’s no Seventh, and it stays up.’ He looks at the gate. ‘Three years. I write it every night. Deep did not come down. I don’t know what else to write. The rhyme says if it doesn’t, send to the Sounding. I sent.’

**Ask Piet what the Sounding said**

He takes two folded papers from under the lock-book and a third with a seal, and gives you all three without being asked twice; he has been waiting for somebody to ask. ‘I wrote it plain. The Deep did not come down, when will the Seventh be sung. And then again at midwinter.’ He taps the sealed one. ‘A young Cantor answered. The Remainders are a matter for the Cantors and not the locks.’ He tries to smile. ‘I thought perhaps it was a test. Of the keeper. To see if I’d keep watching.’ He has kept watching.

**Read Piet's copies of his letters to the Sounding, and the answer**

The Mere began to fall the autumn the Short Hymn was first sung. The Seventh Remainder, which it leaves out, is the verse at which the Deep Gate at the Ninth Lock came down each night; since the Short Hymn, the Deep Gate has never come down. Piet Harl wrote twice to the Sounding, the first winter of the Short Hymn, to ask when the Seventh Remainder would be sung, since the Deep Gate had not come down. The Sounding answered once, that the Remainders were a matter for the Cantors. It comes together like two halves of a broken plate: the autumn the Short Hymn was first sung, and the autumn the Mere began to fall; the Seventh Remainder that the Short Hymn left out, and the nights since then in the keeper’s book, every one of them the same. **Ask Isaura what happens to her now**

‘The Registrar has offered me Second Grade.’ Isaura says it as if reporting a rainfall. ‘To keep the new right. **Ask Isaura what she will say about you, when you have gone**

Isaura considers the question with the care she gives everything. ‘I’ll say the Office engaged an outsider who didn’t want anyone’s water, and they went where the figures went, which is rarer than you’d think.’ A pause. ‘I’ll say they asked me things nobody in the Office has ever asked me. What’s over the door. Whether everything can be measured. What I do when I’m not working.’ The smallest smile you have seen on her. ‘I’ll put the cake in. Some things belong in the record.’
