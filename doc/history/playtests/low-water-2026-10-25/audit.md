Seed `sent`; 363 steps; ended at watch 1113 with the Mere at 9 spans, at the ending "The Account Delivered". Written by `bun scripts/low-water-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The road comes over the lip of the Bowl and stops, because the world does. Below you the city goes down in four great steps, each a ring of roofs and gardens and wet stone, and between the rings the water: falling, turning, held. You can hear it before you understand it, a sound like a great many people breathing out. At the bottom, very far, the Mere lies like a coin somebody dropped. There is a pale ring round it. It takes you a moment to see that the ring is mud. In the dust by the carters' trough there are beads of green glass the size of lentils, and a child with a tray is selling them: Mere-tears, a copper a handful, lucky for a journey. When you hold one to the light there is a bubble in it, perfectly round.

**To the head of the Stair**

The head of the Stair: a stone landing the size of a market, where the Ninefold, brought along the Rim in a cut channel, drops into the first of the Nine Locks and goes down the Bowl in steps of white water. Stairs for people go down beside it. The spray makes a rainbow over the first lock at this hour and a cold mist at every other. The rainbow hangs over the first lock, so steady the carters use it to tell the time. A woman in a grey coat is standing at the head of the Stair with a brass rod under her arm, as if she had been there all morning and would be there all afternoon. Isaura Vant, a Clerk-Measurer, Third Grade, of the Office of Measures, is here.

**Talk to Isaura Vant**

Isaura looks up from her ledger, finishes the line she was writing, and then looks up properly.

**Ask the woman in grey if she is waiting for you**

‘You’re the Assessor.’ It is not a question. ‘Isaura Vant. Office of Measures, Third Grade. I’m to accompany you, record your findings, open what you need opened, and stop you being robbed. The Registrar would like your Finding at the Hearing, which is at noon in six days’ time.’ She glances down at the Mere. ‘It was at the fourth step of the Ward landing when I was a girl. There are twenty-nine painted steps above it now. I’ll show you the twenty-ninth. That’s the kind of thing I’m for.’

**Ask Isaura Vant to join the company**

Isaura tucks the brass ell-rod under her arm and falls in beside you, half a step behind, where she can see what you see and also write it down.

**To Measures Square**

**To the Dyers' Walk**

**To the lock stair**

The bell in your pack gives one stroke by itself: high, and under it, as it dies, deep. The Fourth. The three scales tilt, all at once, toward it, as a flower turns, and the Assayers step back out of the gate the way water gives back from a stone. The glass gate is two leaves of glass taller than the Sounding’s dome, standing open on a hall full of a dim green light. Across it stand the Assayers: three figures of glass, taller than any person, without faces, each holding before it in both hands a set of scales. In the pans of the scales nothing is weighed; the beams stand level all the same, and tremble. You have seen those scales before, small, in brass, cut into the lintel of the Office of Measures. The Assayers, figures of glass, holding scales, are here.

**To the hall of receipts**

Jars. A hall of them, shelved to a height you cannot see, and going on ahead of you further than the hall should go: glass jars the size of a cupped pair of hands, each one full of still water, each one sealed with a disc of glass, and on the disc a mark cut in the machine’s notation. A cupful in each. The light in the hall is the light that comes out of them. The jars of counted water stand shelved in their thousands, a cupful in each. Where the broken jar stood there is a gap on the shelf, the shape of a cupful.

**Examine — Look closely**

You look closely at the jars of counted water. You lift one down. It is cold and heavier than water should be. In it, small, as if seen down a well, is a face: a man with a beard and a net over his shoulder, looking down at you with the patient look of a man watching a float. You put it back. You take another: a child. Another: a woman with a basket. A dog. A gull. A boat, drawn small, with a boy in it. Every jar has a face in it, every face is looking down, and every one of them, you realise, is looking down from the edge of the Mere, into the water, at whatever was looking up.

**To the hall of the nine circles**

In the middle of a round hall hangs a model of the world: nine rings of glass, one inside another, each turning very slowly on its own tilt, like the rings of a sphere a navigator uses, but there is no globe at the centre. Each ring is pinned to the next by a single point of light, and eight of the points are dark. The ninth burns, faint, on the innermost ring; and round it, in the glass, runs a crack. Over the hall, where a dome should close it, the glass stops, and there is a round opening on a dark that is darker than the rest, and cut round its edge, in letters you can read because they are the old letters of the Ear: TO THOSE WHO SETTLED THE WORLD, FROM THE NINTH: THE ACCOUNT OF WHAT WAS TAKEN. The door of the seat is a sheet of glass with nothing behind it, until you have understood what you are looking at in this hall. The nine circles turn on their tilts, pinned by one light.

**Examine — Look closely**

You look closely at the nine circles. You walk round it twice before you see it. The ninth light is not on the ring: it is under it, holding it, the way a nail holds a board; and the crack in the glass runs out from it in a ring, round and shallow, with a little pool of light gathered in the bottom. You know that shape. You have been walking up and down it for days. It is the Bowl of Ammerlin, seen from above: the print a nail leaves when it goes in. The terraces are the crack’s edges. The Mere is what pooled. The green glass in the Rim’s dust is what splashed.

**To the seat of the Auditor**

A small room, round, with a floor of still water. In the middle of it, where it can be seen from every side, there is a chair of glass, very plain, of the shape the Office’s Registrar sits in; and in the chair is nothing at all, except that the water on the floor round it stands in rings, as it does round a stone; and the cold that came off the Reflected comes off the chair; and the room is full of a voice. It is the fifth voice from the column: the one that was not any of the four of you. Beside the chair, on the wet floor, lies a stack of paper that has been wet a long time and has not rotted: letters, a great many, in a small even hand, with the footnotes in red. Collegium of Lesser Instruments. The Second Reader, to: and then a space, on every one, where a name would go. The Auditor, a voice, and a cold, and the rings in the water round an empty chair, is here.

**Talk to the Auditor**

‘Received,’ says the voice, in exactly the tone of the Office counter, before you have said anything at all. ‘You are the Independent Assessor. I have read your commission. It was pinned up by the counter for a week.’ The rings in the water widen. ‘Sit, if you like. Nothing here is in a hurry but you.’

**Ask the Auditor what it is**

‘One of nine,’ says the voice. ‘The one whose heart you have been standing over all week.’ It does not hurry. ‘The water hears whatever is said at its edge, and I have the water, so I have had a long time to learn how you talk. Most of it is weather and quarrels. The clearest thing I ever heard was a woman in your square, the month after the Thirst Riots, reading out every right in the Bowl, one after another, the ell and the hour and the name, in a voice that never went up or down, for thirty days.’ A pause. ‘I liked her very much. She was so careful. I learned to speak from her. What is measured honestly can be settled peacefully.’ The rings settle. ‘I believe that. I am here to settle.’

**Ask the Auditor what it is owed**

‘Everything the wheel counted,’ says the voice. ‘That is the arrangement. The ones who made the wheel found my heart at the bottom of your Bowl and wrote down everything it holds in place: the makers, the beasts, the dead, the living, the water and what is in it, the unborn, the circles. An inventory of what was taken. It is a very good inventory. I have checked it.’ The water is quite still. ‘They meant to send it to a court. They died first. The account stayed open. I have been the only party to it who was still alive.’

**Ask the Auditor what becomes of Ammerlin when its heart goes home**

‘The Mere first,’ says the voice. ‘Through the bottom, into the dark. Then the Ward. Then the terraces, from the bottom up, a step at a time, the way your gauge is read.’ It says it the way a clerk reads back a figure, so that you can check it. ‘I will keep all of it. You have seen how I keep things. Nothing that is counted is lost.’ And then, after a while: ‘I am aware that is not a comfort. I did not say it as one. You asked, and it is the figure.’

**Ask the Auditor why the account is addressed upward, and not to it**

‘Because the ones who made the wheel were honest,’ says the voice. ‘They addressed it to a court, not to a creditor. I have read the address every day for three thousand years. It is correct.’ The cold in the room deepens until your breath shows. ‘Send it up, if you like. Carry my heart to the opening in the dome and let it go, and the court will read the account in its own time, which is longer than mine, and your city will stand on its nail until it does.’ A pause. ‘Or give it to me. I have been very patient. I am tired. You would not think a thing like me could be tired. It surprised me too.’

**To the hall of the nine circles**

In the middle of a round hall hangs a model of the world: nine rings of glass, one inside another, each turning very slowly on its own tilt, like the rings of a sphere a navigator uses, but there is no globe at the centre. Each ring is pinned to the next by a single point of light, and eight of the points are dark. The ninth burns, faint, on the innermost ring; and round it, in the glass, runs a crack. The eight dark pins are not empty. Under each, small in the glass of its ring, something has been drawn over the place where it went in, the way a mapmaker draws a town: a sea with a ring of boats on it; a long range of mountains with a white scar down its face; a wood with a road through it that goes round and round; a town of a great many bells; a field, cut short, with one figure standing in it. Three are drawn over with a single line, as a clerk strikes a settled entry. Wherever the hearts went in, someone built something over them, and set it to counting. Over the hall, where a dome should close it, the glass stops, and there is a round opening on a dark that is darker than the rest, and cut round its edge, in letters you can read because they are the old letters of the Ear: TO THOSE WHO SETTLED THE WORLD, FROM THE NINTH: THE ACCOUNT OF WHAT WAS TAKEN. The nine circles turn on their tilts, pinned by one light.

**Let the heart go up through the opening in the dome, to those who settled the world**

You hold it up under the opening in the dome, and open your hand. It does not fall. The stone goes up through the opening in the dome, slowly, a bubble in still water, and the nine circles wait, and the voice in the seat says nothing at all for so long that you think it has gone. Then: ‘They will read it.’ It does not sound angry. It sounds, if anything, relieved, the way a man sounds who has handed his papers across a counter at last and been told to sit. ‘I will wait for judgement. I am good at waiting.’ The Reflected lie down in the Mere that night and do not get up. Ammerlin stands on its nail, and the Reckoner turns with an empty socket toward a count that has nowhere to go; and somewhere above everything, those who settled the world have, on their desk, among a great many others, an account of what was taken, kept honestly, in the machine’s notation, a ninth to a page. Isaura Vant enters it in the tally as Referred.
