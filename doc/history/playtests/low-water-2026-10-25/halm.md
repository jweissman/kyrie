# The End of the Song

Seed `halm`; 162 steps; ended at watch 712 with the Mere at 15 spans, at the ending "The End of the Song". Written by `bun scripts/low-water-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The road comes over the lip of the Bowl and stops, because the world does. Below you the city goes down in four great steps, each a ring of roofs and gardens and wet stone, and between the rings the water: falling, turning, held. You can hear it before you understand it, a sound like a great many people breathing out. At the bottom, very far, the Mere lies like a coin somebody dropped. There is a pale ring round it. It takes you a moment to see that the ring is mud. In the dust by the carters' trough there are beads of green glass the size of lentils, and a child with a tray is selling them: Mere-tears, a copper a handful, lucky for a journey. When you hold one to the light there is a bubble in it, perfectly round.

**To the head of the Stair**

The head of the Stair: a stone landing the size of a market, where the Ninefold, brought along the Rim in a cut channel, drops into the first of the Nine Locks and goes down the Bowl in steps of white water. Stairs for people go down beside it. The spray makes a rainbow over the first lock at this hour and a cold mist at every other. The rainbow hangs over the first lock, so steady the carters use it to tell the time. A woman in a grey coat is standing at the head of the Stair with a brass rod under her arm, as if she had been there all morning and would be there all afternoon. Isaura Vant, a Clerk-Measurer, Third Grade, of the Office of Measures, is here.

**Talk to Isaura Vant**

Isaura looks up from her ledger, finishes the line she was writing, and then looks up properly.

**Ask the woman in grey if she is waiting for you**

‘You’re the Assessor.’ It is not a question. ‘Isaura Vant. Office of Measures, Third Grade. I’m to accompany you, record your findings, open what you need opened, and stop you being robbed. The Registrar would like your Finding at the Hearing, which is at noon in six days’ time.’ She glances down at the Mere. ‘It was at the fourth step of the Ward landing when I was a girl. There are twenty-nine painted steps above it now. I’ll show you the twenty-ninth. That’s the kind of thing I’m for.’

**Ask Isaura Vant to join the company**

Isaura tucks the brass ell-rod under her arm and falls in beside you, half a step behind, where she can see what you see and also write it down.

**To Measures Square**

A square of wet flagstones on the second terrace, with the Office of Measures down one side, all grey ashlar and tall windows, and the Sounding up the other, its bell-tower over everything. In the middle a fountain: a bronze basin on a column, older than either building, brimming without a sound.

**Into the Office of Measures**

The hall of the Office of Measures: a long room of benches and a counter of black oak, where the people of Ammerlin come to have their rights read, registered, quarrelled over and sealed. Over the inner door, cut in letters a hand high: WHAT IS MEASURED HONESTLY CAN BE SETTLED PEACEFULLY. A queue of people with papers goes along the benches: carters, dyers, two women from the Ward with a child asleep across their knees. Nobody in it is talking to anybody else. On the far wall of the hall, facing the door, hangs an old glass, black with age, as tall as a man. The clerks call it the Check. The words over the inner door are cut in it backward, so that as you stand at the counter you read them in the glass the right way round, over your own shoulder: WHAT IS MEASURED HONESTLY CAN BE SETTLED PEACEFULLY. Your own face is in it too, under the words. The Office clerks are here.

**To the Registrar's room**

The Registrar’s room is small and full of paper, stacked by terrace and tied with coloured tape. On the wall, a plan of the whole Stair in ink faded to brown. Registrar Fenn works standing, at a tall desk, and does not sit down for you. Registrar Odile Fenn, the head of the Office of Measures, is here.

**Talk to Registrar Odile Fenn**

Registrar Fenn finishes the line she is writing, and blots it, and only then looks up.

**Ask the Registrar what she wants from you**

‘A Finding,’ says Registrar Fenn, writing. ‘In writing, sealed, delivered at the Hearing at noon on the seventh day, which the Council has set and which I cannot move. A cause, and a remedy. You are an outsider, which is the point of you: nobody in this city can say why the Mere is falling without being accused of wanting somebody’s water.’ She looks up for the first time. ‘Clerk Vant will go with you and open what you need opened. Find me something I can put in front of the Council that will still be standing at the end of the afternoon.’ She counts twenty crowns out of a drawer for your expenses, and has you sign for them.

**Take your leave**

Registrar Odile Fenn has gone back to work before you have turned round.

**Out to Measures Square**

Measures Square, the Office down one side and the Sounding up the other, and the old fountain brimming between them.

**To the Dyers' Walk**

The Dyers’ Walk runs along the third terrace between the Stair and the basins, under lines of dyed wool hung out to dry, so that you walk through a long room of blue and madder and weld-yellow, dripping. The terrace wall below is striped with old dye-runs to the Ward.

**To the lock stair**

The Nine Locks go down the third terrace like a giant’s stair: nine stone chambers one below another, each with its gates and its balance-beams and its keeper’s bench, and the Ninefold going down them in white falls. Barges of stone and wool wait at the head of each to be let down. A keeper in a blue coat at every lock, and a bell-rope at every keeper.

**To the Ward landing**

Isaura Vant learned to swim off the Ward landing, when the Mere came up to its fourth step. Isaura stops on the top step. She looks at the painted spans one by one down to the mud, the way you would read down a column of figures you already know the sum of. ‘I learned to swim off the fourth step,’ she says. ‘It’s twenty-nine steps down to the water now. And the mud.’ She does not say anything else, and then she does: ‘Noted.’ The Ward landing: a broad stair of stone going down into the Mere, built for boats to tie up at, with the Office’s spans cut on every step and painted. Fishing boats lie along it, and not one of them is afloat. The water stands far below the last painted step, across a flat of grey mud with planks laid over it. On the lowest step the Office has painted a new mark this month and a newer one this week. The Ward gauge reads 40 spans. Dov Vant’s boat, the Tench, still floats at the end of the longest plank, in a channel the Ward has dug for her through the mud.

**To the new mud**

Grey mud to the horizon of the Bowl’s floor, cracked into plates where it has dried and soft as dough where it has not. Planks go out across it, and things stand up out of it: the stumps of posts, the ribs of a boat, the top of a wall. The smell is of old water.

**To the dig**

A pit in the mud the size of a house, shored with old oars and boat-planks, down to a pavement of grey stone and a carved doorway half out of the silt. Baskets go up and down on ropes. Things the diggers have found lie sorted on a tarpaulin: green bronze, carved stone, a few squared weights with holes through them. Here are Dov Vant, a fisher of the Mere Ward, and its loudest man; the Ward diggers, fishers turned diggers, with shovels and baskets; and Teodor Halm, a Fellow of the Collegium of Lesser Instruments.

**Talk to Dov Vant**

Dov Vant straightens up, wipes his hands on his thighs, and grins at you as if you owed him money and he liked you anyway.

**Ask Dov about Quill's agent in the Ward**

‘Basset.’ He says it like the name of a disease. ‘Going door to door with Guild silver, buying plots off people whose well’s failing. Half Tench Row’s sold. He’ll have the dig soon; old Marra owns the ground it’s on and she’s eighty and her daughter wants her up the terraces.’ He breathes. ‘Once Quill has the dig he’ll put the bailiffs on it and dig it himself, with wages, he says. Wages. Off our own Mere.’

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says Dov Vant, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

The new mud, and the planks laid over it.

**To the Dyers' Walk**

The Dyers’ Walk, under its dripping lines of blue and madder.

**To Measures Square**

Measures Square, the Office down one side and the Sounding up the other, and the old fountain brimming between them.

**Talk to Registrar Odile Fenn**

‘You,’ says Registrar Odile Fenn, neither pleased nor otherwise.

**Ask the Registrar to freeze the Ward's titles until the Finding**

Registrar Fenn listens, and takes a fresh sheet without a word, and writes. ‘An order of the Office: no title in the Mere Ward to pass between hands until the Assessor’s Finding is read. Grounds: the value of the land depends on the matter under assessment.’ She seals it. ‘Master Quill will be very charming to me about this at the Council. Let him.’ To a clerk, through the door: ‘To Basset, at the Ward, by hand, now.’ In Tench Row Wenn Basset reads an Office order with his mouth getting smaller, folds it into his ledger, and walks off up the Ward steps without a word to anyone.

**Take your leave**

Registrar Odile Fenn has gone back to work before you have turned round.

*(6 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

Over the whole Bowl, terrace below terrace, the Sounding’s bells go into the Hymn, and under them, faint and enormous, the choir.

*(6 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

An hour passes. It is a long time since anyone slept. The company is weary, and will fight the worse for it until it sleeps.

*(3 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

Down in the Ward the last boat on the Mere has gone over on her side in the mud: Dov Vant’s Tench.

*(12 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

Seven o’clock, and the Sounding’s bells go into the Hymn again, as they have every evening for three thousand years; and up and down the terraces, at every lock and sluice, keepers put their hands on their wheels.

*(23 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

The Hymn. Somewhere below you a dog howls along with the bells, and is hushed, and howls again.

*(10 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

At first light there is a noise from the Ward like a door the size of a hill slammed shut, and then screaming. The diggers broke the wall at the bottom of the Hall’s throat, and the silt went, and the Mere came down after it. Three were in the hole. Word comes down the terraces, and up them: the Ward well has failed. In Tench Row the handcarts are coming out.

*(12 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

The Hymn, at seven. Down in the Ward, where it used to come back off the Mere, nothing comes back; the mud takes it, and the handcarts in Tench Row stop, all together, while it lasts.

*(23 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

*(7 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

In the morning, word: the scholar from the Collegium was seen coming up the Ward steps at dawn, wet to the chest, laughing and weeping at once. Every bell in Ammerlin rings at once. Nobody is at the ropes. The sound goes round the Bowl and comes back off the Rim, and under it, from the water itself, from the Stair and the cisterns and every pipe and basin in the city, a chord comes up that nobody living has heard: the whole Long Hymn, in one breath, sung by the water. Then nothing. Far down, the Mere draws back from the Ward like a held breath let go, and does not come back.

*(5 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Say something to Isaura about the diggers who drowned**

‘Hob Kettle,’ says Isaura. ‘Annet Kettle. Jory Pale.’ She says it the way her brother said it, and you realise they must have learned that from the same person. ‘I had the figure. Three spans under. I had it in my book.’ She does not cry; her voice gets more exact. ‘I should have taken the book down to him myself. I should have stood in the hole and read it out to him in a loud voice until he believed it.’ She closes the book. ‘I didn’t, because he’d have shouted at me. Three people. Against being shouted at.’

**To the north road, at the Rim**

The north road ends at a gate in a low wall, and past the gate the ground goes out from under the world. Carters water their horses at a trough fed by a bronze spout older than the wall. From the Rim the ring of mud round the Mere is wider than the water now, grey, with things standing up in it. In the dust by the carters’ trough there are beads of green glass the size of lentils, and a child with a tray is selling them: Mere-tears, a copper a handful, lucky for a journey. When you hold one to the light there is a bubble in it, perfectly round.

**Go up out of the Bowl by the north road**

The high voice was sounded into the water, and the count closed with what it had, and the Mere went down six spans in one breath and stayed down. It stands at 15 spans on the Ward gauge. The Reckoner will never drink again, and never count again, and somewhere past the round door the census of everything bound has been delivered, early, and short. The children in the Ward still skip to the Lock-Rhyme. They have stopped saying the last line, the way you stop saying a word that has come true. Six days after you have gone, a letter comes after you on the north road, in a hand you know, very small and very even: The Mere went through the bottom of the Bowl last night. Not falling. Going. The Ward is going after it a step a night. Received, it said, in every room. Received. I am measuring it. It has no number. I. V.
