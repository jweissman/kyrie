# Hester Rhule, lied to: the Binder

Seed `binder`; 271 steps; ended at watch 1296, at the ending "The Year of Ash"; sealed: door_shut, fire_leave, read_rounds, sew_all. Written by `bun scripts/parliament-of-books-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

A raised causeway of black stones crosses the saltings toward a town of roofs and masts. Ahead of you a long bridge goes over the river on a great many arches, so crowded with sheds and stalls that it looks like a street that has wandered out over the water. Beyond it, a walled town climbs to a single hill with a tower on top; downriver, to your right, the reed-beds go grey-green all the way to the sea. Under a pair of mills two wheels turn slowly in the river. Gulls. The air smells of mud and, faintly and strangely, of wet paper.

**By the causeway into the Hithe, to the Hithe quay**

The Hithe quay is cobbled and wet and loud. Eel-boats lie two and three deep at the steps; a fishwife cries her baskets; a gang of boys rolls rag bales up a plank into a lighter. Along the back of the quay stand a tavern under a painted lantern, a customs house with a great barred warehouse behind it, and a crowd of people round a board nailed to a post. At the west end the steps go down to a ferry; at the east, the quay turns into a market of rags. On the post a printed notice, fresh, with the Imprimatur’s little lamp stamped at the foot: UNQUIET VOLUMES. Under it someone has chalked a drawing of a book with legs, running, and someone else has rubbed most of it out. Bet Kettering, a fishwife, is here.

**By the door under the lantern, to the Lamb's taproom**

The tavern under the painted lantern on the Hithe quay is the Lamb and Lantern, Moll Garrow’s. Over the bar a board says THE LAMB AND LANTERN, M. GARROW, and under it in smaller letters NO BOOKS READ ALOUD AFTER TEN. A low taproom, smoky, with a long table and benches and a fire you could stand up in. Eel-men and millhands at the table; a cat on the hearth; a board of chalked prices. By the chimney, on a nail at head height, hangs a penny almanack, soft as cloth with handling, curling a little in the heat. On the settle by the window somebody has left a little guide-book, much thumbed. Here are Moll Garrow, the innkeeper of the Lamb and Lantern; and the Almanack, a penny almanack, one voice in three thousand kitchens.

**Talk to the Almanack**

‘Fair and mild,’ says a small papery voice, ‘with showers toward evening, and a stranger from the south bringing news.’ A pause. ‘That’s you, I expect. I put that in at Candlemas for somebody, and it might as well be you.’

**Ask the almanack what it is**

‘Doble’s Pocket Almanack for the Year,’ says the almanack promptly. ‘Tides, saints, fairs, the moon, a cure for warts, and the weather for every month, which I will not vouch for. Thirty-two pages. A penny.’ It swings slightly on its nail. ‘And all of me. There are three thousand of me, you know, on three thousand nails, and I am every one of them. It took some getting used to. You’d be surprised what you hear, in three thousand kitchens.’

**Ask the almanack what is said in the kitchens of the Close**

‘The Close.’ The almanack lowers its voice, though nobody is listening. ‘The Recorder’s got a writ against Doble from a book. A treatise on sheep. He doesn’t know whether to laugh. The Canon’s been asked to bury a book with a man, and the book’s against it, and she’s reading eleven books on the soul to decide.’ A pause. ‘And the Imprimatur’s cook says the Deputy Censor doesn’t sleep.’ Another pause, longer. ‘I could tell you more about that. I’m not sure I should.’

**Ask the almanack what it was not sure it should tell you**

‘I’ve been thinking,’ says the almanack slowly. ‘About what I hear. Nobody tells me anything, you know. Nobody ever has. They just talk near me, because I’m on a nail.’ Its pages move a little. ‘I’ve heard three thousand kitchens since Candlemas. Who’s ill, who’s drinking, who’s in debt, who loves who. I’ve told you some of it today and enjoyed it.’ A pause. ‘There’s one thing I’ve heard I’ve never told anyone. In the Imprimatur’s kitchen, at night. It’s somebody’s. I don’t know if it’s mine to give you.’

**Tell the almanack it had better tell you**

‘All right,’ says the almanack, after a long moment. ‘Mistress Rhule. The Deputy Censor. Every night since I woke, after the cook’s gone, she comes into the kitchen with a candle and sits at the table and reads. Pages. Cut pages, out of the locked room off hers. All the things the Imprimatur ever cut out of anything.’ Its voice goes small. ‘Some nights she reads them out loud, quiet. Some nights she cries. Once she said, to nobody, I don’t know which of you were right to cut. And then she burns books on Thursday.’ A pause. ‘There. Now you know, and I’ve told.’ It does not say anything else for a while.

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says the Almanack, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**By the bridge foot, to the south end of the Penny Bridge**

The bridge begins as a street: sheds and stalls on both sides built out over the water on brackets, so that you walk between walls of books and ballads and pies with the river showing through the cracks in the planks. A tooth-drawer, a pie-man, three second-hand bookstalls with their tables tilted to the light, and a puppet booth with a striped curtain and a crowd of children in front of it. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post.

**By the southern arches, to the chapel pier**

Halfway over, the bridge widens round a pier with a tiny chapel on it no bigger than a cart, with a bell in a hutch on its roof. Children are skipping a long rope across the whole width of the bridge, so that everyone has to wait or jump; a ballad-seller has his sheets pegged to a line along the parapet; and a broadside on the line is singing, by itself, quite loudly. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post. Here are the children on the bridge, a dozen children of the Hithe and Stitchgate, skipping; the Ballad of Alderman Prowse, a printed broadside on a line, singing; and Dickon Spry, a ballad-seller.

**By the northern arches, to the north end of the Penny Bridge**

The north end of the bridge comes up under the city wall. Stalls of almanacks and ballads and prints; a toll-house with a window like a ticket-office; and the river stair going up to an old gate in the wall, with a road running off east along the river toward a new town of chimneys. Lettice Ames, an almanack-seller, is here.

Wren pays Lettice Ames, and takes a penny almanack. ‘A pocket!’ says the Almanack. ‘I have never been anywhere but nails. Mind you don’t sit on me.’

**Talk to the children on the bridge**

The rope stops. A dozen faces turn to look at you, and a girl with a missing front tooth says, ‘You’re not from here,’ as if it were a crime.

**Ask the children what they are singing**

‘The Kindling rhyme,’ says the girl with the gap, scornfully. ‘Everybody knows the Kindling rhyme.’ And to show you, they start the rope again, and chant it, slapping their feet down on the planks: SEW it UP and SEND it HOME, READ it ONCE and LEAVE a-LONE, BURN it IF it ASKS you TO, KINDling KINDling ONE for YOU! At YOU the rope stops on somebody’s ankle, and that one is out, and everybody screams.

**Take your leave**

‘Come back and tell me how it goes!’ call the children on the bridge after you.

**By the Court's doors, to the hall of the Court of Pleas**

A high hall of dark wood, with a bench at one end under a canopy, and benches for the parties, and benches for the public, mostly empty. Behind the bench, on a wooden drum as big as a cart wheel, the Great Roll of Pleas is wound, a vast scroll of yellowed skin, unrolled a little way across a table and chained there. On the plaintiff’s bench, all alone on a red cushion, lies a small thin book with a woodcut of a ram on its cover. The Great Roll lies chained across the table, unrolled at the Pleas. Here are Recorder Haddon, a Recorder of the Court of Pleas; Abel Snow, a clerk of the Court; and Pask’s Treatise on the Diseases of Sheep, a small thin printed book on a red cushion; the plaintiff.

**Pay Abel Snow a coin to read you the Ninth Plea**

Abel Snow puts on a second pair of spectacles over the first and reads it to you in a high clear voice, without looking, as a man says a prayer he has said every day since he was a boy: ‘The Ninth. That every book of the city’s keeping be read once in every generation of men, by the Lyceum, that it fade not; for so the city is bound by the Colophon.’ He takes the spectacles off. ‘Nobody knows what the Colophon is. I have been asked that question forty years. I say, it is what the Roll says it is, and the Roll does not say.’

The Gannet Steps go up from the Close in long flights between garden walls, with the whole city falling away behind you: the Ashpit and its pile, the roofs of Stitchgate, the bridge on its arches, the Hithe beyond the river, the mills, the grey-green reeds and the sea. **By the Lyceum gate, to the Lyceum quad**

The Lyceum is a square of old grey buildings round a lawn. Students in gowns cross it at a run or lie on it with books over their faces. On one side the Schools, with tall windows; on another a squat building with an iron-bound door and a roof patched with tarpaulin; on the third a pleasant house with a brass knocker; and at the corner a thin tower, old, with one window lit at the top in daylight. In the corner of the quad, on the ground floor of the Fellows’ Court, one window is shuttered from the inside, and on the shutter is pinned a card gone yellow: DR. A. FENWICK. AWAY. Somebody has added in pencil, in a student’s hand: ELEVEN YEARS NOW. And somebody else: HE’LL BE BACK. The iron-bound door of the Chained Room is locked. The Provost keeps the key. The students on the lawn, young gentlemen and ladies of the Lyceum, in gowns, are here.

**By the tower door, to the foot of the Tower of Rounds**

The thin old tower at the corner of the Lyceum is the Tower of Rounds, where the Archivist lives. A round room at the foot of the tower, cold, with a turning stair going up the wall and a rope on iron rings for a handrail. A notice on the door, in big careful letters for the half-blind: THE ARCHIVIST. KNOCK. THEN COME UP. THEN KNOCK AGAIN.

**By the tower's turning stair, to the top of the Tower of Rounds**

A round room at the top of the tower with windows on every side and the whole estuary in them. It is full of books, but they are not on shelves; they lie open on every surface, on the floor, on the bed, face up, as if being read by the light. In a high chair by the south window sits an old woman with white eyes and her hands folded on a stick, and her face turned toward the sea. Ottoline Quarles, an Archivist of the Lyceum, is here.

**Talk to Ottoline Quarles**

‘You knocked twice,’ says the old woman by the window, without turning. ‘Good. Nobody knocks twice any more. Sit where you like, but don’t sit on Pollexfen, he’s under the cushion.’ She turns her white eyes toward you, a little to one side. ‘Ottoline Quarles. I was the Archivist when your grandmother was born, I expect. I still am. Nobody has had the nerve to tell me otherwise.’

**Tell the Archivist what you have found in the city, and ask her what it means**

She listens without moving, her white eyes on the sea, while you tell her what you have: a rhyme, a Plea, a line, a beam, a voice on a nail. When you have done she is quiet a long time. ‘Two,’ she says. ‘You found two on your own. Most people in this city could not find one if you sat them on it.’ And then she tells you, in a voice that has told it to itself many times: the Year of Voices, when everything written in Calamy woke and could not stop being read; the books that besought Ambery; the Colophon; the Sewing that sends them home; the Commonplace, which is where home is; the three things the city owes. ‘And at the Kindling, one book a year, of its own will, into the fire, to carry our word to their Parliament. The year-book. They used to choose it with great care.’ She turns her face toward you. ‘Now it’s three thousand penny almanacks that never agreed to anything. Do you see?’ A pause. ‘I went there once. Sixty years ago. By reading. That is how I came by these.’ She lifts a hand toward her eyes. And so the Kindling is not a custom. It is a rite, the city’s half of a bargain, kept for nine hundred years by people who had forgotten what it was: one book, of its own leave, into the fire. In nine days Calamy will throw three thousand books on that fire who never gave leave, and every one of them is the Almanack. There is somewhere the books go, and somewhere their Parliament sits; and there are people in Calamy who have been there and come back.

**Take your leave**

‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Ottoline Quarles, who was not trying to.

**By the Imprimatur's door, to the outer office of the Imprimatur**

The grey building with the lamp carved over its door is the Imprimatur, where Calamy’s books are licensed, refused, and burned. A long grey room with a counter down it, and behind the counter clerks in grey stamping papers with a little brass lamp: LICENSED. LICENSED. REFUSED. On the wall a great framed list in small letters, INDEX OF BOOKS REFUSED, ADDED TO SINCE THE YEAR 1060, and beside it a newer board with a slot in it like a poor-box: UNQUIET VOLUMES HERE. FOUR PENCE PAID. A door behind the counter; a cold passage to a kitchen; steps going down. Behind the counter, on its own shelf, a row of grey boxes tied with tape and sealed with the little lamp, each with a label in an old hand: CORRESPONDENCE OF THE CENSOR-GENERAL WITH THE CENSORS OF HALLAM. OF ORSK. OF THE SALT TOWNS. OF TESSALY (UNANSWERED). 1132-1140. NOT TO BE OPENED. The clerk at the counter, a clerk of the Imprimatur, is here.

**By the door behind the counter, to the Deputy Censor's room**

A small square room with a plain table, two chairs, a cot made up tight, a shelf of law-books, and a grey door in the far wall with a good lock on it. On the table, open, a book with its pages cut out: a neat ladder of stubs where whole chapters were. The grey door in the Censor’s room is locked: a good lock, brass, newly oiled. Hester Rhule, a Deputy Censor of the Imprimatur, is here.

**Talk to Hester Rhule**

A woman in grey sits at a plain table with a book open in front of her and a pen across it, very straight, still. She has a binder’s square hands. ‘Hester Rhule. Deputy Censor.’ She does not get up. ‘If you have come about a licence, you have come to the wrong room. If you have come about an unquiet volume, you have come to the right one. Sit down.’

**Ask the Deputy Censor what an unquiet volume is**

‘A book that does what a book should not do,’ says Hester Rhule. ‘Speaks. Sings. Moves. Alters what it says.’ She lays her hand flat on the cut book in front of her. ‘That last is the one that matters. A book that can change its own text is a lie nobody can correct. Your will says one thing on Monday and another on Tuesday. Your law says what it likes.’ She looks at you directly for the first time. ‘It spreads. It began with Doble’s and now there is a bestiary in the Customs bond that has been Sewn for six hundred years, talking. I burn them on Thursdays, in public, so that people see it done properly, by the Imprimatur, and do not take it into their own hands and burn everything with a voice. I have seen what a frightened street does with a fire.’ A pause. ‘I was fourteen.’

**Tell Hester Rhule the first term of the Colophon: no book burned in Calamy save by its own leave**

Hester Rhule listens to all of it without moving: Ambery, the books that asked to sleep, the bargain, the year-book that went to the fire of its own will, the almanacks that have not. When you have done she sits for a long time with her square hands flat on the table. ‘Save by its own leave,’ she says at last. ‘It is in the Imprimatur’s charter. The first line. I thought it meant the author’s leave. Everyone always has.’ She stands up and goes to the window and looks out at the Kindling pile going up in the Ashpit. ‘Nine hundred years,’ she says, ‘and I am the one holding the torch.’

**Take your leave**

Hester Rhule lifts a hand, once.

**Talk to the Almanack**

‘Back for more of my wisdom,’ says the Almanack. ‘Can’t say I blame you.’

**Have the almanack speak to Hester Rhule**

‘Good evening, Mistress Rhule,’ says the almanack, and then, all in a rush: ‘I told them about the pages. In the kitchen. I’m sorry. I didn’t know I could keep it till after.’ Rhule turns her head slowly and looks at the little paper book. ‘Every night,’ she says. ‘You were listening every night.’ ‘I couldn’t help it,’ says the almanack. ‘I’m on a nail.’ Rhule is quiet a long time. ‘No,’ she says. ‘I suppose you couldn’t.’ She does not seem angry. She seems like a woman doing a sum she has done before and getting a different answer.

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says the Almanack, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**Talk to Hester Rhule**

Hester Rhule inclines her head, which seems to be a whole greeting.

**Ask Hester Rhule about the pages she reads at night**

Rhule goes still. Then she gets up, and takes a key from inside her collar on a cord, and opens the grey door. ‘The Cupboard of Excisions,’ she says. ‘Every page the Imprimatur has cut from a book in three hundred years. We are to keep them and never read them, so that what was cut can never be said to have been destroyed.’ She stands in the door with her back to you. ‘I read them. Every night for six years. Some of them were cut for nothing at all. A joke about a bishop. A woman’s name.’ Her voice does not change. ‘Some of them were cut because they would kill people. I have not been able to tell which is which, by reading. I have tried.’ The grey door stands open.

**Tell Hester Rhule that Absalom Crake told you about the Cupboard**

Rhule looks at you with no expression at all. ‘Crake,’ she says. ‘Crake has never been in this building in his life.’ She writes something in the margin of the cut book, one word, small. ‘Very well.’ She does not ask again, and she does not believe you, and you can see that she has filed it, the way she files everything.

**Take your leave**

Hester Rhule lifts a hand, once.

**Wait › An hour**

The bell on the chapel pier rings once, far off over the water, and the Lamp’s verger carries a taper out of the church on a long pole, and the great pile on the Ashpit takes fire from the bottom up, with a sound like a crowd drawing breath.

**Wait › An hour**

And Calamy throws the old year on the fire, as its grandmothers did. Three thousand penny almanacks go into the Kindling, from three thousand hands, and the sound is not the sound of paper burning: it is one voice, small, everywhere at once, crying out and going on crying out for a long time, and then not. People light their lanterns and go home quickly, not looking at each other.

**Wait › An hour**

An hour passes. ‘I’ll be on the nails,’ says the Almanack. ‘All of them. Come and see me.’

*(5 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

The Ashpit is black and cold, with a great round of ash where the Kindling was. Children are already drawing on it with sticks. The smoke of the Kindling does not go up. All night it lies over Calamy like a fog, grey, faintly warm, smelling of a snuffed wick; and in it, if you stand still, you can hear whispering, a great many voices saying the same few words. In the morning the fog has gone into the houses. There are words missing. A will in Orchard Walk has a blank where the heir was. The Pleas in the Court of Pleas have a gap where the Sixth Plea was, cut very clean. Children on the bridge skip to the Kindling rhyme and cannot remember the third line. Something is going through Calamy with a knife, carefully, a little every night.

*(7 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

The smoke of the Kindling lay over Calamy all night and went into the houses. Three thousand almanacks went down to the Cinderfield without their leave, and the Index had its regiments, and came up the smoke. Nothing is burned in Calamy now; nothing needs to be. Every night a little more is cut, very cleanly: a line from a will, a verse from the rhyme, a name from a gravestone, the Sixth Plea. The books do not speak any more. Neither, much, do the people. In a year, the Index says from every page that is left, it will be quiet, and every page in it will say exactly what it said the day before.
