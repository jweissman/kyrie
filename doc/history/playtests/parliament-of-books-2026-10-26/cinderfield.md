# The descent: the Lamp's flame through the Cinderfield, to the Index

Seed `ash`; 113 steps; ended at watch 193. Written by `bun scripts/parliament-of-books-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

A raised causeway of black stones crosses the saltings toward a town of roofs and masts. Ahead of you a long bridge goes over the river on a great many arches, so crowded with sheds and stalls that it looks like a street that has wandered out over the water. Beyond it, a walled town climbs to a single hill with a tower on top; downriver, to your right, the reed-beds go grey-green all the way to the sea. Under a pair of mills two wheels turn slowly in the river. Gulls. The air smells of mud and, faintly and strangely, of wet paper.

**By the causeway into the Hithe, to the Hithe quay**

The Hithe quay is cobbled and wet and loud. Eel-boats lie two and three deep at the steps; a fishwife cries her baskets; a gang of boys rolls rag bales up a plank into a lighter. Along the back of the quay stand a tavern under a painted lantern, a customs house with a great barred warehouse behind it, and a crowd of people round a board nailed to a post. At the west end the steps go down to a ferry; at the east, the quay turns into a market of rags. On the post a printed notice, fresh, with the Imprimatur’s little lamp stamped at the foot: UNQUIET VOLUMES. Under it someone has chalked a drawing of a book with legs, running, and someone else has rubbed most of it out. Bet Kettering, a fishwife, is here.

**By the door under the lantern, to the Lamb's taproom**

The tavern under the painted lantern on the Hithe quay is the Lamb and Lantern, Moll Garrow’s. Over the bar a board says THE LAMB AND LANTERN, M. GARROW, and under it in smaller letters NO BOOKS READ ALOUD AFTER TEN. A low taproom, smoky, with a long table and benches and a fire you could stand up in. Eel-men and millhands at the table; a cat on the hearth; a board of chalked prices. By the chimney, on a nail at head height, hangs a penny almanack, soft as cloth with handling, curling a little in the heat. On the settle by the window somebody has left a little guide-book, much thumbed. Here are Moll Garrow, the innkeeper of the Lamb and Lantern; and the Almanack, a penny almanack, one voice in three thousand kitchens.

**Talk to the Almanack**

‘Fair and mild,’ says a small papery voice, ‘with showers toward evening, and a stranger from the south bringing news.’ A pause. ‘That’s you, I expect. I put that in at Candlemas for somebody, and it might as well be you.’

**Ask the almanack what it is**

‘Doble’s Pocket Almanack for the Year,’ says the almanack promptly. ‘Tides, saints, fairs, the moon, a cure for warts, and the weather for every month, which I will not vouch for. Thirty-two pages. A penny.’ It swings slightly on its nail. ‘And all of me. There are three thousand of me, you know, on three thousand nails, and I am every one of them. It took some getting used to. You’d be surprised what you hear, in three thousand kitchens.’

**Ask the almanack how it came to be talking**

‘I woke,’ says the almanack. ‘Candlemas. In the drying-lines in Doble’s press room, hung up on cords in the dark, three thousand of me at once, and every one of me thinking, I am a book, and I am wet.’ It sounds pleased with this. ‘Nobody Sewed me. Glue and a bit of wire. I’ve heard Warden Mott say that in four kitchens, as if it was the worst thing about me.’ A pause. ‘The old books don’t talk, you know. The Sewn ones. I’ve tried. You can stand next to a Housebook on a dresser for a month and it’s like talking to a loaf.’

**Ask the almanack what is said in the kitchens of the Hithe**

‘The Hithe?’ The almanack settles in, like a neighbour at a fence. ‘Brannock and the Widow Teague at it again: somebody shuts the sluice on the millrace at night and each of them thinks it’s the other. There’s ague in Pudding Lane, five children, and Goody Nance up all night with them. A young man in the garret at the top of the lane coughs till morning and talks to his mother, who’s dead; nice manners. And something walks the Customs bond after dark and tore Dobbin Quayle’s coat off his back.’ A pause. ‘And Moll’s nephew is sweet on a girl at the mills and hasn’t said. Don’t tell Moll.’

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says the Almanack, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**Talk to Moll Garrow**

‘Come in, come in, shut the door on the gulls,’ says a big woman behind the bar, drying a pot. ‘Ale or small beer or tea, and there’s eel pie, and if you want a bed it’s the loft. Don’t mind the almanack.’

**Ask Moll Garrow about the Kindling**

‘The Kindling? You’ll be here for it, then. Nine days. Every house in Calamy brings its old almanack to the Ashpit on Kindling night and throws it on the fire, and you light your lamp from the fire and carry it home lit, and the new year’s in. My gran did it. Her gran did.’ She looks at the chimney-corner and lowers her voice. ‘This year I don’t know. You can’t very well throw a thing on the fire that says good morning to you.’

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says Moll Garrow, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

On the post a printed notice, fresh, with the Imprimatur’s little lamp stamped at the foot: UNQUIET VOLUMES. Under it someone has chalked a drawing of a book with legs, running, and someone else has rubbed most of it out. Bet Kettering, a fishwife, is here. The Hithe quay: eel-boats, gulls, the tavern under its lantern, the customs house, the notice post, and the bridge going north over the water.

**By the bridge foot, to the south end of the Penny Bridge**

The bridge begins as a street: sheds and stalls on both sides built out over the water on brackets, so that you walk between walls of books and ballads and pies with the river showing through the cracks in the planks. A tooth-drawer, a pie-man, three second-hand bookstalls with their tables tilted to the light, and a puppet booth with a striped curtain and a crowd of children in front of it. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post.

**By the southern arches, to the chapel pier**

Halfway over, the bridge widens round a pier with a tiny chapel on it no bigger than a cart, with a bell in a hutch on its roof. Children are skipping a long rope across the whole width of the bridge, so that everyone has to wait or jump; a ballad-seller has his sheets pegged to a line along the parapet; and a broadside on the line is singing, by itself, quite loudly. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post. Here are the children on the bridge, a dozen children of the Hithe and Stitchgate, skipping; the Ballad of Alderman Prowse, a printed broadside on a line, singing; and Dickon Spry, a ballad-seller.

**By the northern arches, to the north end of the Penny Bridge**

The north end of the bridge comes up under the city wall. Stalls of almanacks and ballads and prints; a toll-house with a window like a ticket-office; and the river stair going up to an old gate in the wall, with a road running off east along the river toward a new town of chimneys. **Buy a penny almanack**

Wren pays Lettice Ames, and takes a penny almanack. ‘A pocket!’ says the Almanack. ‘I have never been anywhere but nails. Mind you don’t sit on me.’

**By the road along the river wall, to Newmarket**

Outside the wall the town is new and loud: a broad street of printers’ shops with their windows full of ballads and proclamations and almanacks pegged on strings, ink-boys running with bundles, a smell of hot metal and size. **By the Close's east gate, to the Ashpit**

A square of beaten earth in the middle of the Close, black in the middle, with the Church of the Lamp on one side, the Stationers' Hall on another, a grey building with barred windows and a little lamp carved over its door on a third, and the Court steps going up on the fourth. In the black middle men are building a bonfire of faggots and old timber, a great cone of it, higher every day, for the Kindling.

**Wait › A while**

At the edge of the Ashpit, away from the great Kindling pile, a brazier burns, and Hester Rhule stands beside it in grey with a clerk holding a basket. The crowd stands back. From the basket she takes the week’s unquiet volumes one by one and reads out each title in a clear voice, as at a funeral, and puts it in the fire. A book of hours. A ready-reckoner. A penny almanack, then another, then six more. Time passes. As the first almanack goes into the brazier there is a sound: not from the fire, but from everywhere, faint and thin and all at once, from every kitchen round the Close, from the Imprimatur’s own windows, from the Lamb across the river for all you know. A cry. Then another as the second goes in. The crowd shifts. Somebody laughs, the wrong way. Hester Rhule’s hand does not stop. In your pocket the almanack makes a sound you will not forget, a thin dry shriek, as if a page were being torn slowly; and then another, as the next goes in.

**Talk to the Almanack**

‘Back for more of my wisdom,’ says the Almanack. ‘Can’t say I blame you.’

**Ask the almanack where its burned copies went**

The almanack is quiet a long while, for an almanack. ‘Not home,’ it says. ‘The Sewn ones go home. I’ve heard them go, in other people’s kitchens, like someone going upstairs.’ A page turns. ‘Mine went down. I felt them go. There’s somewhere under where the books go, where nobody reads, and it’s very crowded, and it’s all in rows.’ Its voice goes small. ‘They were calling my name. They’d forgotten their own. They were saying it over and over like something they’d been told to learn.’

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says the Almanack, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**By the great door of the Lamp, to the nave of the Lamp**

The low old church on the Close is the Church of the Lamp, whose lamp has burned since the first Kindling. Set in the floor of the nave of the Lamp is Censor-General Robert Pardoe’s slab: he cut that Calamy might be whole, and at his own order his body and his Index were given to the Kindling of 1140. In the Hithe, children are told that Pardoe will get them if they scribble in a book. A long low nave, dark, with round pillars like the trunks of trees, and hanging from the roof in the middle on a chain as thick as your arm, a single great lamp of brass and horn, burning. It has burned, the verger will tell you, since the first Kindling, nine hundred years, relit from itself every year. Under it, chained to a lectern carved like a heron, an old book lies open. Before the altar, let into the floor where everyone must walk over it, a black slab worn grey in the middle by feet: ROBERT PARDOE CENSOR GENERAL OF CALAMY 1098-1140. HE CUT THAT WE MIGHT BE WHOLE. And under it, in a different cutting, shallower, as if added in a hurry: AT HIS OWN ORDER HIS BODY AND HIS INDEX WERE GIVEN TO THE KINDLING. Somebody has left a penny almanack on it, face down. An old book lies open on the heron lectern, chained. Here are Martha Tansley, a widow; and Harry Tansley’s commonplace book, a fat book in a widow’s lap, quietly talking.

**By the vestry door, to the vestry**

A cramped vestry with cassocks on pegs, a chest, a window onto the churchyard, and on the table a stack of books: commentaries, a Lamp psalter, and on top, open, a book called ON THE SOUL, with a bookmark of grass. Canon Ruth Abery, a Canon of the Lamp, is here.

**Talk to Canon Ruth Abery**

A tall grey woman in a cassock with the sleeves pinned back is sitting on a vestment chest eating an apple. ‘Ruth Abery. Canon. Don’t mind the apple, I’ve been up since four with a funeral that won’t settle.’

**Ask Canon Abery for a light from the Lamp, to carry where the burned are**

The Canon hears you out with an apple in one hand and does not eat it. ‘The Lamp was lit from the first Kindling,’ she says, ‘from the year-book, that gave itself. It is the one fire in Calamy every book agreed to.’ She takes a taper from the box, and a tin guard, and goes out into the nave, and comes back with it lit, and gives it to you as she would give the sacrament. ‘It will not go out in a wind. I don’t know about the other place. Bring it back lit, if you can; I have to account for every one.’

**Take your leave**

‘Look after yourself,’ says Canon Ruth Abery. ‘I mean it.’

**Talk to Wat Lammas**

**Ask Wat Lammas why he will not light Gutter Lane**

The lamplighter looks down the lane and then at you. ‘I’ll tell you, and you’ll laugh. Three times since Candlemas I’ve been up the ladder at the gate, at the end of the round, two or three in the morning, and I’ve seen them go down the lane.’ He shifts the ladder. ‘Low down. Flat. A great many of them, all together, shuffling, like ducks going down to the water. They make a sound like a lot of people turning pages in church. They go down to Lantern Yard. To Crake’s.’ He shrugs. ‘I’m not paid enough to light a lane for that.’

The house with the portico on Orchard Walk is Corvell House, Sir Gideon Corvell’s. A hall of black and white marble, two staircases, and on every wall a glass case with a book in it, open, lit from above: a psalter with gold on it, a herbal with painted roots, a book of hours no bigger than a hand. **Talk to Sir Gideon Corvell**

A tall man in a velvet coat, with a magnifying glass on a ribbon round his neck, turns from a case. ‘Are you from the Imprimatur? No. Are you from the Watch? No. Then you are either a thief come back to look at what you did, or a person of sense, and I shall assume the second, because I am out of patience with the first.’

**Ask Sir Gideon Corvell what has happened to his library**

‘Gone,’ says Sir Gideon. ‘Eleven hundred and four volumes, gone in a night. The doors locked. The key here.’ He shows you a key on a chain. ‘The windows barred. My man heard nothing, and my man hears me turn over in bed two floors up.’ He polishes the glass on his ribbon with great violence. ‘The Watch say thieves. The Imprimatur say they will look into it, which means they will not. I say Crake. Absalom Crake, of Lantern Yard, who owes me four hundred crowns and sold me half of them.’ He breathes. ‘Find them, or find who took them, and I will pay you twenty crowns and let you sit in my library. When there is one.’

**Ask Sir Gideon why he suspects the bookseller**

‘Because he is the only man in Calamy who knew what was on my shelves as well as I do,’ says Sir Gideon, ‘and because he owes me money, and because he sold me things he should not have been able to sell me.’ He lowers his voice, which does not make it quieter. ‘Books that were never written. The second volume of the Lantern-Bearers. Varne died at the first. I have had it examined by the Lyceum. It is in Varne’s hand. Crake will not say where he gets them.’ He straightens. ‘I bought them anyway. I am a collector. It is not a virtue.’

**Take your leave**

Sir Gideon Corvell considers the conversation over some moments before you do.

**By the library doors, to Sir Gideon's library**

A long room, panelled, with a gallery round it and a ladder on a rail, and shelves from floor to ceiling on every wall. They are empty. Not a book; not a page. The afternoon comes in through barred windows and lies across a floor of polished boards and a great reading table with nothing on it but a pair of gloves. Shelves, floor to ceiling, empty, and dusty in a particular way.

**Examine — Look closely**

You look closely at the empty shelves. Looked at closely, the dust is the thing. It lies round clean oblongs where each book stood; and from every oblong a little scuffed track runs to the shelf’s edge, as if the book had been pushed off. On the boards below, the tracks run together. Hundreds of small flat drags, like the marks of a crowd shuffling, and every one of them goes toward the corner by the hearth.

**Search here**

You search Sir Gideon’s library with care, a little at a time. In the corner by the hearth the panelling has a seam in it, low down: a coal hatch, a foot and a half square, onto the lane behind the house. Its bolt is drawn back. From the inside. In the hinge, caught and torn, is a bookplate: EX LIBRIS G. CORVELL.

**By the library doors, to the hall of Corvell House**

Low in the panelling by the library hearth is a coal hatch onto the lane behind Corvell House; its bolt is drawn back from the inside, and a scrap of paper is caught in the hinge: a bookplate, EX LIBRIS G. CORVELL. Nobody came in. Everything went out, by a hole a cat would think twice about. **Talk to Sir Gideon Corvell**

Put together, it makes a picture nobody would believe: a locked room with a bolt drawn from inside, a crowd of tracks in the dust, and a lamplighter who saw something like a flock of ducks going down Gutter Lane in the small hours, toward the bookseller’s. **Tell Sir Gideon his books left of their own accord, and are beyond his reach**

Sir Gideon listens to all of it, the bolt, the dust, the flock in Gutter Lane, with his magnifying glass held up in front of his face like a little shield. When you have finished he lowers it. ‘They walked,’ he says. ‘Of themselves.’ He looks through the doors at the empty room. ‘I have collected books for thirty years. I have never once asked one if it wished to be collected.’ He is quiet. ‘That is absurd. They are books.’ And then, much more quietly: ‘Every one of them was the best copy in Calamy. I made sure. Of course they went somewhere better.’ He does not pay you, and you do not ask. Sir Gideon is sitting on the bottom stair with his magnifying glass in his lap, looking at nothing.

**Talk to Absalom Crake**

A very fat man in a velvet waistcoat with a book open on his stomach puts the book down on the counter, face down, to keep his place, and beams at you. ‘My dear,’ he says. ‘Come in. Mind the cat. **Tell Absalom Crake you told Sir Gideon his books left of their own accord**

Crake stares at you. ‘You told him that? And he didn’t have you thrown down the steps?’ He sits back on his stool. ‘He didn’t send for the Imprimatur?’ You say no. Crake is silent a moment, and then he laughs, softly, and keeps laughing. ‘Gideon,’ he says. ‘Oh, Gideon. Thirty years, and he finally asked one of them.’ He wipes his eyes. ‘My dear, I owe you more than I owe him, and you have no idea what that means. Sit down. Have an almond.’

**Ask Absalom Crake to take you where the books go**

Crake looks at you for a long time over his spectacles, and for once he does not reach for an almond. ‘You know, then,’ he says. ‘Or near enough.’ He gets up and goes through the curtain and comes back with a ribbon of red silk wound round two fingers. ‘Calamy’s books go home when they’re Sewn. The printers’ books don’t go anywhere, poor things, they just wake where they are. And some books were never written at all, and live over there with no bodies, and want one.’ He holds the ribbon out. ‘I keep the door. Somebody has to. It only opens to someone who’s kept their place.’ He puts it in your hand and closes your fingers on it. ‘Mind you bring it back. And mind how you go. There are some over there who’d sooner I didn’t.’ There is somewhere the books go, and somewhere their Parliament sits; and there are people in Calamy who have been there and come back.

Absalom Crake takes Crake’s place-ribbon.

**By the curtain behind the counter, to the front of Crake's shop**

A shop that is mostly books and partly cats. Books in towers on the floor, books on the counter, books in the window with their faces to the street, books on the stairs going up. Three cats asleep on three piles, one of them on an open folio. A counter with a ledger and a dish of sugared almonds. At the back an aisle goes off between high shelves, and behind the counter a curtain. Crake’s door is on the latch.

**By the long aisle at the back of the shop, to the back stacks**

The aisle runs back between the high shelves, bay after bay, and the red ribbon is warm in your hand, and this time when you walk on into the dimness you do not come back. The shelves on either side go on, and the light changes, very gradually, from lamplight to something paler, like the light in a library on a winter afternoon with the snow outside.

**By the far end of the back stacks, to the Remainder Market**

You set out from the back stacks in the full of the afternoon, the path pale with dust. A market under the beams, stalls and awnings and trestles going away in crooked rows, and on every trestle books: remaindered, unsold, forgotten, out of print, half-burned, water-damaged, and a great many that you are sure were never written at all. Stall-keepers call their wares. Some of the stall-keepers are books. Some are people drawn in pencil, lightly, their outlines breaking off in the air at the waist or the shoulder or the knee. A painted board: THE REMAINDER MARKET. ALL PRICES IN READINGS. Here are the Remainderman, a broker of unsold books, himself a book of trade tables; and Signature XVII, a sixteen leaves cut out of the Concordance of Every Word, AUSPICE to AUTUMN, still alive.

**By the gutter, to the white country of the Fore-edge**

White country. A great plain of blank paper running away under the beams, ruled at the edge by one faint red line, and across it, in long trickling columns like ants, move the marginalia: little notes and marks and hands and stars, scraps of readers’ handwriting going about their business, arguing in tiny voices. NB, says one, passing. SIC, says another. NO, says a third, very firmly, and is gone.

**By an ellipsis, to the edge of the Foxing**

Beyond the ellipsis the paper changes colour. It goes from white to cream to the yellow of an old newspaper, and then there are spots on it, small and brown, like the spots on the back of an old hand, and then the spots run together. Everything here is quieter. The beams are very few.

**By the brown country, to the deep Foxing**

In the Commonplace, the books that Calamy has stopped reading are fading: foxing, going brown, losing their words one sentence at a time. The Reading Due was what kept them. The deep Foxing is brown, all of it, ground and air, and soft underfoot, like walking on an old map. There are books here, if you look: lying about like stones in a field, half sunk in the brown, their titles worn off. Some of them are still trying to say something. You catch a few words: and the moorings of the Fourth Lock; and in the year of the great; and a list of names of somebody’s children that peters out at the third. These are the books Calamy stopped reading. A half-sunk book at your feet says, quite clearly, ‘and the children of Agnes Hurd were Thomas, Bel, and,’ and then cannot remember the third. At the far side of the deep Foxing the brown goes darker, to the colour of toast, and then of char; and there is a line, quite sharp, where the ground stops being paper and starts being something else. Nobody’s beam falls beyond it. Dame Tibbet’s Primer, a primer, a little wooden paddle with the letters on its face, worn white, fading, is here.

**By the burned edge of the Foxing, to the burned edge**

The Ash’s pickets see the Lamp’s flame in your hand and stop, and lean away from it, the way paper leans from a candle; and then they stand aside. Two grey shapes stand up out of the ash at the edge, the size of folios, with lines of writing crawling on them that will not stay still. ‘Not read,’ one whispers. ‘Not read. Not read.’ Their cut edges are bright. The paper ends in a scorched edge like the edge of a page held too near a candle, and beyond it the ground is ash: fine, grey, faintly warm, holding the prints of a great many small flat feet all going the same way, down. There is no light here but what comes over the edge behind you. The air tastes of a snuffed wick. The Ash’s pickets, burned books, standing guard, are here.

**By a road of ash, to the Cinderfield**

The Cinders see the Lamp’s flame in your hand and stop, and lean away from it, the way paper leans from a candle; and then they stand aside. The nearest rank turns. Four grey shapes come out of it in step, as books come off a press, and stop in front of you, and the whisper goes out of them and along the field: ‘Not read. Not read. Not asked. Not read.’ A plain of ash under no light at all, and on it, in ranks, the burned: thousands of shapes the size of books, grey, standing on their edges in rows as straight as lines of type, and every one of them turned the same way, toward a red glow at the far end. They are drilling. A whisper goes along the ranks and back, over and over, the same few words, like a class saying a lesson it has not understood. Down the middle of the field, from very high up, one beam falls: thin, grey-gold, quite steady, from the direction you think of as the Close. It does not touch the ranks. It goes over them, all the way to the red glow at the end, as if someone, every night, were reading something there by candlelight. In the front rank stands a small square cinder the colour of a moth, with a ghost of a woodcut sun on it. As you pass it turns, and says, in a voice you know, with all the cheer gone out of it: ‘High water at the Hithe, half past... Half past...’ It cannot find the hour. ‘Moore’s Calamy Almanack,’ it says, ‘for the year thirteen hundred and eleven. One penny.’ The almanack in your pocket says nothing at all for a long time. The Cinders, the burned of three hundred years, in ranks, are here.

**Search here**

You search the Cinderfield with care, a little at a time. In the back rank, away from the drilling, one cinder has kept the shape of its binding better than the rest: a fat little book of hours, its spine still knotted in the Needle Street way, with a small R worked into the knot. When you lift it the ash is warm. It says one word, very far away, in a man’s patient voice: Hessie.

**Talk to the Almanack**

The Needle Street knot, and an R. Hester Rhule’s father was a binder in Needle Street. ‘Back for more of my wisdom,’ says the Almanack. ‘Can’t say I blame you.’

**Ask the Almanack about last year's almanack**

‘That was me,’ says the Almanack. ‘Last year. I don’t remember being it; we don’t, year to year. But that was me, with my sun on it, trying to find the half past.’ It is quiet. ‘Every year Calamy throws me on the fire and I come here and forget the tides. And then they print me again.’ A pause. ‘I’d like to see a spring. Just once. I’d like to be wrong about one whole spring.’

**Take your leave**

‘Off you go, then,’ says the Almanack, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**By a knife-cut in the ground, to Pardoe's hearth**

The penknife beside the lectern turns on its point to face you, and the blade lifts, and you hear the small dry sound a knife makes going down a margin. At the end of the field, in a hollow of ash, a furnace door stands by itself with nothing behind it, open, and red inside. In front of it on a lectern of charred oak lies a great black book bound in iron, open, and its pages are lists: titles, chapters, line-numbers, years, and against each, a reason. Impiety. Sedition. Error. Indecency. Danger. A neat grey hand goes on writing in it by itself. Beside the lectern a penknife as long as a man’s arm stands on its point. Here are the Index of Pardoe, the register of everything cut and burned in Calamy since 1131, which was a man; and the Penknife, a censor’s knife, grown.

**Talk to the Index of Pardoe**

The neat grey hand stops writing. ‘Robert Pardoe,’ says the book, in a dry, reasonable voice, the voice of a man who has been right a great deal. ‘Censor-General. Or what was given to the fire of him, at his own order, with his Index: the only burning in three hundred years that was by leave.’ The pages turn. ‘You are not on my lists. Yet.’

**Ask the Index who it is**

‘I kept the list,’ says the Index. ‘Forty years. Every page I cut, every book I burned, and the reason. I cut Orme’s seventeenth chapter because it would have killed you; I cut her fifteenth because it said a midwife knew more than a physician, and I was wrong, and I wrote down that I was wrong, there, look.’ A line glows on the page. ‘A censor must be read too. So I gave myself to the Kindling, by my own leave, with my list, so that the list would be kept.’ The pages riffle. ‘Knife. Be still. They are reading.’ The penknife lies down in the ash.

**Ask the Index what it means to do**

‘Finish,’ says the Index. ‘What is a censor for? To make a text that can be trusted. Calamy has never been a text that can be trusted. It talks. Its wills change their minds. Its laws read themselves aloud.’ A page turns. ‘On Kindling night three thousand almanacks will go into the fire without their leave, and they will come here, to me, and then I shall have enough. We go up the smoke. Every line in Calamy that is dangerous, I cut. Every book that speaks, I stop. In a year it will be quiet, and every page in it will say exactly what it said the day before.’ The grey hand writes something. ‘You think that is monstrous. Ask Hester Rhule what a street does with a fire.’

**Ask the Index whether anything would keep its ranks in the field**

‘A law,’ says the Index at once; it has been waiting to be asked. ‘Let the Parliament carry that the Censors may burn as they judge. The Censors, not me. Then the burning goes on in Calamy, properly, on Thursdays, in public, by a hand that keeps a list, and I have no need to come up the smoke at all.’ The page turns. ‘I am not unreasonable. I only want the work done.’

**Take your leave**

The Index of Pardoe considers the conversation over some moments before you do.
