# Nobody does anything: the Year of Voices

Seed `idle`; 227 steps; ended at watch 1296, at the ending "The Year of Ash"; sealed: door_shut, fire_leave, read_rounds, sew_all. Written by `bun scripts/parliament-of-books-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

A raised causeway of black stones crosses the saltings toward a town of roofs and masts. Ahead of you a long bridge goes over the river on a great many arches, so crowded with sheds and stalls that it looks like a street that has wandered out over the water. Beyond it, a walled town climbs to a single hill with a tower on top; downriver, to your right, the reed-beds go grey-green all the way to the sea. Under a pair of mills two wheels turn slowly in the river. Gulls. The air smells of mud and, faintly and strangely, of wet paper.

**By the causeway into the Hithe, to the Hithe quay**

The Hithe quay is cobbled and wet and loud. Eel-boats lie two and three deep at the steps; a fishwife cries her baskets; a gang of boys rolls rag bales up a plank into a lighter. Along the back of the quay stand a tavern under a painted lantern, a customs house with a great barred warehouse behind it, and a crowd of people round a board nailed to a post. At the west end the steps go down to a ferry; at the east, the quay turns into a market of rags. On the post a printed notice, fresh, with the Imprimatur’s little lamp stamped at the foot: UNQUIET VOLUMES. Under it someone has chalked a drawing of a book with legs, running, and someone else has rubbed most of it out. Bet Kettering, a fishwife, is here.

**By the bridge foot, to the south end of the Penny Bridge**

The bridge begins as a street: sheds and stalls on both sides built out over the water on brackets, so that you walk between walls of books and ballads and pies with the river showing through the cracks in the planks. A tooth-drawer, a pie-man, three second-hand bookstalls with their tables tilted to the light, and a puppet booth with a striped curtain and a crowd of children in front of it. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post.

**By the southern arches, to the chapel pier**

Halfway over, the bridge widens round a pier with a tiny chapel on it no bigger than a cart, with a bell in a hutch on its roof. Children are skipping a long rope across the whole width of the bridge, so that everyone has to wait or jump; a ballad-seller has his sheets pegged to a line along the parapet; and a broadside on the line is singing, by itself, quite loudly. The bridge gate stands open, the chain coiled on its post. Here are the children on the bridge, a dozen children of the Hithe and Stitchgate, skipping; the Ballad of Alderman Prowse, a printed broadside on a line, singing; and Dickon Spry, a ballad-seller.

**By the northern arches, to the north end of the Penny Bridge**

The north end of the bridge comes up under the city wall. Stalls of almanacks and ballads and prints; a toll-house with a window like a ticket-office; and the river stair going up to an old gate in the wall, with a road running off east along the river toward a new town of chimneys. Lettice Ames, an almanack-seller, is here.

**By the road along the river wall, to Newmarket**

Outside the wall the town is new and loud: a broad street of printers’ shops with their windows full of ballads and proclamations and almanacks pegged on strings, ink-boys running with bundles, a smell of hot metal and size. At the end of it a cart arch opens into a yard stacked with paper bales, and over the arch, in letters a foot high: DOBLE. PRINTER TO THE CITY.

**By the Close's east gate, to the Ashpit**

A square of beaten earth in the middle of the Close, black in the middle, with the Church of the Lamp on one side, the Stationers' Hall on another, a grey building with barred windows and a little lamp carved over its door on a third, and the Court steps going up on the fourth. In the black middle men are building a bonfire of faggots and old timber, a great cone of it, higher every day, for the Kindling.

An hour passes. It is a long time since you slept. You are weary, and will fight the worse for it until you sleep.

**Wait › An hour**

At the edge of the Ashpit, away from the great Kindling pile, a brazier burns, and Hester Rhule stands beside it in grey with a clerk holding a basket. The crowd stands back. From the basket she takes the week’s unquiet volumes one by one and reads out each title in a clear voice, as at a funeral, and puts it in the fire. A book of hours. A ready-reckoner. A penny almanack, then another, then six more. As the first almanack goes into the brazier there is a sound: not from the fire, but from everywhere, faint and thin and all at once, from every kitchen round the Close, from the Imprimatur’s own windows, from the Lamb across the river for all you know. A cry. Then another as the second goes in. The crowd shifts. Somebody laughs, the wrong way. Hester Rhule’s hand does not stop.

**Wait › An hour**

The Ashpit is packed to the walls with Calamy: millers and binders and printers and fishwives, students on the Stationers' Hall steps, the Lord Mayor on a cart. The great pile stands in the middle, unlit, taller than the Stationers' Hall door. Everyone has an unlit lantern. Almost everyone has an almanack. And the almanacks, all of them, in all their hands, are quite silent. And still they come: over the bridge and down the Gannet Steps and in from Newmarket and up from the Hithe: whole households, carrying lanterns not yet lit, and under their arms, in their aprons, in their children’s hands, last year’s almanack.

**Wait › An hour**

Hester Rhule stands at the foot of the pile in grey with a lit torch, very straight, as she stands every Thursday at the brazier. An hour passes.

**Wait › An hour**

The bell on the chapel pier rings once, far off over the water, and the Lamp’s verger carries a taper out of the church on a long pole, and the great pile on the Ashpit takes fire from the bottom up, with a sound like a crowd drawing breath.

**Wait › An hour**

And Calamy throws the old year on the fire, as its grandmothers did. Three thousand penny almanacks go into the Kindling, from three thousand hands, and the sound is not the sound of paper burning: it is one voice, small, everywhere at once, crying out and going on crying out for a long time, and then not. People light their lanterns and go home quickly, not looking at each other.

*(6 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

The Ashpit is black and cold, with a great round of ash where the Kindling was. Children are already drawing on it with sticks. The smoke of the Kindling does not go up. All night it lies over Calamy like a fog, grey, faintly warm, smelling of a snuffed wick; and in it, if you stand still, you can hear whispering, a great many voices saying the same few words. In the morning the fog has gone into the houses. There are words missing. A will in Orchard Walk has a blank where the heir was. The Pleas in the Court of Pleas have a gap where the Sixth Plea was, cut very clean. Children on the bridge skip to the Kindling rhyme and cannot remember the third line. Something is going through Calamy with a knife, carefully, a little every night.

*(7 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

The smoke of the Kindling lay over Calamy all night and went into the houses. Three thousand almanacks went down to the Cinderfield without their leave, and the Index had its regiments, and came up the smoke. Nothing is burned in Calamy now; nothing needs to be. Every night a little more is cut, very cleanly: a line from a will, a verse from the rhyme, a name from a gravestone, the Sixth Plea. The books do not speak any more. Neither, much, do the people. In a year, the Index says from every page that is left, it will be quiet, and every page in it will say exactly what it said the day before.
