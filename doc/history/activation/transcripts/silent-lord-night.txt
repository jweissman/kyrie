[Ring the bell and ask the porter] A walled keep stands on a rise above the fields, smoke at its chimneys and a banner over its gate. A fox crosses the way ahead and is gone. Below the north wall the ditch has been scythed clean in one place, and a stone outfall there breathes out a thread of cold air that smells of old water. Nothing else on the wall has been tended. A bell-rope hangs by the wicket. A banner hangs over the gate, a tower with a star over it in white on green, and under it, lower, a black cloth the width of a hand that has been there some days. The bell rings somewhere inside, and goes on ringing. After a while a lamp comes to the grating and a voice asks who comes at this hour. You tell it. The wicket opens a hand's breadth, and then enough.
(the dice are held to none)
[Into the keep by the gate] The gatehouse is a vaulted passage with a guardroom on either side, a murder-hole in the roof over your head and a lamp in a niche. The portcullis is wound up in its slot; below it the porter's stool stands against the wall. Names and tallies are scratched into the plaster by men who waited out their watches here, and under the lamp one hand has written, over and over, in a good clerk's letters: not yet.
[To the ward] The hounds come out of the dark in a loose arc, low and silent, and the one in front stops a stride short with its lip lifted. The ward is trodden earth round a well-head, with the stables to the east and the granary to the west, the hall steps going up on the far side and a mounting-block that has been used more than anything else here. The ward is dark but for the lamp over the hall steps. Chains are dragged in the yard by the kennels, and a low sound goes with them. The armoury door is locked. The marshal wears the key.
