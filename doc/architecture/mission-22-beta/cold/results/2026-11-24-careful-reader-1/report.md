# Cold play: careful reader, Wren (birdfolk bard, once an inquisitor, born under the Lantern), report

revision ef6da210, world iscoed, viewport 1280x800; 146 presses (counting arrow-key previews)

**Clarity: 6 / 10**
**Wanting to go on: 7 / 10**

## What I wanted
- **Choose a character who reads and asks.** Done: a Birdfolk Bard, once an Inquisitor, born under the Lantern. I could read every option before choosing, but only after I found by guessing that the arrow keys preview and a press chooses.
- **Get out of the dark adit.** Done. I lit a candle, beat the badger in a bout I understood (one bolt), and lifted the prop with Dai Probert. Dai ran off home with a warm farewell.
- **Claim my aunt Tegwen's weir.** I formed this goal from the lease in my pack. It was never taken up as an aim. Nest tells me she has kept the weir going since the burying, and then the weir disappears from the game.
- **Find out why the sea-gate won't shut.** Done, and it was the best part. I found the missing pawl in the gate-well silt, saw its pin was sawn through, hired a strong pilgrim (Gesk) when the windlass was too heavy, and shut the gate on the third try.
- **Find out who sawed the pin.** This half-resolved itself. Ifan Rhys turned up in a smuggling boat and argued over the toll, and I chose to let his boat through for one tide. The aim then quietly vanished. He never admitted it, and I was never offered a way to accuse him.
- **Next: tell the reeve at Porthalen.** I stopped here.

## What worked
- **The prose.** At its best it is lovely and economical:
  - "Your foot finds it before your hands do";
  - "The pawl drops into its socket with a sound like a bell… and the mill-race sound stops";
  - "Ifan lifts a hand from the pole as he passes under. Nobody else sees it";
  - the old dog putting its chin on my boot;
  - Gek's too-bright coin ("It buys a loaf. I have never spent it.").
- **The sea-gate mystery.** It rewarded careful reading. I guessed the empty socket wanted a pawl, searched and found it, and the sawn pin turned the job into a mystery.
- **Odds that answer to the world.** The attempt went from "unlikely" to "an even chance" when I hired a strong companion, and "How it fell" shows the die honestly.
- **Ifan's choice.** A real moral knot: the town's safety against his children eating. The quiet consequence fit it.
- **Risk stated before the fight.** "It is a bout, to the first fall: no one will be killed." I fought because I understood the risk.
- **The book's Notes.** Who told me what, and when, is a good instrument for a reader.
- **Character creation's reading material.** The star signs' one-line sayings and the skill glosses.
- **The paintings and the encyclopedia.** The candlelit adit, the storm over the sea-bank, and the parchment encyclopedia with its badger plate are handsome.

## Verdict
I would go on. The sea-gate story was a small, complete pleasure. I read, noticed the empty socket, searched, found the sawn pin, hired help, shut the gate, and was handed a quiet moral choice with a consequence only I saw. That is what a careful reader wants. What held me back was the interface, not the world. Rows I needed kept vanishing behind the Look and Talk views. Number presses sometimes did the wrong thing. The most important paragraph of the opening was faded off the drawn page. And small continuity slips kept reminding me the scene was assembled:
- the boy still afraid of a badger I'd beaten;
- sun on a rainy morning;
- a man mending a trap at his door while poling a boat under my feet.

The thing that brought me to Gatesend, my aunt's weir, was dropped once the gate took over, and I'd like it back. I'd play another hour to see Porthalen and the reeve. If the weir never returns and the controls keep hiding, I'd likely drift off.
