# The Numbered House, kahitude, a human mage alone: the Question Hall and its hum, the drawer ring, the governor and the lift.
# Played by a driver over the page's own labels. The company is set down at the Hall with '@hall' to skip the walk (the Bridge is crossed light by a mage).
# This is a machine-played transcript, not a person's play.
[Ask the tablet marked with steps] The lamp-hollow beside the tablet fills with a pale oil that was not there, and the brass hums an order of three notes: middle, high, low. The hum has risen a little, and the lamp-hollows ring faintly to it, one after another, like a row of glasses. There is no ambiguity in it. The tablet has settled the matter of the stair. The tablet with the steps has given its answer: the stair of the Lodge is struck MIDDLE, HIGH, LOW. The surveyors had it right.
[Ask the tablet marked with a disc and a hand] The tablet’s lamp-hollow brightens, and a pale oil fills it, and the brass says its answer once, in a voice like a bell heard through a wall. The tablet with the disc and the hand has answered: THE DISC RISES FOR THE COUNTED, WHEN THE RING IS SET TO THE LIGHT AT NOON, OR TO ANY LIGHT THROWN BY THE SURVEYORS’ PLATE.
[Ask the tablet marked with a notched ring] The tablet’s lamp-hollow brightens, and a pale oil fills it; the brass says its answer, and on the last word hums for a moment out of tune, as if the number were an effort. The hum has climbed a step. In the niches along the wall, the oil in the lamp-hollows stands trembling in its bowls. The tablet with the ring has answered: THE RING OF THE DRAWERS STANDS AT five.
[Ask the tablet marked with a row of dots] The row of dots on the tablet brightens one by one, counting, and stops one dot beyond what you would have said. Down the hall, the lamp-glass of three pairs of eyes comes alight. The figures in the niches step out of them, in step, and take their places across the arches, with their hands at their sides. They have not been told to attack anyone yet. The dots stand in their row, and there is one more than your party and the House together can account for. The keepers stand across the arch into the gallery, shoulder to shoulder, with their hands at their sides. The keepers stand across the Reckoners’ door, shoulder to shoulder, with their hands at their sides. The tablet with the dots has answered: THE COUNTED ARE A NUMBER, AND ONE PLACE MORE IS KEPT THAN THEY. The keepers stand across the arches in a line, their eyes lit, their hands at their sides.
[Wait › An hour] The hum has climbed a step. In the niches along the wall, the oil in the lamp-hollows stands trembling in its bowls. An hour passes.
[Wait › An hour] The hum has risen a little, and the lamp-hollows ring faintly to it, one after another, like a row of glasses. In the niches along the wall, three tall figures of brass plate stand among the tablets, their lamp-glass eyes dark, so still that you took them for statuary. The hum falls, and with it the lights in the keepers’ eyes. They turn, one after another, and go back to their niches.
[Wait › An hour] An hour passes.
[To the Drawer Gallery] It is pitch dark. Without a light you can see nothing here, and do nothing that needs seeing.
[a candle: Light] You light a candle. A gallery of drawers, floor to roof, each with a brass face and a number, and rolling ladders on rails to reach the high ones. A reading-desk stands in the middle of it with a book chained open on the slope. One bank of drawers has a notched ring fixed in the middle of its face, as big as a dinner-plate. The lowest rows stand a hand in water, and the brass has gone green; damp has got into the numbering, and a few of the drawers have swelled shut. Scratched on the brass beside the ring, in a rough hand, a column of figures with a line through each: TWO, THREE, FOUR. They tried them in order, and the drawer jammed, and a monk wrote the next beside it. A rolling ladder on its rail stands at the foot of a stair that goes up to the rim of the Great Void, a hundred feet of light and air above, to which it is joined by a long unrailed catwalk. A ring of bronze is set in the middle of the drawer-face, cut with six notches round its rim and a mark on the face that the notches pass under. It stands at one. The drawer behind the ring is shut. The ring can be turned a notch at a time, and pressed home. A book as long as a man is tall is chained open on the slope of the desk. Its pages are ruled in columns of names, in a hundred hands, and the last page written is a name in no script and a number. The next line is ruled, and empty, and the chain that holds the book has a loop of chalk-dust at its end, as if chalk had been kept there.
[Press the ring home] You lean on the ring. The ring goes home with a flat clank, and every drawer in the gallery shudders in its bank. The one before you jams. The hum climbs. The ring has been pressed home wrong, and the drawer has jammed with a click like a tongue.
[Wait › An hour] The drawer behind the ring is shut. The ring can be turned a notch at a time, and pressed home. The drawer’s jam lets go with a click, and the ring stands free again.
[Turn the ring one notch] The ring clicks round one notch. A ring of bronze is set in the middle of the drawer-face, cut with six notches round its rim and a mark on the face that the notches pass under. It stands at two.
[Turn the ring one notch] The ring clicks round one notch. A ring of bronze is set in the middle of the drawer-face, cut with six notches round its rim and a mark on the face that the notches pass under. It stands at three.
[Turn the ring one notch] The ring clicks round one notch. A ring of bronze is set in the middle of the drawer-face, cut with six notches round its rim and a mark on the face that the notches pass under. It stands at four.
[Turn the ring one notch] The ring clicks round one notch. A ring of bronze is set in the middle of the drawer-face, cut with six notches round its rim and a mark on the face that the notches pass under. It stands at five.
[Press the ring home] You lean on the ring. The ring goes home with a sound like a latch lifting, and the drawer slides out on its runners, smooth as milk. The drawer has slid open on its runners.
[Take from the drawer of the ring] You take the lift’s governor from the drawer of the ring.
[Fit the governor and free the cage using the lift's governor] The lift’s governor is used up. The lift’s engine has its governor fitted; the gears turn together and the cage hangs level in the shaft. A brass lever on the lattice wall is marked UP and DOWN in the Empire’s numerals.
[To the foot of the Great Void] The floor of the Void is a bowl of rubble and fallen leaves, a hundred feet down from the sky and a hundred paces across, grown over with ferns that live on the light from the broken roof. The waterfall lands in a pool at one side and goes down through a grating. The air is damp and cool and the sound of the waterfall fills it to the edges. Things have fallen here. A bundle of tent-cloth; a pair of boots, one with a foot still in it; the shaft of a surveyor’s tripod; a lantern with its glass cracked. Part of a ladder, cut square at both ends. A white scatter of bones. The owlbear, a great shaggy thing, fed by what falls, is here. Something huge uncoils among the ferns at the edge of the pool and turns its head to look at you. It is covered in feathers and fur in equal parts, and its eyes are round and yellow. It breathes in, slowly, through a beak the length of your forearm. It will not have you here long. The owlbear will give you a few minutes, no more.
