[To the landing at Thornhaven] A quay of grey stone runs out into the water at Thornhaven, its edge worn round and green with weed, and the town climbs behind it. Gulls sit along it in a row, facing the wind, over the slap of water on stone. The Fishwater runs by below the houses, and beyond the roofs lies the Moonmere. The town herd has been driven twice this month from the verge of the old road by the Hall’s keeper, and the swineherd has opinions about it that he is happy to share with anyone who will stand still. A bear is asleep on the ground by the players’ wagon, with a thin man for a pillow, and a big old man in a faded crimson coat sits on the wagon’s tail-board, squinting up at the sky. A herd-girl from up the hill is telling anyone who will listen that something comes up out of Silkholme sink at night and takes a ewe from the flock by a cave in the hillside in Stonecrag, and that Garth means to burn it out. The bell is gone from its frame over the well. ‘Hobs,’ people say, and spit. ‘Hobs out of Seolforfyrd, where they’ve made their hold.’ Nobody has gone after it. The gangway is drawn in. Someone is tarring an upturned hull on trestles, and the black smell of it hangs over the shingle. Gamel is soldering a hole in a kettle over a little fire, squinting through the smoke. Cuthbert the official is reading a letter with great attention and a seal broken on the table beside it. Hugh is writing a column of figures on a slate and rubbing out the last of them. Alys has an apron with a pocket for every tool and is mending a hinge, tapping the pin home with a small hammer. The inn’s dog lies across the doorstep of the Stormy Horn with his chin on his paws, and watches who comes. A grey cat sits on a wall-top with her eyes half shut and her tail hanging down the stones. Dandelions stand in the grass, yellow and seeding: a tea of them cleans the blood. Amaranth grows in the gardens about the green in the open, its colorful flower clusters standing out among the grass. Paper nailed to the post by the well, written over in several hands. A tall wagon, painted with a sun and a moon and the words GOSSE’S COMPANY in red letters gone pink, stands on the landing at Thornhaven with its shafts down. A chain runs from its back wheel to a post, and the chain has a padlock on it. Wapshott’s bill on the wall says NOW PLAYING, NIGHTLY AT SEVEN, ADMISSION ONE COIN, CHILDREN AND DOGS HALF, on a strip pasted over the word COMING. Here are the castellan, who holds Thornhaven for the house of Ísenheald; Aldous Fenn, the reeve of the wood-wardens, who regulate who cuts what in the woods round Thornhaven; Absalom Gosse, the actor-manager of Gosse’s Company; Hepzibah Dimmock, the players’ prompter; and Jem Pudding, the players’ clown. Patience ambles to the end of her rope to see who you are, and goes through your hands for honey.
[Into Thornhaven Hall by the avenue up to Thornhaven Hall] A house of the grey stone of the hill, long and gabled, its windows set in rows, smoke going up from three chimneys. The forecourt is raked gravel round a mounting-block. Somebody keeps all this, and keeps it well. The front door stands open. The door in the garden wall is on the latch. Wystan Hale, the gamekeeper of the Hall, once a castellan’s spearman, who has been told to stand at the road-wood chain and let nobody cut, is here.
[To the great hall] The hall goes up the whole height of the house to a roof of dark beams. A fire is laid in a hearth carved with the arms of the house, and the long table under the windows is set for nobody. The front door stands open. The steward of the Hall, the steward who keeps the Hall while the family is away, a precise person with a ring of keys, is here.
[Ask the steward of the Hall about the old road] ‘The old paved road?’ says the steward of the Hall. ‘There is a stage-house on it, I am told, out in the woods south-east of Crowmeet: Latusrechies. The family has some right in the ground about it. We have never used it. The papers would say.’
[Set out for Latusrechies] Partway, the path goes down to the water and through it, and up the far bank with your boots full. The road comes down between alders to the Fishwater and crosses it on a ford of flat stones; on the far bank it climbs again toward Elmham. An otter’s head breaks the water downstream and is gone. By the road stands an old milestone, worn to a stub, with a wheel cut in it and something over the wheel that might once have been a crown. The Fishwater runs knee-deep and clear over the ford stones. Cattle fill the road from hedge to hedge, going to Elmham, and somewhere among them is Dunstan, the drover, whistling. A big man in a mended red coat sits on a milestone with a pike across his knees, watching the road both ways. Three men in the rags of a uniform lean on pikes by a fire at the roadside. Willows lean out over the water along the road, grey-green, their roots in the bank and their long shoots trailing in the current. A bramble of goodberries grows along the edge of the trees, the berries small and red. Feverwort grows along the road in the rough grass, bitter-leaved: the herb-wives dry it for a child’s fever. Bay grows along the road with its aromatic leaves and small yellow flowers. Bloodroot grows along the road with its large leaves and clusters of white flowers. Cinquefoil grows along the road with its five-petaled yellow flowers and serrated leaves. Larkspur grows along the road with its small, inconspicuous flowers. Cinnamon grows along the road with its aromatic bark and green leaves. Bruisewort grows along the road with its thick leaves and small purple flowers. Ofbit grows along the road with its delicate blue flowers and thin stems. Here are Sergeant Mawr, who keeps the toll; and the deserters. Pikes come down across the road. ‘Toll,’ says the sergeant, not getting up. ‘Four coin, the lot of you. There’s a war on.’
[Pay the deserters' toll, four coin] The sergeant bites a coin, spits, and waves you through. ‘Mind how you go.’
[Set out for Latusrechies] For a while the path keeps company with a river, close enough to hear it. The road comes out of the woods south-east of Crowmeet as a strip of kerbed stone, straighter than anything the folk have built, and runs through a gate-arch into a walled yard. Thirty paces short of the arch a squat stone post stands at the roadside with a number cut in it, and a worn dish on top. Over the arch, in letters a hand high, the name and the office: LATUSREQUIES, and under it the mark of the Posts: a wheel with a wing. This is Latusrechies, as the folk call it. An owl roosts where a roof-beam was. A squat stone post stands by the kerb with a count cut in it in the old numerals. The dish worn into its top holds rainwater, and in the water, three small coins. Three small coins lie in the dish on top of the post, green with weather, left by someone who did not want to be robbed on the road. Before there was Fearnmaere there was the makers’ well, and the first settlers drank from it and stayed. Elmham is here because the road has to cross the Fishwater somewhere, and here it can. The crossing came first, and the houses came after to sell the crossing what it wanted. Mira gains 20 experience: found Latusrechies.
[Read the numbers cut in the post] The numerals count the miles from the sea-port the road starts from, and under the count: a stage’s distance to the next post, and the next, down the road in both directions. The Posts counted every mile they laid, and told you the count at every one. In the dish are three coins, left by whoever last passed: the folk of Crowmeet still pay the stone before they go on.
[Search here] You search Latusrechies with care, a little at a time. Where the brook goes under the road-wall there is an arch half choked with silt, and the water coming out of it is clear, as water is that has stood in a cistern.
[Into the cistern by the outfall culvert] It is pitch dark. Without a light you can see nothing here, and do nothing that needs seeing.
[Mira: light a candle] You light a candle. Below the yard, a vaulted tank, lined in red plaster, the water in it black and very still. Steps go down into it at one end. A low arch at the far end takes the overflow out under the road-wall. The water in the cistern stands to the third step, black and very still.
[Up to the yard] A cobbled yard ringed by a single range of low buildings, all with their doors to the middle: the stables, the granary, the hall. A stone trough, a well-head with a carved lip, a mounting-block at each door. The surface is lifting wherever a root has found it. A beam of oak as thick as a man’s thigh, black with age and bound in iron, sits across the inner arch in its brackets.
[To the posting-hall] The posting-hall: a long room with a hearth at each end, benches round the walls, a low platform for the clerk, and on the wall facing the door a painted strip of plaster as long as the room, a road running across it between towns and rivers and hills, each marked with a name and a number. Cups stand on the benches, set down, and bowls with a skin of dust. One bowl has a spoon laid carefully across it, as though to say: I’m not done. The kingdom’s lords used the hall as a hunting-lodge: antlers on every beam, the hearth walled up to a smaller size, and a table set where the clerk’s platform was. The painted road is still on the wall, with a hound drawn in the margin by someone with time. Along one wall a road runs from end to end of the room, painted on the plaster, with towns and rivers and the little pictures of posts along it: a wheel with a wing at every stage. Names are lettered beside each, in the old script, and a number. A tall panel of plaster by the door is painted with a notice in the old script, in columns, with figures. The red of the headings is still red.
[Follow the painted road along the wall] On the painted road the Posts also marked, in red, places beside it that they had found and had not made: the Empire’s surveyors set them down and gave them names in their own tongue, as they did everything. One lies south of here, where the road ends: a broken keep. A line in the plaster’s margin gives its distance from this stage, and the turning off the road. You have traced the painted road along the wall with a finger: the stage you stand in is the fourth from the sea-port; the nearest place marked on it that is not a stage is set down in a different hand, with a note: OLDER THAN THE ROAD. Farther along, the ruled line of the painted road bends once, sharply, round a grey blank on the plaster, with no stage and no toll drawn, and one word in the margin in the old script: THE WORKS.
[Read the notice on the door] A road that was kept: its toll counted, its stages a day apart, its disputes sent upward to an officer who was not a local. People were governed by paper here, and the paper was good. The tariff of the stage: so much for a horse, so much for a cart, so much for a letter, so much for a head of cattle; letters of the Emperor’s service carried free; no beast changed without the stationmaster’s seal; no traveller housed more than three nights without a pass. At the foot, smaller, in a different paint: DISPUTES OF THE ROAD TO THE PREFECT OF THE DISTRICT, AND NOT TO THE COUNTRYMEN.
[To the stationmaster's office] A small square room, shelved from floor to ceiling in pigeonholes, each with a lead tag stamped with a number; a desk with a sloped top; a balance with two bronze pans; a stair going up. A smell of old wax and dry dust. The fisc-room door is iron, with a plate of bronze over the keyhole stamped with the wheel and the wing. A seal-ring is cut for it; there is a keyhole as well. Hundreds of little wooden cells, each with a lead tag, each stuffed with tablets of wax gone grey or slips of thin wood, tied in threes. A balance with two bronze pans stands on the desk. One pan is down; the other, empty, is up. In the lower lies a stack of small bronze weights, graded in size, with a mark on each. The day-book lies open on the desk at the last page. It has been written to the middle of a line. A scruffy ledger of grey wooden leaves lies on the sloped desk with a pen in it. The hand is quick, careless, and amused at its own expense.
[Read the clerk's day-book] A young man’s happiness, written down in the Empire’s own ledger, where nobody was ever meant to see it. You hope he got to the Fourth Stone. The clerk who kept this was not meant for the Posts and knew it. He writes about the horses by temperament; he gives the mules names. He is in love with a baker’s daughter in the nearest village, and his love is conducted through the post-bag, and the stationmaster knows and does not interfere. In the margin he draws a map of the road to her door. The last three letters are the shortest: SHE SAYS YES. SHE SAYS YES. WE ARE TO BE MARRIED AT THE FOURTH STONE, ON THE FIRST DAY OF THE NEXT MONTH.
[Read the last page of the day-book] An ordinary hour, then nothing. There is no struggle, no letter, no hurry. Whoever was in the hall at their meal did not finish it; and there are no bones anywhere in the stage, though there are forty stalls and a hall for sixty. The last entry is an ordinary one: FOUR HORSES CHANGED FOR A CART OF OIL. CLERK AT HIS MEAL. WIND IN THE EAST. And then, in the same hand, in the middle of a stroke, the pen leaves the page in a long line, as if lifted. There is nothing more.
