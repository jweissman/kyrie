[To the landing at Thornhaven] A quay of grey stone runs out into the water at Thornhaven, its edge worn round and green with weed, and the town climbs behind it. Gulls sit along it in a row, facing the wind, over the slap of water on stone. The Fishwater runs by below the houses, and beyond the roofs lies the Moonmere. The town herd has been driven twice this month from the verge of the old road by the Hall’s keeper, and the swineherd has opinions about it that he is happy to share with anyone who will stand still. A bear is asleep on the ground by the players’ wagon, with a thin man for a pillow, and a big old man in a faded crimson coat sits on the wagon’s tail-board, squinting up at the sky. A herd-girl from up the hill is telling anyone who will listen that something comes up out of Silkholme sink at night and takes a ewe from the flock by a cave in the hillside in Stonecrag, and that Garth means to burn it out. The bell is gone from its frame over the well. ‘Hobs,’ people say, and spit. ‘Hobs out of Seolforfyrd, where they’ve made their hold.’ Nobody has gone after it. The gangway is drawn in. Someone is tarring an upturned hull on trestles, and the black smell of it hangs over the shingle. Gamel is soldering a hole in a kettle over a little fire, squinting through the smoke. [Ask Aldous Fenn about the chain at the Hall] ‘The chain across the old road at the Hall’s end,’ says Aldous Fenn, ‘was put up six years ago, when the family stopped coming. The steward says the road-wood is the Hall’s and always was. The wardens have cut there since before there was a Hall. My father swore to it.’ He studies his boots. ‘My father was put out of that house for saying so, and the family never paid his last quarter. I am not asking you to be unfair. I am asking you to find the paper. There is one. There is always one.’

[To the Hall kitchen] A flagged kitchen with a hearth you could stable a horse in, a table scarred by a century of cleavers, copper pans in rows, and a dresser with a missing plate. A boy of ten or so sits on the settle by the hearth turning the same potato over and over in his hands. Every so often he hums, three notes down and one up, and stops, and looks surprised. Here are Orla Pennick, the cook of the Hall, who feeds forty on a budget for ten; and Wimble, the kitchen boy, who went up to the dome and came back not knowing his name.
