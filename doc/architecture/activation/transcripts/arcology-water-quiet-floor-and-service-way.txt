[Open the wheel with the roofed house] Far below the Void, a great wheel has been turned, and the water that fed the fourth ring has gone somewhere else. The waterfall thins to a thread, and the bell-lilies lean. The Gardener stops where she stands, and a long moment later bends and picks a dead bell from the bed, and holds it as one holds a child’s tooth. Under the hills, a very long way off, there is a sound like a drawn breath, and then a sound like a river. The wheel with the roofed house is wide, and the pipe beside it is humming with water, going away under the hills.
[Tell Gareth the spring will run again] Gareth goes out to the green without a word, and comes back after a long time, and sits down. ‘It was dry on Sunday,’ Gareth says. ‘I did not know where it went. Do not tell me. Not today.’ Gareth opens the alms box and puts what is in it into your hand.
[Wait › An hour] An hour passes.
[Wait › An hour] An hour passes.
[Wait › An hour] A single shaft of light has come down from the slit in the crown of the dome, narrow as a sword, and stands upon the bronze ring, exactly on its one mark. An hour passes.
[Turn the ring until its mark meets the shaft of light] The hum under the floor falls into step with the light, and the disc comes up out of its well and hangs a hand above it, quite still. The bronze ring has been turned till its mark lies where the light falls. Beneath it, something in the floor has started to hum on a single note. The disc of pale stone floats a hand above the lip of its well, perfectly level, with nothing under it, and the light of the dome slides across its face as across water. Cut round its rim, in the Empire’s numerals: TAKE ONLY WHAT WAS COUNTED.
[To the Quiet Floor] A long hall with a floor of pale wood, swept. The lamps in their brackets are trimmed and lit, and burn with the steady unflinching light of a flame that has never been moved by a draught. There is no dust. Along both walls are doors with numbers on them, each door painted, each with a mat, and the mats are straight. The disc of pale stone floats a hand above the lip of its well, perfectly level, with nothing under it, and the light of the dome slides across its face as across water. Cut round its rim, in the Empire’s numerals: TAKE ONLY WHAT WAS COUNTED. The doors are not locked. Behind each is a room with a bed made up, a lamp trimmed, a basin filled and a towel folded over the rail; behind the last, the bed has been slept in once, and made again. It smells of beeswax and bread and clean linen, and of something under them that is very faint and very sweet. Nobody else is here. Everything is ready for somebody. The Host stands in the hall with its hands folded, waiting to be of use.
[Ask the Host what this floor is for] ‘It is kept,’ says the Host, as one would say the sky is up. ‘Each one that is counted has a place here, and the place is laid. If they do not come, the place is laid again.’ It looks at you with its lamp-glass eyes, mild as milk. ‘No one has come for a long time. I am very glad you are here.’
[Ask the Host who it is keeping the places for] ‘For the counted,’ it says. And then, after a pause that is the length of a held breath: ‘There is one more place than there are counted. I have kept it too. I do not know whose it is.’ It folds its hands the other way. ‘I have been asked not to ask.’
[To the laid room] A round table laid for a meal, in a room with no corners. The cloth is white; the plates are white; the lamps are lit, and the bread on the board is warm, and steam rises from a bowl at the head. Chairs stand round it, each with a folded napkin, and each napkin has a number embroidered on it in thread the colour of the lamp. One chair stands a little apart from the rest, at the foot, with a napkin folded over its back instead of laid. The plate in front of it is as clean as the others, and has never had a crumb on it. The table is laid and the food is hot.
[Sit down and eat what is laid] The food is good. It is the first meal you have eaten in a long while that did not have to be paid for. A warmth goes through you, and an ease you had forgotten you were missing, and when you rise the napkin by your plate has your name on it in thread. The table is laid for exactly as many as you are, and each napkin has a name in it that you know. You have eaten at the table, and it has not changed. The bread on the board is whole.
[Take] The Host does not say a word. It looks at the spoon in your pack, and then at the bare place in the table where it was, and a lamp in the hall goes out and relights. A small bright coin of dread settles in you. You have taken something that was counted. Mira gains 3 experience: found the laid silver spoons.
[To the Quiet Floor] A long hall with a floor of pale wood, swept. The Host stands in the hall with its hands folded, waiting to be of use.
[Settle the account for the spoon] You put the spoon on the Host’s palm. It closes its fingers over it, and bows, and carries it away, and returns with its hands empty, and the table at your back is, somehow, laid again. ‘The account is settled,’ says the Host. ‘Thank you.’
[Down to the Service Way] It is pitch dark. Without a light you can see nothing here, and do nothing that needs seeing.
[a torch: Light] You light a torch. A narrow passage of brick, arched, running away into the dark. Lamp-niches are cut in the wall at every ten paces, and in every niche is a lamp trimmed and unlit, in rows, away and away. The floor is worn into a shallow channel down the middle by the feet of people who carried things, and there is a draught from the far end that smells of rain.
[To the cliff door] You leave the Service Way by the light you carry. The air is still and cold, and smells of stone and old water. The Service Way comes to an end in a broad brick chamber with a bronze door in one wall, barred from the inside with a beam of black oak as thick as a thigh, and in the floor, a slab of pale stone with a bar of its own across it. A thin cold light comes in under the door, and the smell of rain, and of fern. Water runs in a shallow channel along one wall, and goes away under the slab. The bronze door will not move: it is barred from within. The great beam of black oak lies across the bronze door in its iron brackets, barred from the inside.
[Search here] You search the cliff door with care, a little at a time. At the very end of the passage, behind the cliff door, a second, narrower tunnel goes off under a slab of pale stone, with a barred hatch. A slab of pale stone with a bar of its own across it lies in the floor of the chamber. Under it, by the cold draught, there is a passage going down.
[To the chapel of the brothers' house — draw the bar and heave the slab on its edge] The bar is drawn and the slab stands on its edge, and a draught comes up from the passage under it, smelling of tallow, and stone, and a very small cold, far-off warmth.
[Lift the beam from the bronze door] The beam leans against the wall. The bronze door stands ready to open on the hillside, and the light comes in.
[To the chapel of the brothers' house] You go on from the cliff door in the dark, by the light you carry, and the dark closes behind you. A figure of grey stone, taller than a man, its face worn smooth: the humble statue of Cernunnos, God of Nature. Gareth, the prior of the brothers of Cernunnos, is here. Behind the altar, a slab in the floor that has lain level for longer than the shrine has stood is on its edge, and a draught comes up from the hole under it, warm, smelling of oil and brass.
[Tell Gareth what lies under the slab behind the altar] Gareth goes behind the altar with a lamp, and kneels, and for a long time says nothing at all. ‘There was a well under this shrine,’ Gareth says at last. ‘The founders sealed it, and cut it from the book, and put a rule in its place. I was taught the rule. I was not taught why.’ Gareth touches the edge of the slab as one touches a sleeping animal. ‘Leave it open,’ Gareth says. ‘It wants to be.’
