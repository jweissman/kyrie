## What the rounds say

- **Trust is the criterion that moved.** It stood at 2 through A1 to C3. In A1 and B2 most of its evidence was the
  instrument's: the runner offered rows the page did not draw (OR-1850). Its last part was the game's own: "easily won"
  under a DANGER mark beside "could bring her down" (OR-1851). With both mended, D4 and E5 read 3: "offers matched
  their results", "the dungeon dressing told me honestly what lay ahead".
- **Clarity rose from 7 to 8** with the runner mended, and held.
- **Wanting to go on is held by fights, not by the loop.** D4's 6 came from one slog: three banshees, woken by a lever
  whose row said nothing of them, resisting every weapon for sixteen rounds (OR-1857, OR-1858). Every round's reasons
  to return are concrete and come from play: a notebook's clue to the winze, an unread tally-board, a jar the
  paymaster kept.
- **The opening is consistently the strongest part** (beginning 4 in four rounds of five): a letter, a named person,
  the way given.
- **The friction left is wording and reach**: a way into the dungeon named as a way out (OR-1859), an errand split over
  two places (OR-1856), and the conversation's kept history read as repetition by every agent (a design question for the
  scrolling conversation, OR-149).

## On the anchors

No anchor disagreed badly with the transcripts. Two adjustments would make the rubric read truer, offered for the
owner's judgement and not applied:

- **Trust should be scored on the game's behaviour only.** A runner's failure is recorded as an instrument defect in
  the round, never as trust. A1–C3's 2s were partly the runner's.
- **Continuation should name the strongest pull and the strongest push.** D4's agent wanted to return to an inscription,
  coin and a fight, but put the slog first among its reasons to stop. The pair reads more than the single score.

The owner's own play is the test the rubric answers to. Where it disagrees with these rounds, the anchors are what
change.
