# Gosse's Company — the second pass

*A second reading of the finished adventure through a harder literary lens: what I was not happy with, what was done
about it, and what is still not right. The changes are in `night.kyr` (the performance), `supper.kyr` (the Bench's
supper), `history.kyr` (what the riot cost, and who remembers), `combat.kyr` (the dogs and the weir), with small
edits elsewhere. Spoilers throughout.*

## What I was not happy with

**The riot weighed nothing.** The whole adventure stood on a joke a young man made forty years ago, and the joke
had cost nothing but an Office. A comedy about censorship where the censor is simply wrong, and the joke simply
innocent, is a pamphlet. Ezra Orde founded the Office "within the month" because he was laughed at, and that was all.
Clemency kept it because she was his daughter, and that was all. Nobody had a reason that could not be dismissed in a
sentence, so nobody was a person.

**The night was a summary of a play.** [11-critique.md](11-critique.md) said the night was watched; it was also
*told*. Each scene said what kind of scene it was ("the Widow scene goes beautifully") and how the house took it. A
reader never heard a line of *The Alderman's Ghost* except the struck ones. The play we were asked to believe was the
best thing anyone had read was not on the page.

**The critics were a sum.** Clay, Gammage, Mallory and Callow had tastes, and each was shown in a speech; but a speech
is a position, and a position is a mouthpiece. Nobody was bored, nobody contradicted themselves, nobody was funny at
anyone's expense but their own. The verdict was arithmetic in a lords' room.

**Some of it was pulp exuberance.** The places where the prose was enjoying itself more than the reader:

- the bear in a bonnet, eating honey on stage, with Gammage "standing on her chair": joyful, and the right climax for a
  yard, but written three times in three registers as if it were the point of the play;
- the endings, one facet each, every loose end tied in a bow (the ostler marries the maid, the bell-ringers ring
  together, the bear is paid on time). A fairy-tale tidiness, delightful once, false by the eighth fate;
- "the yard is helpless", "the Wheel goes over like a ship", "the yard howls": the house's laughter as a refrain, used
  so often it stopped meaning anything;
- the Master's secret, which is real, told through clues that are all *charming*: a crayon drawing, ink on a finger,
  a servant's hood. Nothing in the mystery was costly to her.

**No act had a centre of gravity but the night.** Days one to four were a list of errands, each well made. There was
no midpoint, no reversal, nothing that made the week turn. The third night (the other company's) was a place to be.

**Nothing could hurt you.** By design there was no fight; but there was also no danger. The city at night was the
city by day with lamps.

## What was done

### The riot cost a life

On the second night of the singing a lamplighter, **Abel Tunstall the elder**, was up his ladder in the Chepe when the
crowd came up it singing the fool's song, and somebody shook the ladder for the joke, and he fell. Ezra Orde made the
Office the next week, for the widow's sake as much as his own. He wrote the Eighth Ordinance (*the words of the dead
may not be struck*) so that the dead man's own song could be cut on his memorial plate in the minster, and took the
boy into the Office. The boy is Tunstall, the chief clerk: the man who counts the notches.

The fact is found three ways:

- read off the small brass plate by the minster's west door, with the riot couplet cut on it;
- asked of Tunstall, once the plate has been read;
- told by Urania Orde at supper.

A lamplighter's boy in the Chepe at evening foreshadows it; so does a lamp cut very small below A. GOSSE on the
tiring-house wall. Told what the singing cost, Gosse's apology after the Prologue says so, and to whom it is owed.

This changes every character who touches it. Ezra was not merely vain: he was a vain man who was ashamed, and built
the shame into the City. Clemency's Office is a debt as well as an inheritance, and her play is the first thing in
forty years that laughs at her father without forgetting the lamplighter. Tunstall's rigour is grief. Gosse's boast
("I made an Office. How many players can say that?") curdles. And the comedy has its shadow: *it was funny, and a man
died of it, and both are true* is now a thing the company can say at supper.

### The play is on the stage

*The Alderman's Ghost* is now performed: verse and prose, entrances, business, stage directions in parentheses, the
yard's response in the narrator's voice. In outline:

- **the Prologue.** Couplets ("Your pardon, gentles, first; the play comes after"), with the dry, given from the
  wing, as a joke about the City's ear;
- **the funeral.** The Chamberlain and the Master of the Weights quarrel over the Book of Precedence over the coffin,
  in the rain (Tam's sieve of dried peas), until the lid moves. "GHOST: Then why am I lying down? LOB: They've put you
  down to argue. GHOST: In my day we argued standing up.";
- **the Widow.** Pomfret kneels in the pie, the will grants "the right to refuse anybody who kneels on the
  hearth-rug", and Dorcas's longest pause in the history of the Wheel ends in a single "No.";
- **the Council.** It votes to melt the Alderman's statue for the Fountain, and the Ghost rises through the trap to
  speak in the verse he believes is oratory and the City knows is Ezra ("I rise, sirs, as I always rise"). Licensed,
  he *stands*;
- **the lovers and the Tallyman.** Bell's moon couplets: "until she has the top—";
- **the Ghost's complaint.** Blank verse, played as tragedy by a tragedian who has finally been told it is comedy;
- **the Fountain and the pageant.** *The Concord of Arle—of the Town*, a pageant inside the play inside the Trial,
  with Concord, Plenty and Precedence in couplets, and the Mayor pulling the cord;
- **the Keeper.** Patience;
- **who walks first.** Lob's accidental truth ("We laughed at him... We loved him!"), the Ghost's "Then I'll go
  first", the procession, the marriages, the Epilogue ("So ends our town, sirs, which was never yours").

The live choices of the night are unchanged, and each now cuts into a scene the player has heard, not one described.
The applause is held back: the yard laughs where the text has earned it. The house's refrain is used only at the
turns.

### The critics argue, at supper

**The Bench's supper** (`supper.kyr`) follows Molière's *La Critique de l'École des femmes*:

- after a play, in a drawing-room, the people who saw it argue about it;
- the playwright is present in disguise;
- the hostess is called after a muse. Molière's Uranie becomes **Urania Orde**: the late Lord Mayor's widow, the
  Master's mother, and the model, it turns out, for the Widow Gudgeon.

The supper is at her house on Hill Street, on the night of Gosse's play. She keeps her husband's rule: nobody votes
until they have eaten, and nobody eats until they have said one true thing about the play.

It runs in eight courses, two watches each, about the length of an act of the play itself:

| Course | What is argued | What the company may say |
|---|---|---|
| I. The true things | "It was too long." "It was not long enough." "It was... very well acted." "It was an allegory." | its own true thing: everybody in it was laughing at somebody they loved |
| II. The soup | Callow's allegory; Clay's Aulus Fenne (one place, one day, no animal larger than a dog); Gammage's wet Thursday; Clemency, gravely agreeing with everyone, as a censor would | the rule of all rules is to please; or Gosse's note in the margin of Clay's *Dido*; or agree with Clay |
| III. The verb | Mallory laughing "at a hole" where the Office cut a word; Urania remembering Ezra's "I rise, my dear" | the Eighth Ordinance; that Swale did not know the lines were funny; or that it was filth |
| IV. The pie | Urania's pie, her hearth-rug, her proposal: whoever wrote it has eaten in this house | ask her about Ezra; name the author (Grave); or hold your tongue |
| V. The old days | Gammage's Trials: the Drowned Juliet, the bear-garden *Patient Daughter*; the Canon's surplices bought with fines | ask Gammage, ask Clay about his *Dido*, or serve the egg if you came in as a waiter |
| VI. The tally | Tunstall reads the notches; Callow's "muzzle with a motto"; Urania tells the lamplighter | both are true; the liberty of the Soke; the stone under the stage; why your stick has no notches |
| VII. What you would vote | each critic says, in character, what they would vote, if it were now | one appeal for the company, by persuasion, or the Epilogue given again |
| VIII. Somebody might | the candles at the last inch; "Somebody should make a comedy of this evening." "Nobody would license it." CLEMENCY: "Somebody might." | — |

The critics are not consistent, and they are not pleasant all the time:

- **Clay** cites a dead theorist he has made a career of, and is beaten by a margin note he wrote twenty years ago;
- **Gammage** is rude to Callow, fond of him, and eats with her fingers;
- **Mallory** is shocked by his own laughter, twice;
- **Callow** is bored, burns his mouth, says *allegory* until somebody stops him, and falls asleep on his notebook;
- **Clemency** says almost nothing from the window. What she does say is a censor's deadpan agreement, which is the
  funniest thing at the table and the saddest.

There are three ways in:

- the Master's invitation, if her trust was earned;
- Gammage's (carry her bag; "nobody ever looks at who carries a critic's bag"), if Swale was understood or Gammage
  won;
- Tunstall's (a tallyman carries the sticks).

The company can also come in by the kitchen, as a waiter, through Sukey or a lie at the area door. Anyone else is
turned away on the step.

What is said moves the critics' regard, so the supper is the one place where an argument, not a performance, can
change a vote: in the Book journey it brings Callow round, for four voices.

### The week has acts

| | Days | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| **I. Arrival** | the road, the first morning | the parcel; the play read; the company's faults found |
| **II. Labours** | days one and two | the Office, the Bench, the Soke, the small stories; the Office's verdict on the book |
| **III. The third night** | day three | the midpoint. While the other company plays, Gosse goes to the Wheel alone and rehearses on the bare stage to nobody; whoever follows learns he is afraid ("A player dies twice, you know. Once when he forgets the words, and once later, which matters less") and that he cannot hear the left side. The plate can be found the same day; told to Gosse, what it cost turns his apology. |
| **IV. The night** | day four, evening | the performance, in three acts |
| **V. The reckoning** | day four, late; day five | the supper; the verdict; the fates |

The arcs that move across them:

- **Gosse**, from boast to apology;
- **Clemency**, from licensing to authorship ("Somebody might");
- **Tunstall**, from the door to the table ("so does Tunstall, for the first time in twenty years");
- **Swale**, from innocence to understanding;
- **Hepzibah**, from the book to the bride;
- **Urania**, who has kept the house's laughter for forty years and gets to use it.

### Depth, and its illusion

Details that point past the story without explaining themselves:

- **The stone under the stage.** A worn Aenean step in the cellarage, older than the City and its Book: WHO SPEAKS
  HERE IN THE MASK IS NOT ANSWERED FOR. The Soke's liberty is a rule of the old stage. Gammage's mother saw the white
  stone seats when they dug the drain, "and the men wouldn't dig any further because of the faces cut in them". Clay's
  Aulus Fenne "filled the stone theatre that lies under the Wheel's yard... for forty years". Told at supper, it is the
  oldest argument at the table.
- **The wall of names**, by companies: the Children of the Close in round child's capitals; Doll Lacy with a wave for
  the year the Arle came into the yard; the Lord Warden's Men; Tobias Wake, who wrote *The Wolf King* and played the
  wolf. Below them, in a letter nobody reads, names older than the plaster.
- **Gammage's Trials:** the Drowned Juliet; the year the Wheel burned and the Children played *The Patient Daughter* in
  the bear-garden among the dogs, and an angel was bitten and went on.
- **The Office's fines** have bought the choir's surplices for forty years, seven crowns in ten.
- **Gosse's company before this one:** Hepzibah acted once, in a barn at Galt, as the Second Witch, and dried. Pudding
  was a clown before he was a man. Swale's mother is buried at Galt, where Pudding told a joke at her grave.
- **Small things seen early:** two clerks disputing whether anybody has heard the Master laugh; a waterman on the
  Stairs saying the bridge wardens have been at Fallow's twice; the lamplighter's boy in the Chepe; a cold fire on
  the weir stones.

The themes that now run through all of it:

- **Laughter and grief.** The Ghost needs somebody to say that they laughed at him because they loved him. The City
  needs somebody to say that the joke was funny and a man died of it.
- **Who is answered for what is said.** The stone, the Ordinances, the tally sticks, the struck lines, the apology.
- **Voices.** Kit's breaking; Gosse's deaf ear and the prompter's voice from the good side; the yard's voice; Urania's
  rule of true things; the Master's silence at the window; Callow's one word.
- **Precedence against love.** Who walks first, in the play, the Book and the procession, and the Ghost's "Then I'll
  go first".

### Danger, and the ways round it

**Bowle's mastiffs** (three bear-dogs: Grip, Tearwell and Old Madam) keep the bear-garden:

- by day they are in the kennels, unless Patience's pen is picked under Bowle's nose, when he draws the bolt;
- by night they run the ring while Bowle sleeps over them;
- they stand and wait for the word before they come.

The ways round them:

- pay Bowle;
- plead for Maud's sake;
- hold up fire, which they fear;
- hush them, if you know dogs (woodcraft);
- snatch Patience and run, in the moment they wait.

Beaten, you come round in Soke Lane bitten in three places, with the bear back in her pen and a new padlock on it.

**The weir-rats** keep the weir stones from eight at night until the small hours. They are Long Nan Scullard's people,
Gullet folk gone to the bad, who live under the drowned Bishop's Mill. Her man was a waterman, drowned under the
Stairs, and the Wardens would not bury him without the second penny. The weir is now two walks with the stones between.

Nan asks two crowns. The ways round her:

- pay;
- talk her round;
- face her down;
- name the Gullet (Ma Tolly will tell you to, and Garlick's directions count);
- cross before dark;
- take the boats.

Beaten, you are thrown in the Arle and fished out at the Soke landing, lighter in the purse. The night the Gullet
walks the weir on Gosse's night, the rats are scattered and not seen again: the crowd you led is the one thing she
cannot stand against.

## What is still not right

- **Fallow is still mostly offstage.** The second pass gave the watermen's grievance a face (Nan) and the bridge
  wardens a rumour, but not Fallow a scene that matters. He should be at the supper's door, or in the yard on the
  night, losing.
- **Hepzibah is still the exposition** for the company's own troubles. Urania now carries the Ordes' history, which
  helps; Hepzibah still tells the player most of what is wrong in the loft.
- **The night is still mostly watched.** It is now worth watching, which is not the same thing. The supper is more
  interactive than any scene of the play.
- **The supper is reached late.** It is the adventure's best talk and only a company that earned an invitation sees
  it. That is right for the design and a pity for the reader.
- **Combat is staged in the tests.** No journey happens to walk into the dogs or the rats; the tests set the scene
  directly. The wandering test does not reach them.
- **The fates are still tidy.** Fewer would be better; I left them because each records a small story's outcome.

## If I had to 10x the ambition

I would start with **the other two companies**. Gosse's Company is the only one that is people; the Lord Mayor's
Servants and the Children of the Close are a night each and a rival. Make all three full troupes with books, faults and weeks of their
own, playing the same Trial at the same time. Their troubles would be woven into Gosse's: the same bailiff, the same
watermen, Dorcas's place in Fallow's wardrobe. The player would join any of them, and the Bench would
judge three performances, each rendered in full. Then the adventure is not a quest but a *season*: the city keeps
going whichever company you are with, and *The Alderman's Ghost* is only the best of three plays you might help stage.

From there:

- **The repertory as a system.** A play is a set of scenes with parts, each part with lines and business; casting,
  rehearsal and cutting act on scenes; the performance is assembled from what the week made. Write the Kyr for a play
  once, and every company's night is generated from its book and its people, not hand-wired scene by scene.
- **Pentrice's history as an archive.** Forty years of Trials, each with its companies, verdicts and the scandal it
  is remembered for: the Drowned Juliet, the burned Wheel, the riot. It would be found in the Owl's back numbers, the
  Office's ledgers, the wall of names and Gammage's mouth, so that every name has a story behind it if you dig.
- **The Office from inside.** Play a season as a tallyman, ruling lines in the corn exchange, and find out what
  Tunstall knows.
- **The road.** Gosse's Company came from somewhere and goes somewhere. A sequence of towns, each with its own
  licence, patron and audience, of which Pentrice is the hardest, would make the Patent the end of a long road and not
  a weekend.

## Questions

1. **How much of the play should the player see?** The performance now runs to about half an hour's reading. Is that
   the right length for the page, or would you rather each scene were shorter with more live choices between?
2. **Should the supper be reachable by everyone?** As built, a company must earn its way in, or lie its way into an
   apron. A company that sees nothing of it misses the adventure's best argument; a company that walks in for free
   cheapens it.
3. **The lamplighter makes the comedy darker.** I think it is the depth the story lacked; it is also a death in a
   comedy. Is that the tone you want for the Trial?
4. **The dogs and the rats are the only danger.** Would you like the night itself to carry a risk (the Office's men
   with cudgels; a riot of the yard if the house turns), or should Gosse's Company stay a comedy where only the side
   streets bite?
