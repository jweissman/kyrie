# Gosse's Company — an honest critique

*Written after the journeys, by the author, against the work as it plays rather than as it was meant. The
commission asked for honesty; this is where it is kept.*

## What works

**The joke that is also a grief.** The adventure's best idea survived every stage: the funniest lines in the play are
given to the one man in the company who does not know they are funny, and the night turns on what someone in the wing
says to him when the house roars. "They love you. Go on." It is the one moment where the player's preparation (they
chose not to tell him) and the player's choice in the moment (they choose what to tell him now) meet, and it is the
moment the transcripts are best at. The complaint that follows, played as tragedy with the clown's hand in his, is
earned, because the letter that reconciled them was found three days earlier in the prompter's book.

**A city that is a joke about one idea.** Pentrice licenses speech, and everything in it is licensed: the market's
little painted boards, the crier with a licence pinned to his hat, SILENCE IS LICENSED HERE on the Close's lawn, a
Fountain of Concord that has never run because its water goes to the Lord Mayor's carp. The jokes are structural, so
they keep landing as the company walks about, and the river is a real joke too: the City's law stops at the water, so
the bailiff, the Office and the costumes all have to deal with a ferry.

**The coalition.** "Three of four" gives the Book and the Liberty real teeth. Neither is the right answer; each
spends something (the play's satire, or twenty-one crowns and the Canon), and each needs a third critic won by
something done in the week rather than on the night. That the soldier's whole-play route needed Clay's old
manuscript, found in a costume rack and given back, is the kind of connection the commission asked for, and it was
found by playing, not designed.

**Small stories that stay small.** The ringers and the jackdaws, the ostler's proposal, the bees: each is three or
four steps, each has a person and a want, and each pays off in a sentence on the last morning. They are joined by
honey and by the river, not by plot.

## What does not, yet

**The night is mostly watched.** The performance is the commission's centrepiece and the adventure's biggest
structural risk, and the risk is only half answered. In the Book journey the night is sixteen "Wait › A while" steps
and four live choices. The scenes read the week well, and a player who prepared well is rewarded by reading the
consequences; but between choices the page is a stream of beautifully written paragraphs that the player advances
by waiting, and three scenes' tellings arrive together in one step. A player who prepared little has almost nothing
to do on the night but watch it go wrong. The play needed a few more things to do at each post (cues to give, a
property to hand on, a light to shutter), and a presentation that holds a scene apart from the next.

**The mystery was solved for you, and is now only easy.** As first built, five pairs of clues, any two of which were
enough, and then the engine announced the answer ("It comes together all at once, the way a joke does"); in the Book
journey it dawned while Tunstall was reading out the strikes, before the player had gone looking. This critique was
acted on: now the page says only that the company has seen enough, and the company has to say who it was, to
Hepzibah, and may name Callow or Fallow and be told why not. It is still easy. The clues are good (the watermark, the
hand, the child's crayon drawing over the Master's mantel) and found by kindness rather than by searching, which is
right for a comedy; but two of five is a low bar, and a wrong guess costs nothing.

**Hepzibah is the exposition.** She tells the player the cast, the quarrel, the letter, the deafness, the bailiff,
Fallow's offer and the weir's existence is somebody else's; she lends the letter, marks the cues, keeps the book.
She is the character the adventure loves most and uses most as a quest-giver, and the second fact damages the first.
The proposal at the end lands anyway, because her lines are good; it would land harder if she asked for less.

**Fallow is mostly offstage.** The antagonist is a timetable. Corin Fallow visits the loft once, appears once in the
tiring-house to thank or not, can be talked to at the Mayor's Room about the Trial and Gosse, and otherwise acts by
consequence: Dorcas gone, the heckler hired, the quarto informed on, the watermen paid. One more conversation was
added after this critique was first written ("Ask Fallow what he thinks he is doing": "Winning… A heckler is a cost of
business; so is a boatman; so is a wardrobe-mistress"), and it helps; but he is still a man the player hears about
more than meets.

**The critics are a number the player cannot see until the end.** Each critic's taste is shown (at the Siege, at the
Daughter, in their own words), which is good; but whether the week and the night have won them is a sum. This
critique was acted on too: after the play each critic's face in the lords' room shows which way they will vote (Clay
sitting on, or his chair pushed in; Callow's programme torn through, or one word underlined twice). The sum itself is
still invisible during the week, and Gammage, who likes joy, was won by almost any night with the bear in it until
her margin was raised. The thresholds were tuned against journeys, which makes them right for those journeys and not
necessarily for the player in front of them.

**The stranger's road is thin.** Signing on as a tallyman is the most interesting moral position in the adventure (a
critic paid by the censor, sitting with a knife in front of the stage) and the least consequential: a tallyman who is
not also one of the company can let an angel off, lay a stick down and answer for a book, and cannot otherwise help a
company that is falling apart in front of them. The journey ends, correctly, in the Company Broken. It should be
possible to be both, and the adventure allows it, but nothing on the bench rewards having been both.

**A question the city nearly never asked.** Hepzibah says no woman has played on a licensed stage in Pentrice for
forty years, which is why Fallow's boys play his girls; Dorcas plays the Widow at the Wheel on both routes. Tunstall
now answers it when he strikes the book ("the Wheel being in the Soke: the charter reaches the words, not the
mouths"), which is consistent and a little convenient.

## How it reads

The journeys run to about 25,000 words for a thorough week, of which perhaps 12,000 are the adventure's own prose
and the rest are the engine's re-telling of places on each return. The first day is dense (the parcel, the cast, the
bear, the bees, Fallow), the second is the best (the bailiff, the gulling, the Siege), and the third sags: the company
mostly waits for evening, and the timetable's events happen to it rather than with it. A player who sleeps through
the third afternoon misses little, which is a fault.

Repetition is the engine's and the author's both. The engine re-tells a place in full on every entry (the Chepe's
crier cries the same three companies every time the company crosses the market), reports milestones on the step
after they are reached (the bees' experience arrives with the hampers), and tells a scene's end and the next scene's
opening in one breath. The author's are the forty years (the riot, the Office, the first time the Wheel has laughed
like this, the first time anyone has heard the Master laugh): the number is the adventure's spine and it is used
until it creaks.

The season was lost in revision, for a good reason: a generated world can be in winter, and "the Midsummer Trial"
in a market-place full of thaw was false. "The Trial of the Wheel" is accurate and flatter. A country with its own
calendar would let the old name back.

## Whether it meets the commission

It is a substantial metropolitan region with twenty-five small stories, a mystery, a satire, two romances and a
public performance at its centre; it is placed in generated worlds and met in their towns; it is entered from a
generated town, a standalone village, the road, and the Office's bench; its two principal approaches both work and
differ; all eight endings are reached by journeys; and a low-charisma soldier can win it. As literature it is uneven:
its best scenes (the wing, the complaint, the Master's laugh, the empty wing) are as good as anything the author has
written for Kyrie, and its middle is a timetable with good jokes in it. As an experience it rewards a player who reads,
and asks a player who wants to act to wait more than they should.
