# Gosse's Company — first treatment

*Stage 1 of the commission: the work as it wants to be, before the engine is consulted. Written 26 October 2026.
Spoilers throughout. The critique that follows it is [02-critique.md](02-critique.md); the design that survived is
[03-revised-design.md](03-revised-design.md).*

## The kernel

A company of travelling players, old, in debt and very good, comes to the city of **Pentrice** for the **Midsummer
Trial**, the week in which three companies play three nights before the city and a bench of four critics, and the
winner is given the **Patent**: a licensed house of its own, and the right to stop walking. Absalom Gosse has played
kings on village greens for forty-one years. This is the last summer he can stand on the road, and he knows it, and
says the opposite in a voice that carries to the back of any field.

They have no new play. On the first night in the city one arrives: a fair copy of a comedy called **The Alderman's
Ghost**, wrapped in oilcloth, left with the ostler of the Painted Moon with a purse of twenty crowns and no name. It
is the best thing any of them has ever read. It is also, thinly, a portrait of the late Lord Mayor Ezra Aske, who
founded the **Office of Plain Speech**, without whose licence no word may be spoken on a stage in the City; and the
Office is now kept by his daughter.

Who wrote it, and why did they send it to the one company that would be ruined for playing it?

## Why this, and why here

The collection has comedy only as texture: a goose, a queue, a cake. It has no *making*, no *performing*, no romance,
no ensemble who relate to each other while the player watches, and no adventure that inhabits the generated world
rather than sitting beyond its march. Gosse's Company is meant to be all five at once. Its comedy should be
structural: a misunderstanding that compounds, a trap that catches the trapper, a reversal funnier than what it
reverses, a feast (a first night) at the end. Its melancholy is the theatre's own: everything made in it is gone by
morning.

## Pentrice: the region

Pentrice stands where the Ise falls into the slow brown Arle, a day's walk west of the first country's march. It is a
cathedral city that became a market city that became a city of guilds, licences and precedence, and it is
proud of all three in the wrong order. Every procession has an order (the **Book of Precedence** runs to four hundred
pages and is read aloud, in part, before each one); every trade has a charter; every public word has a licence.

What Pentrice is *for* in this design is the satire: a society made visible by exaggerating its law. The Office of
Plain Speech was founded forty years ago, after a riot in a playhouse, to see that nothing seditious, blasphemous or
indecent was said in public. It has grown, as offices do. Every play performed within the walls is licensed line by
line; every unlicensed word spoken on a licensed stage is fined at a penny a word, counted by **tallymen** who sit in
the front row with notched sticks. The city's comedians have become the finest improvisers in Imbar, because the
fines are a tax on improvisation, and the audience pays to see who will risk them.

Across the river is **the Soke**, a liberty the City has never governed: bear-garden, stews, watermen, the oldest
playhouse in the country, and no Office at all. Everything the City forbids is half a crown and a boat away.

### Districts

| District | What it is | Who you meet there |
|---|---|---|

| **The Chepe and Guildhall Square** | The market cross, the Guildhall, the dry **Fountain of Concord** (paid for three times, never run), the Office of Plain Speech | The Censor; her clerks; the Lord Mayor and the aldermen; a water-engineer's apprentice |
| **The Close** | The minster, the choir school, the canons' houses, quiet lawns and loud bells | Canon Mallory, precentor and critic; the **Children of the Close**, a boy company |
| **Paternoster Row** | Booksellers, printers, binders, the coffee-house at **the Sign of the Owl** where the critics sit | Ormsby Clay; Nym Callow; a printer who pirates plays |
| **The Hill** | Tall houses with gardens down to the Ise | Lady Aske's house; the Lord Mayor's garden, which has a fountain that runs |
| **The Shambles and the Water Stairs** | The fish market, the watermen's stairs, the river traffic to the Soke | Dame Philippa Ruddle, ballad-sheet critic; Oswin Garlick the heckler; the watermen's guild |
| **The Soke** | Over the water: the **Wheel** (the round playhouse where the Trial is played), the bear-garden, the stews, the Mint | Corin Lisle's rival company keeps its house in the City; the bear-ward of the Soke wants Gosse's bear |

The Trial is played in the **Wheel**, the old round house in the Soke, because the City allowed it there two hundred
years ago and nobody has dared to read the Book of Precedence to find out whether it may move. The Office has, by a
charter of its own, sat in the Wheel's front row during Trial week ever since.

### The city before the company came

Pentrice has a life of its own whether or not anyone stages anything:

- the **Fountain of Concord** in Guildhall Square has been dry since it was built; the water that should feed it runs
  to the Lord Mayor's garden on the Hill, and an engineer's apprentice can prove it;
- the **watermen** are in dispute with the Bridge Wardens over the fare to the Soke and will strike on the night of
  their choosing;
- the **Children of the Close** have a choirboy who wants to run away and act in a real company;
- **Oswin Garlick**, fishmonger, has heckled every first night for twenty years, and companies pay him to stay home;
- a **printer** on Paternoster Row pirates plays from the stage, by a boy who writes shorthand in the pit;
- **Gosse's creditors** are in three countries, and two of them are in Pentrice.

## The company

The ensemble is the heart of the work, and must be people who relate to *each other* while the player is in the
room.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Absalom Gosse** | Actor-manager, sixty-three. Plays kings, owes everyone, lies magnificently and lovingly. Is going deaf, and has hidden it from everyone but his prompter, who has hidden that she knows. Wants the Patent so the company can stop walking; is afraid of it, because in a house of their own he would be seen getting old every night. |
| **Hepzibah Lusk** | Prompter, book-keeper, wardrobe, accounts and conscience, fifty-nine. Has prompted Gosse for thirty-four years. Keeps the book, the money (such as it is) and a bundle of letters in the property chest that Gosse wrote to her and never sent, which she found eleven years ago and has never mentioned. |
| **Ambrose Vane** | The tragedian. Fifty. Played Lear at twenty-eight before the Margrave of Sallow and has been explaining it since. Will not "work blue": no bawdy, no double meanings, no breeches, nothing he would not say before his mother, who is dead. He is cast as the Ghost, and the Ghost's lines are the bluest in the play, and Vane does not understand a single one of them. |
| **Dorcas Quaile** | Leading lady, forty-eight. Has played the girl for twenty-five years and knows to the day how old she is. The new play has the girl (Bell) and the Widow; the Widow is the best part in it. Lisle has offered her a place in his house. |
| **Jem Pudding** | The clown. Cannot work clean. Improvises in rhyme, falls downstairs for a living, and is the only one in the company Vane will not speak to. They have done a double act for nine years without exchanging a word offstage. |
| **Kit Arden** | Fifteen. Plays the girls. His voice is going this summer, and with it, he thinks, his place. |
| **Duchess** | A bear, old, toothless, gentle, fond of honey and of Pudding. Appears in the third act. Is the only member of the company paid on time. |
| **Tam and Ysolde Fenn** | Husband and wife, hired men for small parts, music and carpentry. Argue in harmony. |

### The rivals

- **Corin Lisle**, manager of **the Lord Mayor's Servants**, holds the Patent and the City's licensed house, the
  **Mayor's Room**. Elegant, sensible, and a better businessman than artist. Lisle once loved Clemency Aske.
- **The Children of the Close**, the minster's boy company, run by Canon Mallory, who is also on the Bench.

### The Bench

The Patent is given by four critics, each named to the Bench by a different authority, each with an honest and
distinct idea of what a play is for. Winning needs three of them.

| Critic | Appointed by | What a play is for | What they hate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ormsby Clay** | the Guildhall | Order: verse, the unities, the proper end of each person | Clowns, ad-libbing, bears |
| **Dame Philippa Ruddle** | the Shambles (she writes the ballad-sheets the fish market reads) | Joy: laughter, spectacle, a good cry and home | Pomp, long speeches, Ambrose Vane (whom she has mocked in print for fifteen years) |
| **Canon Mallory** | the Close | Improvement: a moral, and nobody worse for it | Indecency, satire of the dead |
| **Nym Callow** | the Owl (the coffee-house elects its critic) | Truth: a play should bite the hand of power | Safety, flattery, anything the Office would license |

Their disagreements are the strategy of the last act. A cut that pleases Mallory loses Callow; the bear wins Ruddle
and loses Clay; the satire wins Callow and Ruddle and sends the tallymen's sticks clicking.

### The Office

**Lady Clemency Aske**, Master of Plain Speech, forty-four, daughter of its founder. Precise, witty, alone, and the
best writer in Pentrice, which nobody knows. She wrote *The Alderman's Ghost* after her father died, as a comedy about
the absurd, pompous, loving man he was, and put it in a drawer, because the Office could never license it. Corin
Lisle found it on her desk the week he lost her (an old story, told differently by each), kept it, and has now sent it
to Gosse: if Gosse plays it unlicensed, the Office must close him; if Gosse sends it to the Office for licence,
Clemency must license her own satire of her father or ban the best thing she ever wrote. Either way Lisle's Patent is
safe and Clemency is made to choose, which is what he has wanted for six years.

## The play within: *The Alderman's Ghost, or, Who Walks First*

A comedy in three acts. It must be substantial: a thing the company makes, that can be made better or worse, cut,
stretched, cast well or badly, and that goes differently every time it is played.

**The parts.** Alderman Gaunt's Ghost (Vane); the Widow Gaunt (Dorcas, or Kit); Bell, her daughter (Kit, or Dorcas,
or the choirboy); Hodge, the Ghost's servant, who alone can see him (Pudding); Sir Rowley Tench, the heir, a fop
(Gosse, who wants to play the Ghost); Master Plumb, the Fountain-builder's apprentice, Bell's lover (Tam Fenn, or the
protagonist); the Tallyman, a clerk of the Office who counts words (Ysolde Fenn, in a false beard); the Prologue
(Gosse); the Bear (Duchess); the Constable, mourners, aldermen (whoever is left, the protagonist among them).

**Act I. The Funeral.**
1. *Prologue.* Gosse asks the audience's pardon in advance for everything.
2. *The procession.* The mourners quarrel over precedence until the coffin is left in the street. Hodge sees the
   Ghost climb out of it.
3. *The Widow receives.* Sir Rowley Tench proposes to the Widow over the funeral meats. The Ghost, invisible to all
   but Hodge, objects in blank verse.

**Act II. The Council.**
4. *The Council sits.* The aldermen vote to sell Gaunt's statue to pay for the Fountain of Concord. The Ghost rises
   to speak against it, and the double meanings begin ("I rise, sirs, as I always rose: unbidden, and too soon").
5. *The lovers.* Bell and Plumb in the garden; the Tallyman counts their kisses as unlicensed words.
6. *The Ghost's complaint.* A long speech, written to be played as tragedy, which is either the funniest or the most
   moving thing in the play depending entirely on who plays it and how.

**Act III. The Unveiling.**
7. *The Fountain.* The Fountain is unveiled and does not run.
8. *The Bear.* The statue is sold to the Soke's bear-garden; the bear arrives to collect it.
9. *Who walks first.* The Ghost may rest only when someone in the Council tells the truth; nobody can; Hodge does, by
   accident; the Widow chooses; the lovers marry; the Fountain runs (or doesn't); the procession re-forms, and the
   Ghost walks first.

**Things that happen in it.** Each scene runs in beats. A beat is a line, a business, an entrance. Things go wrong in
it: an actor dries, a prop is not there, a trap sticks, a heckler, rain on the open yard, the bear will not come on,
the bear will not go off, a tallyman stands up. The player (as whatever they are) prompts, improvises, cuts, stretches,
cues, covers, fights a stage fight for real, or does nothing. Each beat moves the house's temper and each critic's
regard.

## How the protagonist comes in

Gosse's Company is the first adventure written to perform in the generated world's own towns.

1. **On the road.** In a town of the first country the company is playing one night on the green on its way to
   Pentrice. The player sees them: a cart with a painted cloth, a short interlude (*The Shepherd's Revenge*), a clown,
   a bear, a hat going round, a heckler. Something goes wrong (the man who carries the bear's chain has run off with
   the takings). The player can help, or not. Gosse, who hires everyone, hires them: "A season's work, a share of the
   house, and the honour of the profession, which is worth precisely nothing and which I would not sell for a crown."
   They go on to Pentrice with the company, as one of it.
2. **By report.** A player who never met them hears in a taproom of the Midsummer Trial in Pentrice, west over the
   march; at the Westgate the bills of the Trial are pasted over each other, and the ostler has a parcel nobody has
   called for. They come to the city a stranger, and may be hired by anyone: by Gosse, by Lisle, by the Office as a
   tallyman, by a critic as their legman.

The two entries are not cosmetic. A member of the company can be cast, rehearses, is on stage on the night, and is
inside every quarrel. A stranger works the city: the Office, the critics, Lisle's offers, the watermen. Both can reach
every ending; they reach them by different doors.

## The small adventures

Each is meant to stand alone with a person, a place or thing, an uncertainty, more than one approach, a complication,
a resolution and a consequence for later.

1. **The Players on the Green** (a generated town). The bear's chain-man has run off with the takings. Find him on the
   road, talk the hat round twice, or lead the bear yourself. Gosse hires you; the chain-man, caught, may be forgiven
   (and joins the crowd at the Trial) or not.
2. **The Parcel at the Painted Moon.** The ostler will not give the parcel to anyone but "the man it was meant for",
   and will not say who that is. Gosse claims it; Lisle's servant has asked after it; the ostler was paid by a lady in
   a hood.
3. **Who Plays the Widow.** Dorcas wants Bell; Bell is nineteen. Persuade her that the Widow is the part (show her the
   Widow's speeches; get Ruddle's old notice of her as a comic actress; let Gosse lie to her; let her play Bell and
   watch the critics). Lisle's offer is the complication.
4. **The Ghost's Lines.** Vane will not speak a blue line. But he does not hear them as blue. Tell him, and he
   demands cuts (rewrite them with Hepzibah; or let him leave). Don't tell him, and he plays them in all innocence,
   and the house roars, and on the night he must decide what the roar means.
5. **Pudding and Vane.** Nine years without a word offstage, over a joke Pudding made at Vane's mother's funeral. Find
   out what the joke was. Get them to rehearse the Ghost-and-Hodge scenes together.
6. **Kit's Voice.** Kit's voice cracks in rehearsal. Honey and horehound from the herb-wife in the Shambles; or the
   choirmaster's remedy; or recast Bell and give Kit Master Plumb, his first man's part.
7. **The Choirboy.** Ned Cherry of the Children of the Close wants to join Gosse. Help him run, return him, or ask
   Canon Mallory to lend him. Consequences for Mallory's vote.
8. **The Pirate Quarto.** The fair copy is the only one. The printer's boy is taking it down in shorthand at
   rehearsal. Catch him; buy the quarto; let it print (and the Office reads it before you send it in).
9. **The Licence.** Submit the book to the Office. The clerks strike lines; the Censor reads it herself, and goes very
   still. Bribe the clerk, argue the lines, forge the seal, submit a decoy, or don't submit at all and play in the Soke
   under the liberty's law.
10. **The Hand in the Margin** (the mystery). The paper bears the Office's watermark; the corrections are in the hand
    of the Office's licences; the purse was sewn by a lady's maid on the Hill; Lisle's servant asked after it; Callow
    says he wrote it. Four suspects, one author, and a sender who is not the author.
11. **Gosse's Debts.** The bailiffs come for the costumes on the fourth day. Pay, hide the costumes in the minster
    (sanctuary for goods, by the Book of Precedence), play a scene for the bailiffs, or fight.
12. **The Bear in the Shambles.** Duchess gets loose and goes to the honey-stall. The bear-ward of the Soke wants to
    buy her for baiting; the fishwives want her shot; Pudding wants her home.
13. **The Heckler.** Oswin Garlick will be at the Wheel on your night. Pay him off; hire him as a plant; outheckle him;
    make him laugh. He has a daughter who wants to be an actress.
14. **The Fountain of Concord.** The apprentice engineer knows the water goes to the Lord Mayor's garden. Prove it,
    unstop the conduit, and the real Fountain runs on the day the play says it will.
15. **The Watermen.** Lisle has paid the watermen to strike on your night so that nobody can cross to the Soke. Settle
    their quarrel, find another way across (the old ferry, the weir path at low water), or bring the audience over
    yourself.
16. **The Critics' Table.** Four critics at the Owl, each with a private story: Clay's tragedy hissed off the Wheel
    twenty years ago by Gosse's company; Ruddle's fifteen years of mocking Vane, whom she loves; Mallory's taste for
    bawdy ballads; Callow's debts and his claim to be the author.
17. **The Letters in the Chest.** Gosse cannot hear the prompts any more. Hepzibah needs someone in the opposite wing
    to sign them. In the property chest, under the crowns, are the letters he never sent. Gosse means to propose to her
    in the epilogue. He has asked you to make sure she is listening.

## The larger conflict and the powers

- **Corin Lisle** wants the Patent kept and Clemency made to choose. He offers Dorcas a place (day 2), informs the
  Office that Gosse has an unlicensed satire (day 3), pays Garlick to heckle (day 4), pays the watermen to strike (day
  6). Each is an ordinary act with a time and a cost, and each can be got ahead of.
- **The Office** licenses, strikes, fines and closes. Its clerks act by the book; its Master, by the end, may not.
- **The creditors** come on day 4.
- **The Bench** watches the other companies' nights too: the Lord Mayor's Servants on day 5 (a handsome, dull
  *Siege of Arle* that everyone has seen), the Children of the Close on day 6 (*The Patient Daughter*, with angels).
  Gosse plays last, on day 7. The verdict is read on the morning of day 8.

## Arcs

- **Gosse**: from the lie (we are not tired, we want nothing, we will play forever) to one true speech, in the
  epilogue, with the whole city listening, which is either to Hepzibah or to the audience or to nobody.
- **Vane**: from dignity as armour to dignity as a gift: he finds that playing a fool's lines with his whole heart is
  what makes the house cry, or he never finds out and leaves the profession, or he walks out.
- **Clemency Aske**: from the drawer to the stage. She can bow, burn it, license it, or sit in the front row and
  count the words.
- **Dorcas**: from the girl to the Widow, or to Lisle's house.
- **Kit**: from girls to men.

## How it can end

A season's account rather than a menu. The ending is the combination of several independent outcomes:

| Outcome | Possibilities |
|---|---|
| **The Patent** | Won by Gosse (three of the Bench), kept by Lisle, given to the Children |
| **The play** | Played licensed and cut; played whole in the Soke and fined into ruin; played whole and licensed by its author; burned; never played |
| **The author** | Unknown; credited to Callow; revealed by the player; revealed by herself, from the stage |
| **The company** | Housed at the Wheel; on the road again, famous; broken (Dorcas to Lisle, Vane gone, Gosse in the debtors' prison) |
| **Gosse and Hepzibah** | Married; still not; she leaves |
| **Vane and Ruddle** | A late understanding; a feud renewed |
| **Kit** | Plays his first man's part, or leaves the stage |
| **Duchess** | Home with Pudding; sold to the bear-garden |

Named endings stitch them: **The Patent House**, **The Liberty** (the play whole, the company banished from the City
and famous in every town), **The Author's Bow**, **The Fair Copy Burned**, **The Company Broken**.

## Afterwards, in the generated world

If the company goes on the road again, it plays *The Alderman's Ghost* in the towns of the first country, and the
towns talk about it: the generated world's own towns are its audiences.

## Rewards

- **A share in the company**: lodging at the Wheel whenever in Pentrice, a share of each house, a place to come back to.
- **Gosse's crown of paste**, which he gives to whoever plays the Ghost on the night if it is not Vane.
- **A trade**: what the player learned (acting, prompting, writing).
- **The Censor's licence**, valid for life, for one play of the player's own.
- Experience for what was done: casting settled, the critics read, the play made.

## Registers

Comedy; joy; a little grief (Gosse's deafness, the dead father the play was written about); desire (Vane and Ruddle,
Gosse and Hepzibah); the melancholy of a first night, which is that it happens once.
