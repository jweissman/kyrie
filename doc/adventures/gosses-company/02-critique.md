# Gosse's Company — the editor's critique of the first treatment

*Stage 2. Read against [01-treatment.md](01-treatment.md) as if someone else had written it, by the questions the
commission asks and the atlas's §30. Each finding ends in what the revision does about it
([03-revised-design.md](03-revised-design.md)).*

## 1. Is the city alive apart from the play?

**No, not yet.** Every one of the "life of its own" items in the treatment turns out, two paragraphs later, to be a
lever for the Trial: the Fountain pays off in Act III, the watermen are Lisle's instrument, the choirboy is Mallory's
vote, the heckler is a beat, the printer is a threat to the fair copy. That is economical plotting and a dead city.
A player who wanders Pentrice for an afternoon with no interest in theatre should still find people with business of
their own that has nothing to do with Gosse.

*Revision:* three purely local stories with no lever on the Trial at all (§ "Kept small" in the revised design): the
ostler's banns, the bell-ringers' quarrel at the minster, the honey-woman's lodger. And the Fountain is cut loose from
the play's ending, so that unstopping it is something done for the Chepe, not for Act III; it is allowed to coincide,
not required to.

## 2. Does the plot grow from the setting?

**Half of it.** The Office of Plain Speech, the tallymen and the Liberty grow from Pentrice's one idea (a city that
licenses words). The mystery does not. *Lisle found the play on her desk and sent it to trap them both* is a plot
device with a love story painted on: it needs a six-year-old romance nobody can show, a motive nobody would guess, and
a villain whose scheme only works if three other people behave exactly as he predicts.

*Revision:* the Office and the company share a history that makes the mystery inevitable. **The riot that founded
the Office of Plain Speech, forty years ago, was at the Wheel, at a performance by Absalom Gosse's company.** Gosse,
twenty-three, playing a fool, said something about Lord Mayor Ezra Aske that the pit took up and carried over the
river. The Mayor, who was in the house with his four-year-old daughter, founded the Office within the month, and
Gosse has not played inside the walls since. The Trial in the Soke is the first time he has been back. Clemency Aske
was the four-year-old. She has been sending herself to the theatre in disguise ever since; she wrote *The Alderman's
Ghost* about her father after he died; and **she sent it to Gosse herself**, unsigned, because he is the one man in
Imbar who would play it as it was written, and because it is his last summer and nothing can be done to him that the
road has not done already. The trap is no one's: it is the Office's own law, which her father made and she keeps,
catching the best thing she ever wrote. That is the satire, and the comedy, and it needs no villain.

Lisle stays, smaller and better: a sensible man who honestly believes Gosse's kind of theatre is over, offers Gosse a
pension to retire and Dorcas a house to play in, and denounces the unlicensed play to the Office because that is what
the law is for. He is not wrong about everything. Taking his pension is a real ending.

## 3. Is the comedy a game, or a long passage of dialogue?

**Mostly dialogue.** The treatment's jokes are in what people say: Gosse's hiring speech, the Ghost's blue lines, the
twins arguing in harmony. Comedy as *structure* needs:

- a **plant and payoff** a player causes (something set up in rehearsal that the player can make pay off on the
  night, or ruin);
- a **compounding misunderstanding** the player can feed or stop;
- a **trap that catches the trapper** the player can spring;
- a **reversal** that makes things worse and funnier.

*Revision:* each is a mechanism, not a speech.

- *Plant and payoff*: Vane's innocence. Whether anyone tells him what his lines mean is a choice with a lasting fact
  (`vaneKnows`); the night's Act II reads it. Rehearsal shows the company trying not to laugh (and the player can
  laugh: a choice that hurts him, and teaches him something on the night).
- *Compounding misunderstanding*: the gulling of Vane and Ruddle (see 8). Each forged note makes the next more
  necessary and the eventual meeting more catastrophic.
- *Trap that catches the trapper*: the Office strikes lines from its own Master's play; the player can carry the
  struck book back and forth between her and her own clerk until she countermands herself.
- *Reversal*: the tallymen in the front row are in the play (the Tallyman is a character). The player can arrange
  for the real tallymen's counting to become part of the show, so the fines are the laughs.
- *The penny a word* makes improvising a cost. An ad-lib is a choice with a price in fines and a gain in the house's
  temper; the player is choosing how much truth the company can afford.

## 4. Are there real alternatives?

The treatment lists approaches but most small quests resolve to "persuade" or "find". Several (the Pirate Quarto, the
Bear in the Shambles) are fetches with a complication described rather than played.

*Revision:* every quest in the revised inventory has at least two approaches that need *different* things (a skill, an
item, a person, a place, time) and at least one approach that needs no charisma and no learning. The build matrix is
drawn up before a line of Kyr is written, and each row is tested.

## 5. Does it reward different characters?

The treatment imagines a bard. Kyrie's callings are warrior, mage, thief, cleric, bard, ranger and monk, and the
skills that open acts are brawn, climbing, stealth, lockwork, mending, lore, insight, persuasion, deception,
intimidation, woodcraft and a few others.

*Revision:* the theatre needs exactly these people. A **warrior** fights the stage fight in Act II for real when
Lisle's ringer comes on with a sharp blade, carries the Ghost's armour and holds the stage door against the
bailiffs. A **thief** gets the fair copy back from the pirate, works the trap under the stage (lockwork, and it is a
lock) and lifts a tallyman's stick. A **mage**'s light is the Ghost's entrance. A **cleric** is the only person Canon
Mallory will hear on the subject of satire of the dead, and can claim sanctuary for goods. A **ranger** handles
Duchess. **Mending** is carpentry: the trap, the fountain conduit, the Wheel's broken flying-rig. **Climbing** is the
flies. Persuasion and insight are the green room. A low-charisma fighter with no letters should be able to do a full,
different week: the bear, the bailiffs, the watermen, the trap, the stage fight, the fountain.

## 6. Too much exposition?

**Yes.** The treatment has eleven secrets (Gosse's deafness, the letters, Clemency's authorship, Lisle's old love,
Clay's hissed tragedy, Ruddle's love for Vane, Mallory's ballads, Callow's debts, the Pudding–Vane quarrel, the
Fountain's water, the riot), and almost every one is imagined as something a person says when asked. That is a
quiz, not a mystery.

*Revision:* cut four (Lisle's old love, Mallory's ballads, Callow's debts as a separate secret, and the funeral
joke's content as a reveal), and make the others things *found, seen or caused*:

- Gosse's deafness is seen in rehearsal (he misses a cue the player gave from his bad side) before anyone mentions it.
- The letters are an object in the property chest.
- Clemency's authorship is proved by paper, hand and a programme from forty years ago, each found in a place.
- Clay's hissed tragedy is a manuscript in the Wheel's tiring-house, annotated in Gosse's young hand.
- Ruddle's love is a scrapbook at her stall of every bad notice she ever gave Vane, each kept with a pressed flower.
- The water's course is traced on the ground, by following it.

## 7. Are the comic moments earned?

Only the ones that are set up. Vane's lines are funny only if the player has seen Vane being Vane first. The bear is
funny only if the bear has been established as gentle, and then is not where she should be. The riot of forty years
ago is funny only if Gosse has told it as a triumph and the player then hears Clemency tell it as the night her father
was humiliated in front of her.

*Revision:* the order of acquaintance is designed. Vane is met first at his most absurd and most dignified (washing
his own linen in the inn yard, declaiming); Duchess is met first asleep with her head on Pudding's foot; the riot is
told twice, by two people, in different districts, and the second telling is the one that matters.

## 8. Romance

Two romances among people over fifty who never spoke is one romance twice.

*Revision:* two different romances in two registers.

- **Gosse and Hepzibah** is tender and almost silent, and is the player's to protect or spoil: the letters, the
  epilogue, and whether she is in the wings to hear it.
- **Vane and Ruddle** becomes a gulling plot (Much Ado, stage-managed): the company decides the tragedian and the
  critic who has savaged him for fifteen years are in love, and the player is handed the forged notes to deliver.
  It is a game of deception with a cost: they may actually be in love, or the trick may humiliate both and lose a
  critic's vote. The scrapbook is the evidence that the company's joke is truer than the company knows.

## 9. Does a character meaningfully change?

The treatment has five arcs on paper. The test is whether any changes *because of the player*, and whether the
change can be seen in play rather than in an epilogue.

*Revision:* two changes are made the spine.

- **Vane** changes in Act II on the night, by what the player told him and what the house does: he hears the roar,
  and either breaks (and leaves) or understands that he has made them happy and plays the rest of the act for it.
  The epilogue tells what he does next; the scene shows the change.
- **Clemency Aske** changes in front of her own clerk: the player brings the struck book back to her, and she
  countermands her Office or upholds it. On the night she is in the front row, and may stand up.

## 10. The play itself

Nine scenes with beats is the right size, but the treatment imagines the night as a run of checks: an actor dries,
roll; a heckler, roll. That is the "handful of performance checks" the commission warns against.

*Revision:* **the night is the consequence of the week.** Each scene's shape is decided by preparation: who was cast
(a part played by the wrong actor goes differently, and each actor has a scene they are good in), what was cut, what
was fixed (the trap, the rig, the fountain-prop), what was rehearsed and with whom, who is in the house (Garlick, the
tallymen, the critics, the crowd the watermen let over), whether the bear is fed. In the scene the player gets one or
two live choices that preparation made possible (cue the light, cover the dry with an ad-lib at a penny a word, signal
the prompt to Gosse from his good side, go on yourself). The house's temper and each critic's regard are moved by
both. A player who prepared well watches the play go right and has small, delicious choices; one who did not spends
the night in the wings putting out fires. Both are playing.

## 11. Two principal approaches

The treatment's two "entries" (on the road, by report) are a good idea but are not yet two approaches to the *story*.

*Revision:* there are two principal approaches to the play, which the week makes and the night tests.

1. **The licensed play** ("the Book"): send it in, fight the Office line by line, cut what must be cut, play it as
   licensed in the Wheel with the tallymen silent. It wins Clay and Mallory, can win the Patent, and depends on
   Clemency.
2. **The whole play** ("the Liberty"): never send it in; play it whole, in the Soke under the liberty's law, with the
   tallymen counting every word and the fines read out at the end. It wins Ruddle and Callow and the crowd, can win
   the Patent only with a third critic, and costs the company the City.

They differ in where the week is spent (the Office and the Close, or the Soke and the Shambles), who becomes an ally
(Clemency and Mallory, or Callow and Garlick), what the night's dangers are (a struck line forgotten, or the Office
standing up in the front row), and how Pentrice remembers it. A third, **the Author's Bow**, opens only to a player
who has solved the mystery and brought Clemency to the point of claiming her own play.

The entries remain, as the two ways the protagonist comes into the story, and are kept distinct: a member of the
company is cast and can go on; a stranger comes in by the Office (as a temporary tallyman for Trial week) or by
Gosse's yard, and can join later.

## 12. Scale

Seventeen quests, thirty-odd people, a week, a generated-town prologue and a generated-town tour after. The
Tessary report says 2,330 lines of Kyr held 55 rooms, 33 people, 109 topics and 49 actions. This treatment would be
larger.

*Revision:* the tour after the ending is cut to what the generated world can carry honestly: an epilogue, and the word
in the first country's towns. The prologue on the green stays, because it is the commission's point. Two quests are
merged (the critics' table becomes four individual critics, each with one quest), the Fenns become one person, and the
bailiffs and the Pirate Quarto are made smaller. The city's districts stay at seven, with about thirty rooms.

## 13. Names

Names were chosen without looking. A search of the repository and of the branch *The Third Night* (Wrayford, in
progress) is required before implementation, and again before merge, as Tessary's friction report (W25) asks. "The
Toll", from the repertoire survey's sketch of this commission, is avoided: Wrayford is about a bridge toll.
