**[To Sixways street]** Six days of the week Sixways is a hamlet on the march with too many carts for its size, and the seventh it is a door. Everyone here lives by the Lull: they cart for it, buy its cloth, sell it what it will not make, marry into it, wait for it. They count its days on a board by the pump and a stone on the rise. They say of someone who has gone in and not come out for a long while that they are lingering, and they do not say it unkindly. Sixways street: the pump, the inn, a carter’s yard, a herbwife’s door with bunches hung in the porch, and the lane to a row of three cottages that stand a little apart. Everybody here seems to know what day it is in a way that people elsewhere do not. The board by the pump says, in chalk: LULL IN 2. Here are Quick Nan Fossick, a pedlar with a tray; and Gurney, a big silent man who carries Quick Nan’s baskets.

**[Into the Waiting Woman]** The Waiting Woman’s taproom is low and long, with a settle by the fire and a window at the end that looks up the road to the stone. Everybody who sits down sits facing that window. On the wall is the inn’s old sign, taken down and kept indoors: a young woman at a window, in fresh paint, her face very carefully done. Dorcas Penhallow, who keeps the Waiting Woman, is here.

**[Talk to Dorcas Penhallow]** A handsome woman of sixty or so, in a good blue gown that is a little young for her, looks up from the barrel she is tapping. ‘Come in, come in, shut it, the wind comes straight down off the stone.’ She looks at your boots. ‘Come for the Lull, have you? Everyone has. Sit where you can see the road; everyone does.’

**[Ask Dorcas Penhallow about the sign on the wall]** ‘That’s me,’ says Dorcas, not looking at it. ‘At twenty-eight. My husband painted it, the week we married, before the old sign blew down. I touch her up every spring.’ She does look at it, then. ‘He’s in there. Piran. In the Lull. Thirty years we’ve been married, my count, and four of his.’ She turns back to the barrel. ‘I go in on Lulldays. He says I look tired. I tell him it’s the walk.’

**[Ask Dorcas Penhallow what a week is like, waiting]** Dorcas puts down the cloth. ‘Six days,’ she says. ‘You keep busy. Carters, the factor’s people, folk come to look at the stone. The first day after is the worst: you’ve just seen him, and he’s just seen you, and for him it was this morning.’ She laughs, short. ‘For him I come every day. Can you think of it? Every single morning, there’s his wife at the door, a bit older. He told me once he can see me age if he looks hard. He was being kind. He meant it was hard to see.’ She picks the cloth back up. ‘He doesn’t know I’m poorly. Ruth Coll says a year, two. That’s three months to him, three and a half.’ Then, briskly: ‘What’ll you have?’

**[Take your leave]** ‘Come back and tell me how it goes!’ calls Dorcas Penhallow after you.

**[Out to Sixways street]** Sixways street: the pump, the inn, a carter’s yard, a herbwife’s door with bunches hung in the porch, and the lane to a row of three cottages that stand a little apart. Here are Quick Nan Fossick, a pedlar with a tray; and Gurney, a big silent man who carries Quick Nan’s baskets.

**[To the Fold-stone]** The Fold-stone is a grey stone, waist-high, on the bare top of the rise, notched along both edges with seven notches to a row, row on row, so many that the lower ones are worn to dimples. Nothing hangs on it but the weather. From it the road goes on round a bare green hill, and comes back to the stone. The newest notches on the stone say that the Lull is two days off. Today the road goes round the bare hill and comes back to where it began.

**[Sit down by the Fold-stone and wait for the Lull]** You sit with your back to the stone and watch the road go round the bare hill and come back, all day, and sleep in your coat against the stone, and wake, and watch it again; people come and go past you from Sixways and do not remark it, because people do. Then one morning at six the light changes, the way it does, and there is another road, going down. There is another road from the stone this morning: down, through a gap in the turf that was not there, into a valley full of beech trees and smoke and birds, with a town in it, and a mill.

**[To the Tump's shoulder]** You leave the Fold-stone with the sun low behind you and the dew still on the grass. It is the holy day. It has been the holy day in this valley for fifty-seven years, which is four hundred of the world’s, and nobody you meet here has done anything today that they did not mean to. The road you came in by will be there until midnight. Wren gains 50 experience: walked into the Lull. The road comes over the shoulder of a round green hill and the whole valley is below you at once: beech woods on both slopes going copper, a tarn at the head of it like a dropped coin, a river, a grey town of long-windowed houses, and above the town a mill of seven storeys with every window dark. Smoke goes straight up from the chimneys. Nothing moves fast. The noise that comes up to you is birds, and a fiddle, and somebody laughing a long way off, and under it all, so faint you have to stop to hear it, nothing at all: no hammer, no wheel, no cart. The Rest-wardens, in grey-gold sashes, with staves, are here.

**[To Linger green]** Down the middle of Linger green runs the Long Table, two hundred yards of trestles end to end, and people are wandering out with cloths and bowls to lay it, in no order, as the fancy takes them. Round the green stand grey weavers’ houses whose top floors are all window, the long loom-lights of the old trade, now hung with plants and curtains and, in one, a cello. Nobody is hurrying. A man is asleep on a bench in the sun with his hat over his face and a child asleep on him. Starlings everywhere: on the ridges, on the Table, on the ropes of the lanterns, talking. Here are the people of Linger, townsfolk of the Lull, in grey-gold coats fifty years old and good as new; and Hobday Pryce, the Chief Rest-warden of the Lull.

**[To Weavers' Row]** A lane of weavers’ cottages, each with its long window at the top, each window open. Out of one comes the sound of a loom going, slowly, stopping, going again: somebody weaving something for somebody. Out of another, an argument about a cake.

**[Into Piran Penhallow's workshop]** A carver’s workshop with the shavings swept into a heap for the gardens, and on every shelf small animals in limewood: hares, a heron, an owl, a woman’s head, the same woman’s head, again, again, at different ages. Piran Penhallow, a carver of Linger, is here.

**[Talk to Piran Penhallow]** A quiet man in his thirties with shavings in his beard looks up from a block of limewood. He is shaping, you see, a woman’s head. ‘Sixer,’ he says, not unkindly, and goes on shaping. ‘If you’re from the Waiting Woman, she’s not here yet. She comes at nine.’

**[Ask Piran Penhallow about the heads on his shelves]** Piran looks along the shelves: the same woman, again and again, a little apart from each other. ‘One every Lullday,’ he says. ‘Four years of mine. Two hundred and some.’ He takes one down from the near end, a young woman, and one from the far end, and puts them side by side on the bench. He does not say anything. He does not need to. ‘She thinks I can’t see it,’ he says. ‘She says the walk tires her. I let her say it.’ He puts the young one back. ‘I’m a carver. I see faces. I see hers every morning.’ He picks up his knife. ‘Don’t tell her.’
! asked first: She has not told him.

**[Tell Piran Penhallow that Dorcas is ill]** Piran’s knife stops. He sets it down, and the block, and sits with his hands on his knees for a long time. ‘A year,’ he says. ‘Two. Her count.’ He looks along the shelf. ‘Fifteen Lulldays. Twenty.’ Then he gets up and takes his coat off the peg. ‘My mother’s in the Almshouse,’ he says. ‘Agnes Crane, that sang it on the Tump. If I go out, she’ll see me once a week and I’ll see her once a year, and when I come back she’ll still be there and I’ll be old.’ He puts the coat on. ‘I’m going out. Tonight. With Dorcas. For the year, or the two.’ He looks at you. ‘Don’t say anything kind. I’ll not manage it.’ Wren gains 75 experience: told Dorcas and Piran the truth.

**[Take your leave]** Piran Penhallow lifts a hand, once.

**[Out to Weavers' Row]** A lane of weavers’ cottages, each with its long window at the top, each window open.
