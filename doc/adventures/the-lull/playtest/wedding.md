**[To Sixways street]** Six days of the week Sixways is a hamlet on the march with too many carts for its size, and the seventh it is a door. Everyone here lives by the Lull: they cart for it, buy its cloth, sell it what it will not make, marry into it, wait for it. They count its days on a board by the pump and a stone on the rise. They say of someone who has gone in and not come out for a long while that they are lingering, and they do not say it unkindly. Sixways street: the pump, the inn, a carter’s yard, a herbwife’s door with bunches hung in the porch, and the lane to a row of three cottages that stand a little apart. Everybody here seems to know what day it is in a way that people elsewhere do not. The board by the pump says, in chalk: LULL IN 2. Here are Quick Nan Fossick, a pedlar with a tray; and Gurney, a big silent man who carries Quick Nan’s baskets.

**[To Rooke's yard]** Dulcie blows through her nose at you, and puts her head out to be noticed. A carter’s yard: two carts, a heap of harness, a grey mare in a stall with her head over the door, and against the wall, under a sack, something with four legs that somebody has been making and unmaking. Abel Rooke, a carter of Sixways, is here.

**[Talk to Abel Rooke]** A big young man with sawdust in his hair is standing in front of something under a sack, looking at it as if it owed him money. ‘Don’t look,’ he says. ‘It’s a chair. Well, it’s a chair till sunset.’ He wipes his hands down his front and holds one out. ‘Abel Rooke. Carts. If you want a load taken, I’m your man; if you want a chair made, I’m not.’

**[Ask Abel Rooke about the chair]** ‘Wedding gift,’ says Abel. ‘In the Lull you give each other a thing you made, at the Long Table, the evening before, and if it’s still there at sunset you’re as good as wed.’ He lifts the sack: a plain good chair, well-jointed, in ash. ‘Wenna Spurling. She’s a chandler’s daughter in Linger. Sixty days I’ve been courting her, her count, and sixty weeks mine.’ He drops the sack. ‘Three times I’ve carried a chair in there and set it down by the Table and three times it’s come to bits at sundown in front of the whole town. Pegs, legs, shavings. Her aunt laughed so hard she had to be helped home.’ He looks at the chair. ‘I don’t know what I’m doing wrong. I mean it. I swear I mean it.’

**[Ask Abel Rooke what he likes best in the world]** ‘Wenna,’ says Abel at once. Then, because you wait: ‘And Dulcie. And the road. You’d not think it, a road, but in the morning when you’ve got the cart loaded and you come up over Sixways rise and the whole march is in front of you and Dulcie’s leaning into the collar and you’ve got a pipe going, and you don’t know who you’ll meet, and it’s just a road, going to somewhere, and you’re on it.’ He stops. He looks at the thing under the sack. ‘In the Lull you don’t go anywhere. That’s the beauty of it.’ He does not sound as if it is.

**[Take your leave]** ‘Come back and tell me how it goes!’ calls Abel Rooke after you.

**[To the Fold-stone]** The Fold-stone is a grey stone, waist-high, on the bare top of the rise, notched along both edges with seven notches to a row, row on row, so many that the lower ones are worn to dimples. Nothing hangs on it but the weather. From it the road goes on round a bare green hill, and comes back to the stone. The newest notches on the stone say that the Lull is two days off. Today the road goes round the bare hill and comes back to where it began.

**[Sit down by the Fold-stone and wait for the Lull]** You sit with your back to the stone and watch the road go round the bare hill and come back, all day, and sleep in your coat against the stone, and wake, and watch it again; people come and go past you from Sixways and do not remark it, because people do. Then one morning at six the light changes, the way it does, and there is another road, going down. There is another road from the stone this morning: down, through a gap in the turf that was not there, into a valley full of beech trees and smoke and birds, with a town in it, and a mill.

**[To the Tump's shoulder]** You leave the Fold-stone with the sun low behind you and the dew still on the grass. It is the holy day. It has been the holy day in this valley for fifty-seven years, which is four hundred of the world’s, and nobody you meet here has done anything today that they did not mean to. The road you came in by will be there until midnight. Wren gains 50 experience: walked into the Lull. The road comes over the shoulder of a round green hill and the whole valley is below you at once: beech woods on both slopes going copper, a tarn at the head of it like a dropped coin, a river, a grey town of long-windowed houses, and above the town a mill of seven storeys with every window dark. Smoke goes straight up from the chimneys. Nothing moves fast. The noise that comes up to you is birds, and a fiddle, and somebody laughing a long way off, and under it all, so faint you have to stop to hear it, nothing at all: no hammer, no wheel, no cart. The Rest-wardens, in grey-gold sashes, with staves, are here.

**[To Linger green]** Down the middle of Linger green runs the Long Table, two hundred yards of trestles end to end, and people are wandering out with cloths and bowls to lay it, in no order, as the fancy takes them. Round the green stand grey weavers’ houses whose top floors are all window, the long loom-lights of the old trade, now hung with plants and curtains and, in one, a cello. Nobody is hurrying. A man is asleep on a bench in the sun with his hat over his face and a child asleep on him. Starlings everywhere: on the ridges, on the Table, on the ropes of the lanterns, talking. Here are the people of Linger, townsfolk of the Lull, in grey-gold coats fifty years old and good as new; and Hobday Pryce, the Chief Rest-warden of the Lull.

**[Into Spurling's chandlery]** A chandlery that has nothing to sell and is full: candles hung in bunches from the beams by their wicks, spurling and beeswax and some green ones that smell of bay, and a girl at a bench dipping them, very slowly, with her tongue between her teeth. Wenna Spurling, the chandler’s daughter, who dips candles, is here.

**[Talk to Wenna Spurling]** The girl at the candle-bench looks up with her tongue still between her teeth, and takes it in. ‘Sorry. If I talk I get them lumpy.’ She hangs a pair of wet candles on the rack by their wick. ‘Wenna Spurling. You’re a Sixer. Do you know Abel Rooke? Everybody out there knows Abel, he says.’ She goes pink. ‘He says that about everybody.’

**[Ask Wenna Spurling what she wants of Abel]** Wenna puts down the wicks. ‘Don’t tell him,’ she says. ‘He loves it here. He’s always saying how restful it is.’ She dips a candle, slowly, and draws it out. ‘I want to go on the cart. I want to see the mill towns, and the downs, and the sea if there is one, and sleep under a cart in the rain and get up in the morning and not know who we’ll meet.’ She hangs the candle up. ‘I’ve dipped candles in this shop since I was six and it’s always the same afternoon. He brings the road in on his boots. I can smell it.’ She looks at you, fierce. ‘Don’t tell him. He’d give it up for me, and then he’d hate me, at sunset, every day.’

**[Tell Wenna Spurling what Abel Rooke loves]** Wenna listens with a candle forgotten in her hand, dripping. ‘He loves it,’ she says. ‘The road.’ She starts to laugh. ‘And he’s been making me chairs.’ She puts the candle down. ‘Then I’ll tell him. Today. I’ll tell him I want the road and see if he can say no to that.’ She takes off her apron. ‘If the next chair comes apart, it’ll be because he’s a fool, not because I am.’

**[Take your leave]** ‘Come back and tell me how it goes!’ calls Wenna Spurling after you.
