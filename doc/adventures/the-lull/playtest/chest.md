**[To Sixways street]** Six days of the week Sixways is a hamlet on the march with too many carts for its size, and the seventh it is a door. Everyone here lives by the Lull: they cart for it, buy its cloth, sell it what it will not make, marry into it, wait for it. They count its days on a board by the pump and a stone on the rise. They say of someone who has gone in and not come out for a long while that they are lingering, and they do not say it unkindly. Sixways street: the pump, the inn, a carter’s yard, a herbwife’s door with bunches hung in the porch, and the lane to a row of three cottages that stand a little apart. Everybody here seems to know what day it is in a way that people elsewhere do not. The board by the pump says, in chalk: LULL IN 2. Here are Quick Nan Fossick, a pedlar with a tray; and Gurney, a big silent man who carries Quick Nan’s baskets.

**[To the Fold-stone]** The Fold-stone is a grey stone, waist-high, on the bare top of the rise, notched along both edges with seven notches to a row, row on row, so many that the lower ones are worn to dimples. Nothing hangs on it but the weather. From it the road goes on round a bare green hill, and comes back to the stone. The newest notches on the stone say that the Lull is two days off. Today the road goes round the bare hill and comes back to where it began.

**[Sit down by the Fold-stone and wait for the Lull]** You sit with your back to the stone and watch the road go round the bare hill and come back, all day, and sleep in your coat against the stone, and wake, and watch it again; people come and go past you from Sixways and do not remark it, because people do. Then one morning at six the light changes, the way it does, and there is another road, going down. There is another road from the stone this morning: down, through a gap in the turf that was not there, into a valley full of beech trees and smoke and birds, with a town in it, and a mill.

**[To the Tump's shoulder]** You leave the Fold-stone with the sun low behind you and the dew still on the grass. It is the holy day. It has been the holy day in this valley for fifty-seven years, which is four hundred of the world’s, and nobody you meet here has done anything today that they did not mean to. The road you came in by will be there until midnight. Ashe gains 50 experience: walked into the Lull. The road comes over the shoulder of a round green hill and the whole valley is below you at once: beech woods on both slopes going copper, a tarn at the head of it like a dropped coin, a river, a grey town of long-windowed houses, and above the town a mill of seven storeys with every window dark. Smoke goes straight up from the chimneys. Nothing moves fast. The noise that comes up to you is birds, and a fiddle, and somebody laughing a long way off, and under it all, so faint you have to stop to hear it, nothing at all: no hammer, no wheel, no cart. The Rest-wardens, in grey-gold sashes, with staves, are here.

**[To Linger green]** Down the middle of Linger green runs the Long Table, two hundred yards of trestles end to end, and people are wandering out with cloths and bowls to lay it, in no order, as the fancy takes them. Round the green stand grey weavers’ houses whose top floors are all window, the long loom-lights of the old trade, now hung with plants and curtains and, in one, a cello. Nobody is hurrying. A man is asleep on a bench in the sun with his hat over his face and a child asleep on him. Starlings everywhere: on the ridges, on the Table, on the ropes of the lanterns, talking. Here are the people of Linger, townsfolk of the Lull, in grey-gold coats fifty years old and good as new; and Hobday Pryce, the Chief Rest-warden of the Lull.

**[Into the Almshouse of the Old Shift]** A long low room with a fire at each end and a row of high-backed chairs between, and in the chairs the Old Shift: the strikers, the eleven of them that are left, in their seventies and eighties, in coats of the first Lull-cloth ever woven. On the wall over the fire at one end, framed, is a weaver’s shirt, torn at the shoulder; over the other, a shuttle. They are arguing, comfortably, about something that happened fifty-seven years ago. The Old Shift, the eleven strikers who are left, in their chairs, are here.

**[Talk to the Old Shift]** Eleven old faces turn to look at you, and an old woman at the near end says: ‘Another one come to hear about the Strike. Sit down. We’ll tell it you, and then we’ll tell it you right.’

**[Ask the Old Shift what was said on the Tump]** ‘Holy day and never another,’ says the old woman at once. ‘Those were the words.’ ‘They were not,’ says a man with one eye. ‘It was Let it be the holy day, and the rest of the week can go hang. I was there.’ ‘We were all there, Amos.’ ‘Agnes Crane sang it,’ says a third, ‘and it was a hymn, and the words were the hymn’s, Rest and be thankful.’ They argue. It is clearly a very old argument, and a happy one. After a while the old woman leans to you and says, low: ‘It was a child that said it, in the end. Little Nell Lumb, that’s dead. She said, Can’t it just stay Sunday? And it did.’ She sits back. ‘Don’t tell them I said.’

**[Take your leave]** ‘Don’t let me keep you,’ say the Old Shift, who was not trying to.

**[Ask the Old Shift to teach you Agnes Crane's hymn, Rest and be thankful]** They teach it you the way the old teach anything: all at once, in three keys, with the words in dispute. An old woman at the far end, who you gather is Agnes Crane, beats time on the arm of her chair with a shuttle and stops them every second line. It is not a good hymn. It goes up where you expect it to go down, and the last verse is only the first one again, slower, the way you would sing it to a child you were putting to sleep. By the end of the hour you have it, and so, somehow, does the cat.

**[Out to Linger green]** Down the middle of Linger green runs the Long Table, two hundred yards of trestles end to end, and people are wandering out with cloths and bowls to lay it, in no order, as the fancy takes them. Here are Quick Nan Fossick, a pedlar with a tray; Gurney, a big silent man who carries Quick Nan’s baskets; the people of Linger, townsfolk of the Lull, in grey-gold coats fifty years old and good as new; and Hobday Pryce, the Chief Rest-warden of the Lull. By the Gift Hall steps Quick Nan has her tray open on a trestle, with Gurney behind her like a wall: pasties, tin whistles, and ribbons, red and blue and a yellow like nothing that grows in the Lull. Lull women are buying the ribbons, two and three at a time.

**[Wait › An hour]** The whole town is at the Long Table: four hundred people on benches the length of the green, under lanterns strung on poles, with the food going up and down it hand to hand. **[Wait › An hour]** The sun goes down behind the Hangers and the light goes up the Tump, the way water goes out of a basin, and leaves the top of it last. Along the Long Table people stop eating. On the grass in front of the Table the day’s makings stand in a row, each with its card, and four hundred people look at them. Among the makings on the grass, a little apart, stands a clock in a case of brass and walnut, very fine, ticking, with no card. It comes apart with the light. It does it neatly, almost politely: the case opens, the wheels lift out one by one and lie down in the grass in order of size, the spring uncoils with a small sigh. A young woman with brass dust on her hands gets up from the Table and walks out and kicks the whole lot, wheels and case and all, across the Green into the dark, and walks off after it. Nobody says anything. An old woman near you says, ‘That’s forty-two,’ to nobody.

**[Into the mill's ground floor]** The Mill’s ground floor in the grey light that comes down the stairwell from above, where the noise is. The lint of the Ravel lies on the floor like snow and drifts in the draught from the stair. The great doors are barred on the inside with a beam of black oak cut all over with letters, and they are moving: outward, a finger’s breadth, against the beam, and back, and again, very slowly, though there is nobody at them, as if the whole Mill were leaning on them from inside, breathing. The strikers’ window stands open. Across the Mill doors, on the inside, lies the strikers’ beam.

**[Into the loom floor by the mill stair]** Along the nearest row the Hands stop. All of them, at once, the shuttles hanging. They turn toward you the places where their faces are not, and the looms behind them go on by themselves. The nearest holds out a shuttle to you. There is no fighting a whole floor of this. Lie down, or take it, or go. The Hands will wait a few minutes more, no longer. The loom floor, lit grey from nowhere, and every loom going: a hundred of them, the shuttles flying, the beaters coming down, the noise like a mill-race. At each loom something is working it. They have the shape of weavers, and are made of grey lint, the Ravel packed into the shape of a woman, a boy, a man, and they have no faces, and they do not stop. The cloth comes off the looms grey and endless and goes away into the dark at the end of the hall. The Hands, shapes of grey lint in the shape of weavers, with no faces, working, are here.

**[To the bell loft]** You sing it, the hymn they sang on the Tump, Rest and be thankful, badly, and the tall thing stops with its arm up, and the hat turns as if to listen, and then it goes down the ladder backwards into the noise of the looms and is lost among the Hands. The bell loft, grey-lit and shaking with the looms below. The bell on its beam swings a little, by itself. In the corner, under sacking, stands an iron-bound chest with a hammer and a fleece painted on its lid.
