# The Lull, played: simeon (the-lull)

**[To Sixways street]** Six days of the week Sixways is a hamlet on the march with too many carts for its size, and the seventh it is a door. Everyone here lives by the Lull: they cart for it, buy its cloth, sell it what it will not make, marry into it, wait for it. They count its days on a board by the pump and a stone on the rise. They say of someone who has gone in and not come out for a long while that they are lingering, and they do not say it unkindly. Sixways street: the pump, the inn, a carter’s yard, a herbwife’s door with bunches hung in the porch, and the lane to a row of three cottages that stand a little apart. Everybody here seems to know what day it is in a way that people elsewhere do not. The board by the pump says, in chalk: LULL IN 2. Here are Quick Nan Fossick, a pedlar with a tray; and Gurney, a big silent man who carries Quick Nan’s baskets.

**[To the Fold-stone]** The Fold-stone is a grey stone, waist-high, on the bare top of the rise, notched along both edges with seven notches to a row, row on row, so many that the lower ones are worn to dimples. Nothing hangs on it but the weather. From it the road goes on round a bare green hill, and comes back to the stone. The newest notches on the stone say that the Lull is two days off. Today the road goes round the bare hill and comes back to where it began.

**[Sit down by the Fold-stone and wait for the Lull]** You sit with your back to the stone and watch the road go round the bare hill and come back, all day, and sleep in your coat against the stone, and wake, and watch it again; people come and go past you from Sixways and do not remark it, because people do. Then one morning at six the light changes, the way it does, and there is another road, going down. There is another road from the stone this morning: down, through a gap in the turf that was not there, into a valley full of beech trees and smoke and birds, with a town in it, and a mill.

**[To the Tump's shoulder]** You leave the Fold-stone with the sun low behind you and the dew still on the grass. It is the holy day. It has been the holy day in this valley for fifty-seven years, which is four hundred of the world’s, and nobody you meet here has done anything today that they did not mean to. The road you came in by will be there until midnight. Wren gains 50 experience: walked into the Lull. The road comes over the shoulder of a round green hill and the whole valley is below you at once: beech woods on both slopes going copper, a tarn at the head of it like a dropped coin, a river, a grey town of long-windowed houses, and above the town a mill of seven storeys with every window dark. Smoke goes straight up from the chimneys. Nothing moves fast. The noise that comes up to you is birds, and a fiddle, and somebody laughing a long way off, and under it all, so faint you have to stop to hear it, nothing at all: no hammer, no wheel, no cart. The Rest-wardens, in grey-gold sashes, with staves, are here.

**[To Linger green]** Down the middle of Linger green runs the Long Table, two hundred yards of trestles end to end, and people are wandering out with cloths and bowls to lay it, in no order, as the fancy takes them. Round the green stand grey weavers’ houses whose top floors are all window, the long loom-lights of the old trade, now hung with plants and curtains and, in one, a cello. Nobody is hurrying. A man is asleep on a bench in the sun with his hat over his face and a child asleep on him. Starlings everywhere: on the ridges, on the Table, on the ropes of the lanterns, talking. Here are the people of Linger, townsfolk of the Lull, in grey-gold coats fifty years old and good as new; and Hobday Pryce, the Chief Rest-warden of the Lull.

**[Into the Lindsells' house]** A weaver’s kitchen with the loom long gone and a clockmaker’s bench in the loom’s place under the long window: a vice, a lens on a stalk, trays of tiny wheels sorted by size, and a litter of brass shavings. There is no clock anywhere in the room. On the dresser, plates with starlings painted on them; on the settle, a blanket folded by somebody who folds things when they are worried.

**[To Hester Lindsell's room]** A small room with a big bed, and in the bed a very small old woman, propped up, with a face like a walnut and eyes like a bird’s. The window is open on the Gardens. There is a chair by the bed with the shape of a man worn into it, and a cup on the sill that nobody has drunk from. When she moves, her face goes white round the mouth and comes back, and she does not stop talking while it does. Here are Hester Lindsell, an old weaver of the Old Shift, who is dying; and Tobias Lindsell, an old weaver of the Old Shift, who tells the strike best.

**[Talk to Hester Lindsell]** The old woman in the bed looks at you for a while before she says anything, the way you might look at weather. ‘A Sixer,’ she says. ‘Come in, then. Shut the door; Tobias’ll fuss. Sit on the end of the bed; the chair’s his.’ She closes her eyes, opens them. ‘I’m dying. You’ll have heard. Everybody’s heard. I’ve been at it six of your years, which is ten months of mine, and it’s very dull, and nobody will talk to me about anything else.’

**[Take your leave]** ‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Hester Lindsell, who was not trying to.

**[To the Fold-stone]** You set out from the Tump’s shoulder early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. The Fold-stone is a grey stone, waist-high, on the bare top of the rise, notched along both edges with seven notches to a row, row on row, so many that the lower ones are worn to dimples.

**[To Sixways street]** Sixways street: the pump, the inn, a carter’s yard, a herbwife’s door with bunches hung in the porch, and the lane to a row of three cottages that stand a little apart. The board by the pump says, in chalk, LULLDAY, and underneath, in another hand, BACK BY MIDNIGHT.

**[To Exiles' Row]** Three cottages in a row on the slope behind the street, set a little apart from Sixways, with their windows facing up the rise to the stone. The people of Sixways call them the Outlived: Lull-born who came out, and stayed out, and got old.

**[Into Simeon Eddowes's cottage]** A room full of paper. Sheets pinned to the walls, stacked on the chair, weighted on the table with stones; every sheet headed GLOSS and a number, in a fine old hand, and some of the numbers in the thousands. Simeon Eddowes, a Glosser of the Lull, put out of it, is here.

**[Talk to Simeon Eddowes]** An old man in a white apron with no pockets looks up from his writing as though you had interrupted a sentence of some length. ‘Gloss two thousand and ninety,’ he says. ‘Whether a visitor who knocks meaning to be let in is welcome, if he does not mean to stay. Held: he is.’ He puts down the pen. ‘Simeon Eddowes. I used to be a Glosser. I’m told I still am, by the apron, which I haven’t taken off in twenty years. Sit, if you can find a chair under the law.’

**[Ask Simeon Eddowes why he was put out of the Lull]** ‘For a gloss,’ says Simeon, with relish. ‘Number nine hundred and twelve. A weaver’s lad wanted to pay the woman he loved for sitting to him while he drew her, because he was poor and proud and she was poorer and prouder, and the Rest-wardens said a wage was a wage. I held that a wage paid in love is not a wage.’ He spreads his hands. ‘They said I’d opened the door to the week. Hobday Pryce’s father was Chief Warden then. I was walked to the stone and told not to come back.’ He smiles. ‘The lad and the woman were married. The drawing held. Hobday’s father did not.’

**[Tell Simeon Eddowes that Hester Lindsell is dying]** Simeon is still for a long moment. Then he takes off his spectacles and folds them, very carefully, as if they might break. ‘Hester Lumb,’ he says. ‘Lindsell. Yes.’ He puts the spectacles down on a gloss. ‘When we were twenty, her count and mine, I asked her to walk up the Tump with me, and she went up it with Tobias Lindsell instead. He was the better man. I have held that for sixty years and it has never once ravelled.’ He looks at the window, at the stone. ‘Dying. In there, nobody dies quick. She’ll have been dying for years of mine.’ He gets up. ‘I should like to see her. I should very much like to see her. And I can’t get past the Tump.’

**[Ask them to come with you]** Simeon takes off the white apron, folds it, looks at it, and puts it back on. ‘If I am going to be turned back at the Tump,’ he says, ‘I should like them to see what they are turning back. They keep the shoulder in the mornings, the wardens. After eleven they go down to the Green to fine people for enjoying themselves.’ Simeon Eddowes falls in beside you, keeping close.

**[Take your leave]** ‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Simeon Eddowes, who was not trying to.
