**[To Sixways street]** Six days of the week Sixways is a hamlet on the march with too many carts for its size, and the seventh it is a door. Everyone here lives by the Lull: they cart for it, buy its cloth, sell it what it will not make, marry into it, wait for it. They count its days on a board by the pump and a stone on the rise. They say of someone who has gone in and not come out for a long while that they are lingering, and they do not say it unkindly. Sixways street: the pump, the inn, a carter’s yard, a herbwife’s door with bunches hung in the porch, and the lane to a row of three cottages that stand a little apart. Everybody here seems to know what day it is in a way that people elsewhere do not. The board by the pump says, in chalk: LULL IN 2. Here are Quick Nan Fossick, a pedlar with a tray; and Gurney, a big silent man who carries Quick Nan’s baskets.

**[To the Fold-stone]** The Fold-stone is a grey stone, waist-high, on the bare top of the rise, notched along both edges with seven notches to a row, row on row, so many that the lower ones are worn to dimples. Nothing hangs on it but the weather. From it the road goes on round a bare green hill, and comes back to the stone. The newest notches on the stone say that the Lull is two days off. Today the road goes round the bare hill and comes back to where it began.

**[Sit down by the Fold-stone and wait for the Lull]** You sit with your back to the stone and watch the road go round the bare hill and come back, all day, and sleep in your coat against the stone, and wake, and watch it again; people come and go past you from Sixways and do not remark it, because people do. Then one morning at six the light changes, the way it does, and there is another road, going down. There is another road from the stone this morning: down, through a gap in the turf that was not there, into a valley full of beech trees and smoke and birds, with a town in it, and a mill.

**[To the Tump's shoulder]** You leave the Fold-stone with the sun low behind you and the dew still on the grass. It is the holy day. It has been the holy day in this valley for fifty-seven years, which is four hundred of the world’s, and nobody you meet here has done anything today that they did not mean to. The road you came in by will be there until midnight. Ashe gains 50 experience: walked into the Lull. The road comes over the shoulder of a round green hill and the whole valley is below you at once: beech woods on both slopes going copper, a tarn at the head of it like a dropped coin, a river, a grey town of long-windowed houses, and above the town a mill of seven storeys with every window dark. Smoke goes straight up from the chimneys. Nothing moves fast. The noise that comes up to you is birds, and a fiddle, and somebody laughing a long way off, and under it all, so faint you have to stop to hear it, nothing at all: no hammer, no wheel, no cart. The Rest-wardens, in grey-gold sashes, with staves, are here.

**[To Linger green]** Down the middle of Linger green runs the Long Table, two hundred yards of trestles end to end, and people are wandering out with cloths and bowls to lay it, in no order, as the fancy takes them. Round the green stand grey weavers’ houses whose top floors are all window, the long loom-lights of the old trade, now hung with plants and curtains and, in one, a cello. Nobody is hurrying. A man is asleep on a bench in the sun with his hat over his face and a child asleep on him. Starlings everywhere: on the ridges, on the Table, on the ropes of the lanterns, talking. Here are the people of Linger, townsfolk of the Lull, in grey-gold coats fifty years old and good as new; and Hobday Pryce, the Chief Rest-warden of the Lull.

**[Wait › An hour]** The whole town is at the Long Table: four hundred people on benches the length of the green, under lanterns strung on poles, with the food going up and down it hand to hand. The Old Shift sit at the top, by the chapel. At the bottom the children have a table of their own and are under it more than at it. Everybody’s eyes keep going to the things set out on the grass in front of the Table, a little apart: a chair, a quilt, a cake, a clock. At the top of the Long Table Linnet Gale is playing, standing on the bench: a dance, then a slow air, then something new that nobody knows, which the Table listens to with its spoons held still. An hour passes.

**[Wait › An hour]** The sun goes down behind the Hangers and the light goes up the Tump, the way water goes out of a basin, and leaves the top of it last. Along the Long Table people stop eating. On the grass in front of the Table the day’s makings stand in a row, each with its card, and four hundred people look at them. Among the makings on the grass, a little apart, stands a clock in a case of brass and walnut, very fine, ticking, with no card. It comes apart with the light. It does it neatly, almost politely: the case opens, the wheels lift out one by one and lie down in the grass in order of size, the spring uncoils with a small sigh. A young woman with brass dust on her hands gets up from the Table and walks out and kicks the whole lot, wheels and case and all, across the Green into the dark, and walks off after it. Nobody says anything. An old woman near you says, ‘That’s forty-two,’ to nobody.

**[Wait › An hour]** The Long Table is being cleared by whoever is nearest, and the lanterns are going out one by one along the poles. A few people are still talking at the far end. Somebody plays a last tune in the music room over the Open Hand, and people stop on their way home to hear the end of it. An hour passes. Ashe gains 25 experience: sat through a Watching.

**[Wait › An hour]** It is pitch dark. Without a light you can see nothing here, and do nothing that needs seeing. From up the leat, very faint, comes a sound like rain on a roof that does not stop. It is past midnight. There was no sound to it: the starlings stopped, all at once, in the middle of a note, and the lanterns along the Long Table went out together as if a door had shut on them, and the stars went. Not behind cloud. Out. You are standing in the Lull in a dark with no top to it, and nobody else in the valley is awake. An hour passes. It is a long time since you slept. You are weary, and will fight the worse for it until you sleep.

**[To the Mill yard]** You go out of Linger into the dark, by what light there is. It is pitch dark. Without a light you can see nothing here, and do nothing that needs seeing.

**[Pack › A candle › Light]** You light a candle. The Mill yard in the dark, and the Mill above it lit grey from within, every window, all seven storeys, and the whole building faintly shaking. From inside comes a sound like heavy rain on a roof that does not stop: looms, hundreds of them, going. The boarded window low down is not boarded. It is open, the way it was the night the strikers climbed out of it. High on the Mill’s end wall a hoist-beam sticks out over a loading door at the top floor, with a stub of chain still hanging from it. The Shift Bridge is down: two old piers, and the leat running deep and quick between them. The strikers’ window stands open.

**[Into the bell loft]** By the bell, in the grey light, something unfolds that you took for a heap of sacking: tall, iron under lint, a bailiff’s hat where a face should be. It lifts one long arm toward the bell-rope. It does not care that you are here; it cares that you are idle here. It will ring, and come, unless you take up work, or lie down like a striker, or are gone. Someone who could sing the strikers’ hymn at it might make it remember what it lost. The Overseer will wait 9 minutes more, no longer. The bell loft, grey-lit and shaking with the looms below. The bell on its beam swings a little, by itself. In the corner, under sacking, stands an iron-bound chest with a hammer and a fleece painted on its lid. The Overseer, a tall shape of iron and lint with a bailiff’s hat for a face, beside the bell, is here.
