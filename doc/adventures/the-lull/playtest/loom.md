**[To Sixways street]** Six days of the week Sixways is a hamlet on the march with too many carts for its size, and the seventh it is a door. Everyone here lives by the Lull: they cart for it, buy its cloth, sell it what it will not make, marry into it, wait for it. They count its days on a board by the pump and a stone on the rise. They say of someone who has gone in and not come out for a long while that they are lingering, and they do not say it unkindly. Sixways street: the pump, the inn, a carter’s yard, a herbwife’s door with bunches hung in the porch, and the lane to a row of three cottages that stand a little apart. Everybody here seems to know what day it is in a way that people elsewhere do not. The board by the pump says, in chalk: LULL IN 2. Here are Quick Nan Fossick, a pedlar with a tray; and Gurney, a big silent man who carries Quick Nan’s baskets.

**[To the Fold-stone]** The Fold-stone is a grey stone, waist-high, on the bare top of the rise, notched along both edges with seven notches to a row, row on row, so many that the lower ones are worn to dimples. Nothing hangs on it but the weather. From it the road goes on round a bare green hill, and comes back to the stone. The newest notches on the stone say that the Lull is two days off. Today the road goes round the bare hill and comes back to where it began.

**[Sit down by the Fold-stone and wait for the Lull]** You sit with your back to the stone and watch the road go round the bare hill and come back, all day, and sleep in your coat against the stone, and wake, and watch it again; people come and go past you from Sixways and do not remark it, because people do. Then one morning at six the light changes, the way it does, and there is another road, going down. There is another road from the stone this morning: down, through a gap in the turf that was not there, into a valley full of beech trees and smoke and birds, with a town in it, and a mill.

**[To the Tump's shoulder]** You leave the Fold-stone with the sun low behind you and the dew still on the grass. It is the holy day. It has been the holy day in this valley for fifty-seven years, which is four hundred of the world’s, and nobody you meet here has done anything today that they did not mean to. The road you came in by will be there until midnight. Wren gains 50 experience: walked into the Lull. The road comes over the shoulder of a round green hill and the whole valley is below you at once: beech woods on both slopes going copper, a tarn at the head of it like a dropped coin, a river, a grey town of long-windowed houses, and above the town a mill of seven storeys with every window dark. Smoke goes straight up from the chimneys. Nothing moves fast. The noise that comes up to you is birds, and a fiddle, and somebody laughing a long way off, and under it all, so faint you have to stop to hear it, nothing at all: no hammer, no wheel, no cart. The Rest-wardens, in grey-gold sashes, with staves, are here.

**[To Linger green]** Down the middle of Linger green runs the Long Table, two hundred yards of trestles end to end, and people are wandering out with cloths and bowls to lay it, in no order, as the fancy takes them. Round the green stand grey weavers’ houses whose top floors are all window, the long loom-lights of the old trade, now hung with plants and curtains and, in one, a cello. Nobody is hurrying. A man is asleep on a bench in the sun with his hat over his face and a child asleep on him. Starlings everywhere: on the ridges, on the Table, on the ropes of the lanterns, talking. Here are the people of Linger, townsfolk of the Lull, in grey-gold coats fifty years old and good as new; and Hobday Pryce, the Chief Rest-warden of the Lull.

**[Pack › A torch › Light]** You light a torch. The Mill yard in the dark, and the Mill above it lit grey from within, every window, all seven storeys, and the whole building faintly shaking. **[Into the mill's ground floor]** The Mill’s ground floor in the grey light that comes down the stairwell from above, where the noise is. **[Into the loom floor by the mill stair]** Along the nearest row the Hands stop. All of them, at once, the shuttles hanging. They turn toward you the places where their faces are not, and the looms behind them go on by themselves. The nearest holds out a shuttle to you. There is no fighting a whole floor of this. Lie down, or take it, or go. The Hands will wait 7 minutes more, no longer. The loom floor, lit grey from nowhere, and every loom going: a hundred of them, the shuttles flying, the beaters coming down, the noise like a mill-race. At each loom something is working it. They have the shape of weavers, and are made of grey lint, the Ravel packed into the shape of a woman, a boy, a man, and they have no faces, and they do not stop. The cloth comes off the looms grey and endless and goes away into the dark at the end of the hall. The Hands, shapes of grey lint in the shape of weavers, with no faces, working, are here.

**[Work the loom, as they want, until the Lull's morning]** For the Lull it is the next morning; for you, a week on. Somebody has tied a black thread to the top of the Strike Post. In the Gardens, people talk low over the beans. Hester Lindsell died in the night, and tonight is her Watching; the town says the wasting took her at last, and that it was a mercy, and some of them look at the Lindsells’ door while they say it. The Working Day is the next Lullday. You work. There is no other word for it, and no other thing. The shuttle goes, the beater comes down, the cloth comes, and the grey light does not change, and you do not get hungry or thirsty or sleep, and you do not stop, because there is nothing to stop for, and the Hands beside you do not stop. Somewhere in it you understand that this is what the strikers stopped. Then the bell above you rings once, and you are standing in the Mill yard in the morning with starlings going over and a bolt of grey cloth in your arms that you wove, and your hands ache to the shoulder, and it is Lullday. The six days are over. The dark has gone the way it came, all at once, and there are starlings, and the smell of the Long Table, and it is Lullday; and you have been where nobody in the Lull has been but Hob Linsey and his bees.
