# The repertoire: what Kyrie's adventures tell, and what they have not yet learned to

*An independent critical survey of the seven adventures in `data/adventures`, read together as one collection. It
ends with a map of what the collection lacks, six proposals for new work, and a recommendation for what to commission
next. 26 October 2026. Spoilers for all seven.*

**What was read.** Every adventure's design README and its reports in `doc/adventures`: treatments, critiques,
fidelity specifications, friction ledgers and judgments. The Kyr of each, sampled file by file and read whole in
places. The play transcripts in `doc/playtests/low-water-2026-10-25` and `doc/adventures/what-tessary-owes/playtest`.
The design atlas (`doc/design/ATLAS.md`), the authoring guide, the editorial standards and the five origins. I did not
replay each work in the browser. Where this report says what a player experiences, it relies on the transcripts, the
journeys and each work's own playtest. Counts of words are taken over each module's Kyr with `grep -oi`. They are
evidence of habit, not of quality.

---

## 1. In brief

1. **Seven adventures, one story.** Every work in the collection tells the same story. A community lives by
   something it inherited and misunderstands: a dam it calls a barrow, a titan it calls the fells, a prayer that is
   a granary label, a mountain whose weight is what its people know, an oath, a hymn that is a machine's program.
   A contemporary interest strains that inheritance. A conscientious outsider reads the evidence before a deadline.
   The work then ends in a table of ways the community can stand toward what it inherited. It is a good story. The
   collection has told it seven times.
2. **Its ethic is stewardship, and its grain is epistemic.** In all seven, minds change when someone is *shown*
   something. Reforms made by good people break inherited systems, and the resolution restores the old practice,
   rightly understood, or makes it an institution. Kyr's strongest primitives are `knows`, `Discovery` and a `Topic`
   gated on what is known. The language makes stories about finding out easy, and the collection has written those.
3. **When a work grows, it grows down and out.** Five of the seven end outside the world. Four name the Kiritarun or
   the Varakiiri. The atlas says in so many words that this is not obligatory (§27), but every revision that asked
   for more found it below the floor.
4. **The two most recent works are converging on each other.** *What Tessary Owes* and *Low Water* both have a city
   that owes a creditor beneath it, a drowned Aenean layer, and doubling discovered in revision as the governing
   figure. Each has a clerk who cannot or will not lie, in a grey coat with a ledger, who says "noted" and pays for
   their honesty at the end. They are the collection's two best companions, and they are the same character.
5. **The protagonist wants nothing, is guilty of nothing, and is loved by no one.** They arrive, read, show and
   choose. The companions have arcs; the protagonist has findings.
6. **What Kyrie has not learned to express:** wanting; being at fault; a person as an end rather than a witness;
   making something; comedy as a structure rather than a texture; fear that lasts; and a story that stays small.
7. **Commission *The Third Night* next.** It is a tragedy at the scale of *The Long Man*: a homecoming to a father
   chained on a bridge rail, three days long, with nothing to discover and everything to decide. It breaks the
   template on every axis at once. It completes the kin origin's letter, which is already in the game. And it forces,
   at the smallest cost, the capability every later proposal needs: people whose minds are changed by people, not by
   exhibits.

| **What Tessary Owes** | 2,360 | Investigates a lawful collection; works a tidal ruin; negotiates with alien clerks; stands with the Muster | Investigation and deadline, in five movements | City comedy into wonder | The Holding, a white floor under no sky |
| **Low Water** | 2,907 | Measures; reads a hymn as code; works a city's water; gives a Finding at a Hearing | Professional investigation ending in testimony; then a creditor's citadel | Dry civic wit, then mirror-horror | The Glass Citadel, beyond the Interval |

**Declared ambition against what can be played.**

- ***The Long Man*** is the purest of the seven and the shortest. What it declares, it delivers. Its one
  misunderstanding has a physical consequence. Its people act on their own clocks. One of its endings leaves the
  dale ignorant of how close it came, and that is the bravest thing in the collection's endings. It is also the
  template for everything after it.
- ***Thrume*** declares a six-part supermodule and plays its critical path in 199 steps over three days. Several of
  its branches (House Lull's betrayal, the war on day ten) are designed but not written, and three endings are written
  but not tested ([thrume/README.md](thrume/README.md)). It is the most conventional of the seven: keys from three
  peoples, a tomb, an abyss. It is also the one that most gives the old pleasures honestly: Skarn kills a company that
  ignores the challenge.
- ***The House That Grows*** set out to test whether a strange physical law could be written in Kyr, and the law
  works. Its emotional centre is a relationship, which is rare in this collection: a student who wants to know why her
  teacher shut her eyes on purpose ("I want to know why she did it"). The reader undercut it by filing the world's
  central verb under "Yourself". Of the seven, it is the least driven by a clock and the most domestic.
- ***The Patient Star*** adapts an outside sourcebook. Its opening, *Hold One Season*, is among the best openings in
  the collection: a debt, a held uncle, counterfeit felt told by a wet thumb. The setting's emotional heart is that
  being slow is the danger. That survived only as half pace, and the weeks in the Keeping survived as 35-second
  clicks ([patient-star/README.md](patient-star/README.md) §1). Companions acting on their own did not survive.
- ***Let One Remember*** has, in Act I, the collection's most original social mechanism: "telling is acting", people
  moved one at a time, a crowd told at once dropping a mountain. Acts II to V each give a whole plane or a war to a
  single file of 150 to 290 lines. The war is fought by an army no spell can touch, and it happens mostly offstage.
  The ambition outran the texture.
- ***What Tessary Owes*** has the collection's best idea that is also a mechanism, obligation as physics, and its best
  ending, the Release. Its own judgment is exact: "It is still, at bottom, an investigation followed by a deadline."
  A cold player had the mystery by the first night and waited eleven days. The Board is "a well-written vote".
  ([what-tessary-owes/08-judgment.md](what-tessary-owes/08-judgment.md))
- ***Low Water*** has, in Act I and the Hearing, the collection's high point of civic drama. The Accord transcript's
  Hearing, with Orr's confession and Quill's "Probably. In the end.", is as good as anything Kyrie has produced. Then
  the second pass, commissioned as "ten times the ambition" ([low-water/07-second-pass.md](low-water/07-second-pass.md)),
  added a Varakiir creditor, a mirror city and a glass citadel, and brought the endings to twenty. Its own critique
  called the result, before revision, "a better adventure and a worse book". The best of it is still the smallest of
  it.

## 3. The story the collection keeps telling

### The misread inheritance

| | Protagonist and hook | What is inherited, and how it is misread | What strains it now | Clock | Shape of space | Minds change by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Long Man | A hired company; a bounty | A dam, read as a giant's barrow; gauge-notches read as a tally of sins | The steward selling the bronze; the miller's spade | Storm, 3 days | Into the wall | Being shown: the notches, the spade-cuts, the lantern |
| Thrume | A hired company; a bounty and a missing son | A titan, read as the fells; its blood, read as salt | The Margravine's cutting, to pay for a war | Stirring to 10; the host on day 10 | Down through a body | Being shown: the warm salt, the gauge of sleep |
| House | An apprentice; a teacher who lies about her dreams | A dream-house, read as a relic to buy | The Company buying and mapping | The council sells on day 3 | Up to the Keel | Being told: the house is one house with the city |
| Patient Star | A debtor; a held uncle | A light, and a prayer that is a granary label | A citadel quarrying moments; the Corbel coming down | The Corbel, 2 m a day | Down an inverted citadel; out past the planes | Evidence moving the Concord's seats |
| Let One Remember | A hired company; a debt | A mountain whose weight is its people's knowing | Runners paid to cut links | Gales to midwinter | Down the Well; up and out | Telling, as physics |
| Tessary | A drifter; a mother's notice | An oath and a war-loan; a release clause nobody reads | The Factor buying the note | Feast on day 15 | Down through Lowtide to the Holding | Proof laid before the Board |
| Low Water | An Independent Assessor | A hymn that is a machine's program | A good Cantor's reform; the mills' channel | The Hearing on day 6 | Down under the Mere; up the Interval | Exhibits raising the Council's credence |

Read across, the rows are one sentence. *An outsider, hired or obliged, comes to a community that lives by an
inheritance it has misread. A present interest has begun to break that inheritance. The outsider reads the evidence
cut into the place, shows the right people, and descends to the mechanism before a deadline. Then the community chooses
how to stand toward what it inherited.*

### Chesterton's fence, seven times

In every work, the person who breaks the inherited system is a reformer, a modernizer or an entrepreneur, and usually
a decent one:

- a steward who strips assets;
- a miller who widens a spring;
- a margravine who mines for a war;
- a Company that buys a relic;
- a Hartmark that pays for cut links;
- a factor who means to govern honestly;
- a Cantor who shortened a liturgy so the young would come.

The resolution restores the old practice rightly understood: sing the Seventh again, put the teeth back, give the
song. Or it makes the old practice an institution: the Measured Accord, the Kept Promise, the roster. *What Tessary
Owes* comes nearest to an exception, because its Release is something the soldiers invent. Even there, the invention
is reading a clause that was always in the oath.

This is a coherent and sympathetic philosophy: a quiet conservatism of the steward, Le Guin's more than Burke's. It
is one position, held seven times. It tells the player that the new is dangerous unless it is the old understood. The
atlas's own philosophical direction (§5) argues against a template of contradiction and resolution, and for "a
differential account of transformation, invention… the emergence of possibilities not already implicit". Nothing in
the collection is invented that was not already there to be found.

### Minds change when shown things

Seven for seven. Ysolt changes her mind entirely when shown what the notches are. Hob closes his cuts when shown
them. Cass will not carry the bronze once told what it held shut. Brannoc stops cutting if shown that the salt is
blood. The Concord moves on evidence. *Let One Remember* makes it the setting's first law. The Factor talks about her
father once shown the Room of Defaults. Low Water's Council acts "on what it is shown", exhibit by exhibit.

There are exceptions, and they are the most moving scenes in the collection:

- Teshen naming Corin Webb and giving him a house;
- Sella Marsh's price, a promise to speak first for the mill hands;
- Quire, given a reason not to drown;
- Durrance deciding to say *I hold it quit*.

These are minds changed by being named, by being promised, by being heard. They are a handful, against a repertoire
built on exhibits.

The cause is partly the language. *The House That Grows*' assessment says it plainly: "The language rewards an
author who thinks in states, evidence and consequences. It punishes one who thinks in perception, bodies and physical
quantities." The Patient Star's report calls its own best mechanism "knowledge as keys". When the language's
strongest primitive is a fact the company comes to know, and a topic gated on it, the easiest story is the one about
finding out.

### The escalation reflex

| | Ends outside the world | Names the Kiritarun or Varakiiri | Mathematical Age or older machine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long Man | — | — | a Hesk keeper, not Mathematical |
| Thrume | the Between | (the Mouth, its own) | the Warder |
| House | — | — | — |
| Patient Star | the Keeping; the Masonry | ✓ | ✓ (Seridane mirrors, the Arbiters' Way) |
| Let One Remember | the Margin; beyond the gates of being | ✓ | ✓ (the Registry) |
| Tessary | the Holding | ✓ (the nine lights) | ✓ (the Strongrooms) |
| Low Water | the Interval; the Glass Citadel | ✓ | ✓ (the Reckoner) |

The atlas lists, among "things explicitly not to make obligatory", "cosmological spectacle, planar travel, Kiritarun,
or Mathematical Age artifacts in every story". The collection has not made them obligatory. It has made them the only
direction available for growth. The editorial cycles that produced the strongest work here all ask for more depth:

- history beyond the plot;
- the reversal foreshadowed;
- the doubles rhymed;
- more danger.

Every time, "more depth" was found by going *older and further down*, never by going *wider and nearer*: more
ordinary life, more relationship, more present time. In this collection, depth means age.

### The conscientious outsider

Four of the seven pitch "a company for" a place and say "choose three companions", with the same Elen, Bran and Dori.
The atlas corrected exactly this opening (§2). The other three give a person: a debtor, an apprentice, a drifter.
Across all seven the protagonist's work is to find out what is true. Their want is the hook's coin, or a debt halved,
and it is forgotten by the second scene. No work makes them the cause of the trouble. No work puts someone they love
at its centre (Garret is an uncle, and his rescue is the opening's hook rather than the campaign's heart). No work
asks them to make anything, or to be changed in what they believe about themselves. The companions do the changing:
Isaura, the Valuer, Teshen, Durrance. The protagonist witnesses it.

### The ending menu and the iterative coda

Every work ends in a table of endings. They run from four to twenty, and each is a different stance the community can
take toward its inheritance: keep it, break it, use it, let it finish, or make it an institution. Most tables carry a
sacrifice:

- the Re-Sealing, where a keeper stays in the Chair;
- the Broken Note, the Valuer's grey coat with nobody in it;
- the False Measure, after which Isaura never measures again;
- the Long Watch;
- Saro holding the Heart;
- Teshen staying in the grey.

So tragedy is in the collection, but as one flavour on a menu, chosen at the climax. It is never a form the work
narrows toward.

The endings also share a cadence. Each closes in the iterative present on a custom that keeps the memory:

> on the night of every spring ebb she beats the stand-down on it, at two in the morning, for anyone who might be
> listening. (*Tessary*, the Release)

> somebody keeps chalking AND SUNG, and the Office keeps not washing it off. (*Low Water*, the Accord)

> The children of the Withies still call the stair at the end of the boardwalk Docket's Step, though none of them can
> say why. (*Tessary*)

> the jewel sings once an hour to nothing at all. (*The Patient Star*)

It is lovely every time, and it consoles every time. Loss in this collection is always survived and ritualised. No
work ends on a loss it does not redeem with a custom.

## 4. Convergence, and what is coherence

Some repetitions are Imbar: a world with habits, which makes the next adventure feel as if it is in the same world.
Others are one author's habit, repeated by authors who read each other. The difference matters, because the first
should be kept on purpose and the second varied on purpose.

| Motif | Where | Reading | Advice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Honest weights, scales on counters, swearing as a public act | Tessary, Low Water, the Patient Star's weighing-yard, Saint Evenhand | **Imbar's.** A world that weighs and swears is a real culture | Keep. Record it as a cultural fact in the atlas, so the next author uses it rather than rediscovering it |
| Bells as a town's clock | Long Man, Tessary, Low Water, Thrume | **Imbar's** | Keep |
| Inscriptions in capitals; no voice in capitals | All seven; Low Water's critique made it a rule | **House typography** | Keep |
| Drowned Aenean ruins revealed when water falls | Tessary, Low Water; the Long Man's Hesk town | **Imbar's layer, revealed by a habit.** That the Aenean world drowned is history; that each adventure *drains* it into view is a device | The next Aenean site should be dry, or lived in, or absent |
| A keeper of an inherited mechanism | All seven (`keeper` appears in every module) | **The collection's ethic, now a reflex** | Commission works with no mechanism to keep |
| An old woman who holds the key or the rhyme | Gammer Coller, the Rememberer, Mother Saska, the Prioress, Cantor Orr, Old Gudrun | **A device.** It could be an Imbar institution, if it were named as one | Either make it one (a named office of memory) or retire it |
| A ledger or tally as the decisive exhibit | Six of seven (Venn's ledger, Hauk's, the Mutual's boards, the Registry, the Room of Defaults, Isaura's tally) | **Habit, born of the language's grain** | Rest it |
| A vote, a council, a hearing | House, Patient Star, Tessary, Low Water | **Habit.** Tessary's editor called the vote "a spreadsheet"; Low Water rebuilt it as a hearing | Rest it for at least two commissions |
| A clerk who cannot or will not lie, in a grey coat, with a ledger, who says "noted", and whose honesty costs them at the end | Tessary's Ninth Valuer; Low Water's Isaura Vant | **The most important accidental convergence in the collection.** Its two finest companions are one character written twice | The next companion should be wrong, warm, frightened, funny or in love |
| Doubling found in revision as the governing figure | Tessary 09 ("doubling everywhere and nowhere"); Low Water 09 ("double entry") | **One editorial checklist producing one discovery** | Notice the checklist |
| A creditor beneath the city | Tessary (the Balance, the Comptroller); Low Water (the Auditor) | **Convergence of plot** | Do not write a third |
| Empty coats and worn things without wearers | Thrume's pegs, Tessary's "coats with nobody in", the House's hall of coats, the Unsaid's gloves and hats | **An image habit, and a good image** | Use it once more at most |
| Kiritarun and Varakiiri at the end | Four of seven | **The escalation reflex** | Commission works that end in the parish they began in |

### One narrator

The seven are said to be by independent authors. They have one narrator: second person, present tense, plain
declaratives, concrete nouns, speech followed by a small action beat ("She looks at your hands." "He wipes his
knife." "She drinks."), numbers given exactly ("Twenty-two ells and a half, if I'm generous to them"), and a
characteristic sentence built on an absence: "Nobody laughs." "Nobody releases anybody." "Nobody since has put those
three sentences together." `nobody` appears 7 times in *The Long Man* and 79 times each in *Tessary* and *Low Water*.

It is a very good voice, observant, restrained and dry, and it is the right default for the game. But the atlas asks
that independent authors "produce genuinely different works, with different modes of adventure, voices, spatial
structures, and dramaturgical forms" (§12). The collection has a house style, not a range of voices. No work has:

- a comic narrator who times a joke;
- a lyrical one;
- a terse, violent one;
- a narrator who lies, or who has opinions;
- letters;
- verse that matters.

### Why it converged

1. **The grain of the language**, above: Kyr makes finding out easy, so finding out is what got written.
2. **Setting-first sources.** Three works adapt sourcebooks built from a world-law outward: attention, the stilling,
   knowing-as-weight. A law of the world makes a superb consequence engine, and it makes the law the protagonist.
3. **The collision check works on nouns.** Low Water's corpus comparison ([low-water/01-corpus-comparison.md](low-water/01-corpus-comparison.md))
   is careful and honest. It renamed Mother Cantor Orr, dropped the eels, turned the Tallymen into the Equals and the
   Council into the Hearing. It did not ask whether this was, again, an outsider reading a misread machine before a
   deadline. The collection checks furniture, not form.
4. **One critique, applied well.** Tessary's literary review and Low Water's critical reading have nearly the same
   headings:
   - the depth is in the design, not the texture;
   - the antagonist explains himself;
   - the reversal is not foreshadowed;
   - the danger is mild;
   - there are pulp lines;
   - the doubles should be rhymed.

   Each critique is excellent. Applied twice, the same critique makes the two works alike.
5. **"More" has one direction.** When a brief asks for ten times the ambition, and the language rewards depth of
   evidence, the author digs.

## 5. The negative space

### Forms

| Form | Present? | Nearest thing in the collection | What is missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mystery | Pervasive | All seven | A mystery with a *human* culprit who chose harm (the collection's answer is always "a system"; Low Water says so: "It is not a culprit") |
| Investigation of institutions | Strong | Tessary, Low Water, LOR Act I | — |
| Exploration and dungeon | Strong | All seven descend; LOR ascends | Exploration *for its own sake*: a cave or a coast with no machine at the bottom; and the README's own loop of going out, coming back, recovering and going out again, which no authored work is built around |
| Epic | Present in scale | Thrume, LOR, Patient Star | Epic as *human* war, seen close; LOR's war happens offstage |
| Political intrigue | Present as procedure | Councils, Concord, Board, Hearing | Intrigue as *personal*: allies who betray, secrets kept between two people, a court |
| Tragedy | As a menu item only | Sacrifice endings | Tragedy as *structure*: a work that narrows toward a cost the protagonist chose |
| Comedy | As texture only | Tessary's goose and queue; Low Water's cake and Quill | Comedy as *structure*: complication, mistaken identity, the reversal that makes things worse and funnier, the feast |
| Satire | None | The Scruples' forms, faintly | A society made visible by exaggerating its law |
| Romance | **None** | `married` appears nowhere in the Kyr; `wedding` three times; `love`, as a word, seven times in 12,500 lines, and never between two people the player meets | Desire, courtship, marriage, love that changes a plot |
| Domestic drama | Faint | The House's kitchen; Kezu and Tobu | A household as the whole world: meals, quarrels, children, the old |
| Picaresque | None | LOR's voyage; the Keeping's walk | A road of episodes; a rogue; the world seen by moving through it |
| Creative labour | **None** | Dace's map; singing the Seventh | Making a thing whose making is the play: a play, a bell, a feast, a book, a boat |
| Professional tradecraft | One | Low Water's Assessor | Other crafts: a con artist, a physician, an advocate, a player |
| Horror | Moments | Thrume's dream-houses; the Reflected | Dread sustained over days in a bounded place; a household that cannot trust itself |
| Philosophical dialogue | Generated only | The disputation on the green (`data/goings-on/disputation`); Halm; the Valuer | A work whose central activity is argument, with a companion of Kim Kitsuragi's depth (atlas §7) |
| Survival | Honest danger | OSR lethality in places | Scarcity over time (food, fuel, warmth) as the pressure |

### Registers

The collection has wonder, quiet dread, melancholy, civic wit, elegy and the uncanny, and it handles them with real
discipline. It is short of:

- **joy**, a scene in which things go right and people are glad;
- **hilarity**, as opposed to dryness;
- **desire**;
- **grief** as something lived through rather than an epilogue (`grief` appears three times in 12,500 lines, all in
  Low Water);
- **shame**;
- **anger** that the work takes the side of;
- **terror** that lasts;
- **tenderness** between equals.

### Kinds of agency

The protagonist reads, shows, operates mechanisms, fights, persuades with evidence, and chooses an ending. They never:

- **make** something;
- **lie**, as a craft;
- **love** someone;
- **mourn**;
- **endure**, as opposed to race a clock;
- **perform** or lead in public;
- **fail and carry on**, with the failure as the story;
- find they were **wrong about themselves**.

### Relationships

Present: employer and hired hand, in every work; a companion lent by an institution (Isaura, the Valuer, Tobu, Saro,
Teshen); siblings on opposite sides, as NPCs (Isaura and Dov); a parent seeking a child, as NPCs (Hesper and Col, Nan
and Davy); a mentor, usually old and usually a woman.

Absent: lovers; spouses; the protagonist's own family beyond a hook; a friend from childhood; a rival; an enemy with
history; children the protagonist is responsible for; an animal companion. Above all, absent: **relations between
other people played out in front of the player**. People in the collection relate to the protagonist, or to the
inheritance. Rarely to each other while you watch.

### Time and space

Every work runs on a deadline. None is clockless; none is a season, a cycle or a life. Every work but the House
descends, and the House ascends. Every work is an authored country. None is written to *use* the generated world:
its towns, roads and goings-on. Only the House is small enough to be held in the head.

### What Kyrie has not yet learned to express

The question the brief asks is not only which stories are missing, but what the collection has not taught the game to
*say*. Seven things:

1. **Wanting.** A protagonist, or a person, who wants something for themselves (money, a person, a place on a stage)
   and whose wanting drives the plot.
2. **Fault.** Being the cause; arriving late; doing the thing that cannot be undone and living in what follows.
   (`Grave` acts exist in Kyr now. No adventure has made the protagonist's own grave act its centre.)
3. **A person as an end.** Someone who matters to the story not for what they know or can open, but because the
   protagonist loves, owes, or is responsible for them.
4. **Making.** An activity whose result is a made thing, judged by people, that could have been made better or
   worse.
5. **Comedy as structure.** Complications that compound; identities mistaken; a reversal that is funnier than the
   situation before it; a feast at the end.
6. **Fear that lasts.** Days of it, in one place, where the danger is partly the people you are shut in with.
7. **The small that stays small.** A work whose stakes are one household, one marriage, one body, and which ends in
   the parish it began in.

And beneath all seven: **minds that change because of people.** Because they were shamed, comforted, flattered,
loved, worn out, made to laugh, or simply heard. Not because they were shown an exhibit.

## 6. The collection's literary identity

Taken together, Kyrie's adventures are a literature of **stewardship and inheritance**. They join Tolkien's long
memory of misread ruins to a steward's ethic of keeping, and to a civic seriousness about offices, ledgers, liturgies
and clerks that owes something to *Disco Elysium*'s interest in institutions. Their prose is the best thing about
them: plain, exact, dry and humane. They take ordinary work seriously: dyeing, eel-selling, lock-keeping, the
weighing of felt. Their antagonists have reasons. Their wonders are made for a reason and misused by successors, as
the atlas asks. Their best endings (the Release, the Accord, the Long Man's Merrow that never learns) earn their
quiet. Imbar is a real place in them. The world swears, weighs, keeps and forgets in a way that is already culture.

Their limit is the reverse of those strengths:

- a literature of the commons without the private life;
- institutions without intimacy;
- the past without the future;
- knowledge without desire;
- loss always redeemed by custom.

Its idea of depth is age, and its idea of action is finding out. A player who finished all seven would know a great
deal about how Imbar's communities live with what they inherited, and almost nothing about how its people love,
laugh, cheat, grieve or make things.

That is not a failure of the authors. It is what a young engine and a young language were best at, written very well
by people who read each other closely. But the next works have to find depth in **persons and present time**. If
they do not, Imbar will be a world of fine ruins kept by fine clerks.

## 7. A portfolio

Six proposals, each meant to teach the game something the collection has not. None has an ancient mechanism to
misread. None leaves the world. Only one has a mystery, and there the mystery is a household's. Names were checked
against `data/adventures`, `data/campaigns`, `data/origins` and `doc/adventures` for collisions. A commission should
run them again against the generator's name tables (Tessary W25 asks for that lint).

### 7.1 *The Third Night* (tragedy)

**In a sentence.** You come home to Wrayford because your mother wrote that your father went out on the road with the
cart and did not come home. You find him chained to the bridge rail with two other carters, by the reeve's order,
until the lord's captain comes. Your mother means to bury him before the third night, whatever it costs.

**Dramatic identity.** A tragedy in the strict sense. Everyone knows the facts by the end of the first hour, everyone
is partly right, and the question is not what happened but what the protagonist will do and who will pay for it.
Nothing found later changes the situation. There are only people to be moved, and every way of moving them costs
something.

**The situation.** Wrayford is a carters' town at a bridge on the drove road. This spring the lord, who lives
elsewhere, put a toll on the bridge, and the riders keep it: the generated world's own furniture (README, "the riders
keep the lord's toll"). Four days ago the carters rose and burned the toll-house. In the press, the toll-keeper's boy,
Ned Hobday, fourteen, went under a cart's wheel. The cart was Wat Pardoe's. The riders took Wat and two others on the
road and hanged them at the bridge under the lord's warrant.

Reeve Simon Hulme has kept the peace in Wrayford for twenty years. He is Maudie Pardoe's cousin and her son Kit's
godfather. He has decreed that the three stay on the bridge until Captain Lys Sefton comes with the lord's riders at
dusk on the third day. The bodies are his proof that the road is in hand. Finding order, the captain takes the toll
money and goes. Finding rebellion, the lord's law is ten hangings and the carters' row burnt. Whoever touches the
dead is a rebel.

**The pressures**, none of them supernatural:

- Maudie will not have Wat out a third night. In Wrayford the dead are watched three nights and buried on the third
  day, and one who is not "goes looking". The work never confirms or denies it.
- Kit, seventeen, wants to burn the reeve's house.
- Corbin, the sexton, has dug every grave in Wrayford for forty years. He will dig none while a Wrayford man hangs on
  the bridge. So Ned Hobday lies unburied too, in his mother's front room, in August.
- Jennet Hobday, the toll-keeper's widow, wants her son buried and Wat Pardoe left to the crows. She is the only
  person in the town whose claim on grief is as good as Maudie's.
- The heat. The crows. The town's temper, house by house.

**People.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Maudie Pardoe** | Fifty, practical, unsentimental; speaks in instructions. If nobody else does, she goes to the bridge herself on the third night. |
| **Kit Pardoe** | The brother. He loves you and resents your three years away. His arc runs from fire to grief, or to the gibbet. |
| **Simon Hulme** | The reeve. He is not a tyrant: tired, careful, honest about his reasons, afraid. Shame can move him; so can love for Kit, Jennet's grief, the town turning its backs on him at chapel, or exhaustion. Evidence cannot, because he has all the facts already. He asks the protagonist *What would you have me do?* and means it. |
| **Jennet Hobday** | The other mother, and the key to the ending that is not a synthesis. |
| **Corbin** | The sexton: dry, funny, immovable. The work's one comic voice. His strike is the town's conscience made physical. |
| **Lys Sefton** | The captain, who arrives at dusk on the third day. A professional who wants no trouble, and will make a great deal of it if she finds any. |
| **Hal Hulme** | The reeve's son and the protagonist's oldest friend, or more than that before they went away, if the player wishes. |

**What the player does.**

- Sits with their mother.
- Keeps the vigil: an act that passes hours and tells what passes in them.
- Goes to the bridge by day and by night.
- Washes and shrouds a body, and carries it (two can; one cannot).
- Digs.
- Talks Kit down, or goes with him.
- Pleads, bargains, shames.
- Goes to Jennet.
- Speaks at the chapel.
- Rides out to meet the captain on the road.
- Fights, if they must, and sees what fighting does. Two of the reeve's men were at school with Kit.

**Shape.** Three days and three nights in one place: the town, the bridge, the river, the burying ground, the drove
road.

- Day 1: the homecoming and the facts. Night 1: the first vigil.
- Day 2: positions harden, the heat rises, and Corbin's strike is felt. Night 2: the chance to take the body down,
  past watchers, by a moon.
- Day 3: the riders are sighted. Night 3: the deadline the town believes in.

Doing nothing produces tragedy by people's own reasons. If the protagonist does not act on the second night, Kit
does, and is taken. If they do not act on the third, Maudie goes alone.

**How it can end.** Not a menu: the work narrows toward these by what the player has already done.

- ***Earth on him.*** Wat is taken down by night and buried with Corbin's help. At dawn the reeve must arrest someone,
  and does. The captain finds a rebel already in irons and is satisfied. The price is someone's freedom: the
  protagonist's, or Kit's if he takes the blame.
- ***Too late.*** Moved by Hal, by Jennet or by shame, Hulme relents on the third evening and goes to cut the dead
  down himself. The captain's riders came early, and have Maudie on the bridge with her hands on the chain. By the
  lord's law that is a hanging. Hulme could have prevented it yesterday and cannot today. This ending comes only to a
  player who moved the reeve, but not soon enough.
- ***The two mothers.*** Reachable only by someone who has sat with both. Jennet and Maudie carry both their dead to
  the burying ground in daylight on the third morning, with the town behind them. Corbin digs two graves side by side.
  The reeve's men will not lay hands on the toll-keeper's widow. The captain arrives to a funeral, not a rising, takes
  the toll money and goes. Hulme resigns the reeveship that evening. Nobody is vindicated, and the toll stands. The
  town has done something neither the decree nor the rising imagined. This is the atlas's "more than winning, losing,
  or choosing one of two opposing ideologies" (§5): not a synthesis of two rights, but a new act that makes the
  conflict a different conflict.
- ***The fire.*** Kit and the carters rise again, with the protagonist. The reeve's house burns, the captain comes in
  the night, and the row burns. There are more on the bridge.
- ***Away.*** The protagonist leaves on the second day, and the work allows it. Coming back a season later, in a world
  that went on, they find the bridge, bones in a sack, and Maudie's grave, or not.

No coda of custom. Each ending's last lines are the morning after, told plainly, and then the work stops.

**Why it is different.** There is no inherited mechanism, no mystery, no outsider and no other plane. Nothing in it
is older than the protagonist's grandparents. The kin origin's own letter is why the protagonist is late, and the
lateness is theirs. Grief is the experience, not the epilogue. There are no exhibits, so minds change only through
people.

**What it asks of Kyr.**

- *General capabilities:*
  - questions put to the player (§8, G1): the reeve's *What would you have me do?* and Maudie's *Will you, or must
    I?*;
  - stances moved by who speaks and when (G2);
  - a register for the voice tables, so that a house in mourning does not greet a visitor with "A traveller! Sit, sit"
    (G9);
  - a body carried by two members (G13; weight now exists);
  - a public occasion for the chapel and the funeral (G10);
  - a module that claims an origin's hook (G12): the kin origin's missing drover becomes this chapter when a world and
    a player choose it.
- *Already in Kyr:* grave acts, sleep and perception for the night watchers, endings, kin who join as themselves.
- *Content:* everything else. The folk belief, the decree, the town's temper, the endings.
- *Scale:* the Long Man's: 700 to 1,000 lines, 25 to 40 places, about ten people.

### 7.2 *Gosse's Company* (picaresque comedy of creative labour)

**In a sentence.** You join a company of travelling players for what its master says is its last summer, on a circuit
of nine towns. The season is the adventure: what to play where, who plays whom, how to eat, and whether to put on the
new play that the Lady of Cawl will pay them not to.

**Dramatic identity.** Comedy, with the melancholy that every good comedy about the theatre has. Picaresque in shape:
episodic, with the road as its spine. An ensemble. Making as the central activity. No catastrophe on a clock; at the
end, a season's accounts.

**People.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Aldo Gosse** | Actor-manager, sixty-three; plays kings and owes everyone. A magnificent and loving liar. |
| **Lettice Quennell** | Leading lady, fifty. She has played the girl who waited for thirty years, and knows to the day how old she is. |
| **Rafe Mabb** | The playwright, sober until noon, writing *The Toll*, a play about a thing that really happened. |
| **Faro and Floss** | Twins: the servants and the clowns. They quarrel in rhyme. |
| **Linden** | Fifteen; plays the girls. His voice is going this summer. |
| **Dignity** | The mule, and the only one in the company paid on time. |
| **The Lady of Cawl** | Who has read *The Toll*, and will buy every copy of it. |

**What the player does.** In each town:

- **Read it.** Who is here, what is their trouble, what will they pay for? The generated towns and their goings-on (a
  fever, a quarrel, a drover's son gone after his father) are the audiences.
- **Choose a play.** The repertoire is five authored plays with parts and scenes. Some of them are Imbar's own stories
  as the theatre tells them: *The Giant King of Merrow* is the barrow legend that *The Long Man* overturns, played
  straight to people who believe it.
- **Cast it.** Who plays whom, given who is sick, drunk, quarrelling, too old, or losing his voice.
- **Rehearse.** Scenes between the players, where the ensemble's relationships live.
- **Perform.** A show runs in beats, and things happen in it: an actor dries, a heckler, rain, the steward walks out.
  The player prompts, improvises, cuts and stretches.
- **Take the hat,** and eat, or don't.

Between towns: roads, bandits who want a show, a fire in a barn theatre, the Lady's agent. The protagonist can learn
to act, keep the books, or write.

**Shape.** A season of nine stops. Three are authored set-pieces: a fair, a lord's hall, and the closing night at
Cawl. The rest are generated towns. Mabb's play is the through-line: finished by the sixth stop, bought by the Lady
at the seventh, performed or burned on the closing night.

**How it can end.** A season's account rather than a menu:

- the company solvent, or broken up;
- Lettice's farewell played, or not;
- Linden's first man's part;
- *The Toll* performed, and the company banned from Cawl's country while the play goes on without them in copies;
  or *The Toll* sold and burned;
- Gosse dying on the road, and the company going on under a new master, possibly the protagonist.

**Why it is different.** Comedy as structure. Making as play. An ensemble of voices that relate to each other. Joy.
It is the first work written to use the generated world rather than sit beyond its march. And through the repertoire,
the rest of the collection becomes Imbar's art, which is coherence by quotation rather than by repetition.

**What it asks of Kyr and Deem.**

- *General capabilities:*
  - an ensemble companion model (G5): six companions who keep their voices, have relations among themselves, quarrel
    and leave;
  - assigning members to parts (G6);
  - time that skips between towns, with interruptions (G7);
  - a performance as a public occasion with beats and an audience whose temper moves (G10);
  - above all, a module that places itself into generated towns and reads their goings-on (G12; atlas §13, §18).
    This is the hardest requirement in the portfolio and the most valuable.
- *Deem:* towns with audiences; a fair; local history to put on stage. The disputation already imports
  `@history.makers`, which is the pattern to follow.
- *Presentation:* casting is a screen, and a performance wants its own mode (atlas §23, "different representations").
- *Content:* the plays, the songs, the company's voices.
- *Scale:* large; 1,500 to 2,500 lines, plus generation.

### 7.3 *Pellam Fair* (rogue's comedy, tradecraft of deception)

**In a sentence.** For the three days of Pellam's hiring fair, you and your partner Ursel Cobb mean to sell a
wool-merchant a flock of sheep that does not exist. It goes wrong the way good plans do, one complication at a time.

**Dramatic identity.** Farce and caper; a rogue's picaresque. The protagonist wants money and has no virtue they
would admit to. Complications accrue, which is the reverse of the collection's revelations. The pleasure is building a
lie and keeping it standing.

**People.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ursel Cobb** | The partner and the brains, sentimental about exactly one thing. |
| **Master Harker** | The mark: a wool-merchant who runs a fraud of his own, hiring servants for a year at the fair and selling their indentures south. |
| **Thessaly Harker** | His daughter, with whom someone in the partnership falls in love, inconveniently. |
| **Justice Broom** | Of the pie-powder court, which tries offenders on the fairground within the day. A connoisseur of lies. |
| **Absolute** | A dancing bear, not tame. |
| | Also: a pardoner selling Saint Evenhand's weights, and the real shepherd whose flock you are pretending to own. |

**What the player does.**

- **Plan.** Choose the con: the flock, a forged letter, a borrowed name.
- **Prepare.** Props, accomplices, a coat, a seal.
- **Lie to particular people.** Each lie is a claim that person now believes, acts on and repeats.
- **Watch lies travel,** and improvise when two of them meet.
- **Get caught,** and talk your way out in front of Justice Broom.
- **Or turn the con on Harker,** for his servants' sake. The work makes this costly, not compulsory.

**Shape.** Three days on a fair's timetable: hiring at dawn, horses at noon, the bear at dusk, the court at four. A
few fixed people and a large crowd.

**How it can end.** The score, and away. Ursel's double-cross. The pillory. The honest turn: Harker's servants freed,
and no money. A marriage, which is comedy's own ending. The bear.

**Why it is different.** A protagonist with appetite. Deception as a craft. A comedy that escalates. Low life seen
from inside it. And the editorial rule "never state what the game cannot vouch for" has to live beside a world full of
false belief.

**What it asks of Kyr.**

- *General capabilities:*
  - **belief apart from knowledge (G3).** The player can tell a person something false; that person holds it, acts on
    it and repeats it; people infer; lies collide when two people compare stories. In Kyr today, what is known is
    learned and is true ("What is known is learned, not set"). The disputation's `believes`, `conviction` and
    `refutes` are the seed. This is the largest kernel change in the portfolio, and after G1 and G2 the most generally
    useful: every intrigue, spy and unreliable witness needs it;
  - who someone is to each viewer (G4). `Seen when` exists; a person *seen as* someone else by some viewers does not;
  - gossip that travels at its own pace (G8).
- *Presentation:* comic timing. The narrator tells "at most three lines for one command, the most important first",
  which puts the punchline before the setup. Farce needs authored order kept (G15).
- *Content:* the cons, the jokes, Broom's judgments.
- *Scale:* medium; 1,200 to 1,800 lines.

### 7.4 *The Banns at Hessel* (comedy of manners, romance, domestic life)

**In a sentence.** Your cousin Ismay Fennel is to marry Rob Wenlock at Hessel on Saturday, and the banns have been
read twice. You have come home for the week to help. By Tuesday it is plain that nobody involved wants this wedding,
for four different reasons, and that nobody can say so.

**Dramatic identity.** A warm domestic comedy and a romance: an ensemble like Austen's, on farms like Hardy's but
without Hardy's fate. The stakes are who marries whom, and whether two families end the week still speaking. The
danger is social. The adventure is a household and a week.

**People.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ismay Fennel** | The bride: sensible, and in love with someone she will not name. |
| **Rob Wenlock** | The groom: kind, frightened of his mother, and in love with the idea of the drove road. |
| **Mopsa Wenlock** | Rob's sister; sharp and funny. |
| **Old Dumbrell** | Ismay's grandmother. She arranged the match for the sake of the Long Orchard. She is dying and knows it. |
| **Merriman** | The cook hired from town; a genius with a temper, and a past with Dumbrell. |
| | Also: the two mothers; the curate who reads the banns; the bull. |

**What the player does.**

- **Makes the feast.** Creative labour: ingredients fetched from the market, from the fair, from a neighbour who owes
  a favour, and a cake that can fail.
- **Carries messages,** listens at doors, keeps and tells secrets.
- **Makes and breaks matches.**
- **Is in church for the third reading of the banns,** which has a moment for "if any person knows".
- **Dances on Friday.** Who dances with whom changes things.
- **Courts, if they wish.** Invitations, refusals, shared tasks, at a pace both sides set. It can fail gracefully.
- **Walks to the hill farm one night** for Dumbrell's mother's ring: the week's one small expedition.

**Shape.** Seven days, Sunday to Saturday, with the farm's daily round, and three set occasions: the banns on Sunday,
the dance on Friday, the wedding on Saturday.

**How it can end.**

- The wedding as planned, happy or not.
- A different wedding: Ismay to the one she loves, Rob to the road.
- Two weddings, one of them the protagonist's.
- No wedding, and a feast anyway.
- Dumbrell dies on the Thursday and everything changes: a comedy that holds a death.

**Why it is different.** Romance. Domestic life at the precision the atlas asks for ordinary life (§3). Joy and
tenderness. The protagonist is family. The only mystery is the oldest one.

**What it asks of Kyr.**

- *General capabilities:*
  - questions put to the player (G1), and minds moved by relationship (G2), between other people as well as with the
    protagonist: who loves whom must change during the week, through what the player does or tells;
  - other people's relations to each other (G5);
  - greetings that remember (Tessary W18, `Greets again`);
  - occasions for the banns, the dance and the wedding (G10);
  - gossip (G8);
  - roles that change, so the bride becomes a wife (G4).
- *A warning:* romance must not become an affection meter. The atlas names "personality table plus affection score"
  as the thing not to build (§7). Romance here is specific shared history: *danced with*, *kept her secret*,
  *laughed at the bull*. Each is a fact a person's words can read, and none is a number on the screen.
- *Content:* everything warm.
- *Scale:* medium; 1,000 to 1,500 lines.

### 7.5 *The Winter at Scarth* (intimate horror, survival)

**In a sentence.** The snow closes the fell road with you inside the Brannagh steading for the winter: a farmer, his
wife, his mother, three children, a hired man, and a lodger who came up the road the week before you did. The sheep
begin to go one by one, and the household begins to watch each other.

**Dramatic identity.** Horror sustained over weeks, in one house: the dread of scarcity and of one another, and an
uncanny that is never confirmed. Ambiguity can be kept to the end if the player wants it kept. It is not lurid. The
horror is what hungry people did last winter, and whether they will do it again.

**People.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Wennick Brannagh** | The farmer: proud and guilty. |
| **Ancret Brannagh** | His second wife, from the valley. The household thinks her unlucky. |
| **Ma Brannagh** | His mother. She tells the children about the Hungry Guest, who sits at the table in a hard winter and must be fed first. |
| **Sim, Ada and the baby** | The children. |
| **Tey** | The hired man: deaf in one ear, gentle, and the obvious suspect. |
| **Mister Fairweather** | The lodger: a pedlar who pays in coin nobody in the valley has seen, and is very good with the children. |
| | Out on the fell, a real animal, a glutton, taking sheep. |

**What happened.** There is one reading the work supports, and it keeps others open. Last winter the Brannaghs turned
a family away from the door in a blizzard to save their own food, and that family died in the snow below the shieling.
Each night Ma lays a place for the Guest. Someone in the house is now taking food and leaving it at the shieling.
Fairweather may be that dead family's son, or merely a pedlar. The work keeps "merely" alive.

**What the player does.**

- **Works:** fodder, peat, the byre, snow to clear.
- **Rations:** food and fuel are stocks that run down.
- **Keeps watch at night:** who is awake, who hears what, by whose light.
- **Searches:** the loft, the byre, and the shieling when the weather allows.
- **Talks:** confession, accusation, comfort.
- **Tracks in snow,** and fights the glutton.
- **Keeps the children safe.**
- **Leaves, if they dare.** The fell in winter kills, with an OSR's honesty.

**Shape.** From the first snow to the thaw, about six weeks. Uneventful days pass fast and the nights are slow. Food,
fuel, health and trust move day by day.

**How it can end.**

- The thaw, with everyone alive, and the truth told or kept.
- Fairweather driven out into the snow, and what that makes of you.
- The household breaking: Ancret takes the children down the valley at the thaw.
- The protagonist dead on the fell.
- The Guest fed, and nothing explained.

**Why it is different.** Horror and survival in the smallest space the collection has attempted. Endurance instead of
a deadline. Suspicion as a state of mind. A household as the whole world. A protagonist who depends on the people they
suspect.

**What it asks of Kyr.**

- *General capabilities:*
  - perception by sound as well as sight: in the dark, through a wall, half-asleep (G14; Patient Star ledger 4).
    `perceives` and `asleep` exist now; sound does not;
  - **suspicion apart from knowledge** in the journal (G3, in a light form). The atlas distinguishes what the
    protagonist knows from what they "infer or suspect" (§14), and the journal only knows the first;
  - time that skips calm days and slows at night (G7);
  - watches assigned to members (G6).
- *Presentation:* the page must be able to withhold. The journal must not resolve what the work leaves unresolved,
  and a result must not name who did something the company did not see (G15). Editorial rule 2 helps here.
- *Deem:* weather, snow, the fell.
- *Content:* everything else. Food already exists; fuel is a measure.
- *Scale:* small to medium; 900 to 1,300 lines, about twelve places.

### 7.6 *The Road to Orm* (philosophical two-hander)

**In a sentence.** Your order sends you to walk Magister Edrin Callow, a teacher of the Flow, twelve days to the Synod
at Orm, where he will recant or be silenced. He is ill, he is funny, he wants to argue, and by the third day you are no
longer sure which of you is being escorted.

**Dramatic identity.** A philosophical journey, with dialogue as the central action. A companion as deep as the
atlas's Kim Kitsuragi (§7), whose relation to the protagonist is custody rather than partnership. The philosophy is
real. The Flow is already Imbar's: the disputation on the green has its philosopher teaching that nothing stays.
Callow argues it to its consequences. If nothing stays, who is bound by a vow made by a former self? Can a city owe?
That is a conversation *Tessary* and *Low Water* prepared without knowing they were preparing it. It is not a
lecture: an argument the protagonist can win, lose, refuse, or be changed by.

**People.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Edrin Callow** | Sixty, consumptive, vain, generous, sometimes wrong, and a mimic. |
| **Brother Tobit** | The second escort, sent by the Synod's prosecutor with private instructions. |
| **Gerda Callow** | His sister, at whose farm they spend a night. |
| **His former students** | Met on the road: one wants to rescue him, one to denounce him. |
| | Also: a ferryman at the Wend who will not carry a heretic, and a village whose well-dressing festival is the One made into a custom. |

**What the player does.**

- **Walks.** Weather, weariness, the ferry, a pass over the fell.
- **Talks.** This is the core. Positions the protagonist takes are remembered and turned: *You said on the first day
  that a promise binds the one who made it. You are not that one now.*
- **Cares for him.** Medicine, rest, carrying.
- **Decides whether to let him go.** He can slip away at night, and Tobit can arrange an accident.
- **Arrives.**

**Shape.** Twelve days and eight stations. Each day has a thread of conversation, and a road event that tests it.

**How it can end.** One place, many inner outcomes:

- delivered, he recants, and asks you to carry the book he did not say aloud;
- delivered, he refuses, and you testify at the Synod;
- you let him go at the Wend;
- he dies at Gerda's;
- you find that you hold the Flow yourself, and do not go back to your order.

**Why it is different.** A two-hander. Conversation as thought. The protagonist's own convictions are the stakes and
are what changes. A journey with no dungeon. The only proposal whose subject is ideas taken seriously.

**What it asks of Kyr.**

- *General capabilities:* the most demanding conversational ones:
  - positions the protagonist takes, held as facts other people can read (G2). This extends the disputation's
    `believes`, `conviction` and `refutes` to the player;
  - topics that open from earlier answers (G1);
  - a companion who acts alone (G5);
  - a journey with interruptions (G7);
  - the Synod as an occasion (G10).
- *Content:* the arguments, which are the hardest writing in the portfolio. The commission needs an author who can do
  philosophy in dialogue.
- *Scale:* medium; 1,200 to 1,800 lines, much of it what people say.

### Two more, kept in reserve

- ***The Long Swallet*** (exploration for its own sake). A cave system explored from a village inn over many
  expeditions: go down, come back, recover, map, go deeper. At the bottom there is no secret machine, only more cave,
  beauty, and a rival party. It is the README's own loop, which no authored work is built around. It is set aside only
  because the collection already has seven descents. The form it would teach, return as a structure, *Gosse's Company*
  teaches too.
- ***The Embassy*** (satire). An embassy to a principality governed entirely by precedence: satire through a social
  law the player must learn. This plays to Kyr's strength, a law of the world, made social. It is set aside because
  satire is easier once Kyrie can do comedy at all.

**What stays authored content.** The plays, the arguments, the jokes, the false beliefs, the recipes, the endings,

**Deem.** The Players and the Fair need generated towns and crowds as audiences, which is placement in both
directions, as the atlas describes it (§13). The Winter needs weather. Orm needs a route. The name-collision lint
should run against the generator's name tables as well as other modules.

**The page.**

- A vigil: a way to let time pass *with someone* that tells what passes, so that "Wait › A while: Time passes" is not
  the only way.
- A casting screen.
- A performance mode.
- No visible numbers for regard.
- Comic and suspense timing kept in authored order.

## 9. Recommendation: commission *The Third Night* next

**Why this one.**

1. **It answers the collection's deepest absence with the sharpest instrument.** The collection consoles; tragedy
   does not. The collection discovers; tragedy has nothing to discover. The protagonist is innocent; here they are
   late, by their own letter. It breaks the template on every axis at once: inheritance, mystery, outsider, cosmos,
   coda. A cold player would know within a page that this is a different kind of Kyrie.
2. **It is small.** It is the Long Man's size: three days, one town, about ten people. The Long Man was the
   collection's cleanest success, and this can be written, played and judged in the time the larger works spent on a
   second pass.
3. **It forces the capability every later proposal needs, at the lowest cost.** Every proposal in §7 wants G1 and G2:
   questions put to the player, and minds moved by people. *The Third Night* wants little else, because it has no
   exhibits to fall back on. If Kyr can make Simon Hulme change his mind for reasons that are not evidence, the
   Banns, Orm and every later companion are within reach. If it cannot, it is better to learn that on a thousand
   lines than on three thousand.
4. **It completes something already in the game.** The kin origin opens on a mother's letter: "Your father went out
   on the road with the cart and did not come home." Today that hook leads into a generated country. *The Third
   Night* would be the first adventure to claim an origin's hook (G12), and the hook would turn out to have been a
   tragedy from its first sentence.
5. **It is a subject the project's own design conversation named.** The atlas records *Antigone* alongside the
   *Oresteia* and the problem of demobilization (§6). *What Tessary Owes* took demobilization. *Antigone*'s question
   (the law of the city against the duty to the dead, kin against order) is still open in Imbar, and the work is
   free to answer it differently than Sophocles did.

**Its risks, and the guards against them.**

- *Grimness.* Corbin is the guard: the tragedy must have its comic voice, as Shakespeare's do, and the town must have
  ordinary life going on around the bridge. The atlas's "not portentous or lurid by default" applies. The bodies are
  under sacking the reeve allowed. The prose never lingers.
- *A two-rights formula.* It would be easy to write Hegel's *Antigone*. The two mothers' ending is the guard: the
  best outcome is an act neither side imagined, reached only by sitting with both.
- *A vote in disguise.* The town's temper must not become a credence counter fed by exhibits. It should move with
  acts: who went to which funeral, who would not look at whom in chapel.

**What the commission should require.**

- No inherited mechanism, no other plane, nothing older than living memory.
- No discovery after the first day that changes the situation.
- Minds that change without exhibits, and at least one person (the reeve) who asks the protagonist a question and acts
  on the answer.
- Doing nothing produces a tragedy, through agents acting on their own reasons, on the second night or the third.
- The two mothers' ending, reachable only through relationship.
- One comic voice.
- No coda of custom. The morning after, then stop.

**What to build first.** G1 (a topic that takes an answer) and G9 (a register for mourning), and a vigil on the page.
These are small, and the work cannot be good without them. G12, binding to the kin origin, can follow. The work
should first be playable on its own as a campaign, as every other adventure was.

**After it.**

1. ***Gosse's Company.*** The necessary comedy, and the necessary test of writing *into* the generated world. It is
   large, so begin its kernel work (G5, G6, G10) while *The Third Night* is being written.
2. ***The Road to Orm***, once G1 and G2 have been proved on the tragedy.
3. ***The Banns at Hessel*** and ***The Winter at Scarth***, at any time. Each needs only modest help from the kernel,
   and each fills a register the collection has never had (desire, and lasting fear).
4. ***Pellam Fair***, when belief apart from knowledge (G3) has been designed deliberately. It is the most valuable
   kernel change in the portfolio and should not be improvised for one adventure.

**What success looks like.** A cold player plays it once, and afterwards cannot stop thinking about what they did on
the second night. The transcript shows nothing discovered after the first hour, and everything changed.
