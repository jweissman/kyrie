# The Going Bank: the story first

*Written before any Kyr. Where play changed it, friction.md says how.*

## The place

The Gape is a gorge four hundred paces across and as deep as a church is tall, with a brown river at the bottom
that the locals call the Grudge. On the west lip stands Tarrow Lip. On the east lip, across a woven bridge, stands
Ketter. The bridge is the Plait: grass-rope, hand-spliced, laid out every spring by two crews who start from
opposite sides and meet in the middle. The meeting is the Knotting. Whoever ties the last knot has the year's luck,
and this year it is also a wedding: Tamsy Rudd of Ketter and Fell Anstey of the Lip are to be married on the Join,
with both villages standing on the span, and the Plait singing under them.

This year the crews did not quite meet. The west rope came out nine paces short. 'Frost,' says everyone in Tarrow
Lip. 'The lip heaves.' It has heaved nine paces in a season.

The east bank is going. It is not a landslip. The east bank is the back of something so large and so slow that
Ketter has built a village on it, and a church, and sown barley, and called it a hill. Every ninety years or so it
walks to the sea. It has started. The Gape is the place where its shoulder came away from the world.

## People

**Sabra Yarrow**, Master Plaiter of Tarrow Lip, sixty-three, forty-one springs on the Plait. Dry, quick, hands like
roots. She has known since her fifth spring. She keeps a knotted cord in her apron: one knot for each year the
Plait came out a pace long on the west side. Ten, twelve, fifty paces over forty years. She has told nobody
because the Lip lives on the Plait's tolls, and because 'a thing that cannot be mended should be mourned
quietly, not by committee.' *Lie she tells herself:* that she is sparing them. *What she does when pressed:* cuts
the cord, and gives you the cord.

**Pipit Anstey**, ten, Fell's cousin, the Lip's ropewalker. Crosses the guide-line on bare feet with a pail of milk
for the Ketter cheesewife and has never spilled. Talks to anybody. Is not afraid of the gap; is afraid that the
wedding will be cancelled, because she is to carry the knot-cord.

**Bettony Halloway**, fifty, kite-flier. Built a man-lifting kite from sailcloth and bean-poles after her husband
drowned in the Grudge, and has crossed the Gape on it eleven times. Charges a silver piece and weighs you first.
'I'm not saying it won't kill you. I'm saying I'll tell you which way.' Needs a west wind, which comes at dusk.

**Osbel Rudd**, reeve of Ketter, bride's father, forty. Has noticed that the church bell is out of true, that the
well rope is longer each spring, that his eldest barley terrace now runs downhill the wrong way. Has told himself
it is the wet. If told the truth he is angry first, then he is the best man in the district: he counts the village
in the churchyard, by name, aloud, to see how many can walk.

**Meggan Lorrimer**, eighty-four, blind, Ketter's drummer, who keeps a hollow stone on the high ground and strikes it at
dawn and dusk. She has always known. Her grandmother taught her the drum-answer: the bank, struck, answers back,
and you can tell by the answer how it feels. 'It is not cross. It is going somewhere. It has not been told it has
passengers.' She has never seen the sea. She would like to be present.

**Magne Varnish**, pinwright, hired by the Lip's council to "pin the east bank": drive nine iron spikes the length
of a man into the Ketter shelf and chain them back to the west, so that Tarrow Lip's toll stays crossable. He has
done it to a bridge-abutment in the Tamrin hills and it held. He is not wicked. He is thorough, and he has a
small boy in the Lip who has never been ill. *Surprise:* he is the only person in the story who sees the Walker
breathe, when he is lowered to drive the first pin, and he comes up changed.

## Scenes

**The lip.** The road comes up through gorse and ends at a windlass, a toll-board and a ragged half-bridge going out
over nothing. 'You'll want the other side,' says Pipit, who is sitting on the toll-board eating a pear, 'everybody
does. It's nine paces short. Don't tell Mother Yarrow I said nine, she says eight.'

**The loft.** The plaiters' loft smells of green grass-rope and goat. There are knot-boards on the walls: each
spring's Knotting, each knot different. The newest has a gap. Sabra Yarrow splices without looking. 'Ask what you
want to ask,' she says. 'I'll answer what I've a mind to.'

**The Long Pull.** The inn. The wedding supper is being argued over by Fell Anstey, the groom, who is afraid, and
his mother, who is afraid of him being afraid. They wish the company to witness it, and would like a song.

**The gap.** Nine paces of air. The Plait ends in frayed grass; the guide-line goes across, a single rope at foot
height with a second at hip. Pipit does it with the pail. You may do it, on the line. The wind plays the rope like a
string. By the second day the gap is thirty paces and the guide-line is all there is. By the third dusk, none.

**The kite.** Bettony's workshop is a barn with the roof taken off. She weighs you on a hay-scale and writes it on
the wall in chalk. The kite lifts one person at a time off the west lip at dusk. It will not carry the old,
who are heavy of bone, nor the infants who must be held in arms, so it is no way to empty a village. It is a way for one
person with something to say to arrive.

**The Seam.** A ladder goes down the west wall to a ledge where the Grudge runs. Across the river the east wall is
not rock. It is a hide: grey-brown, ribbed, pocked with limpets of lichen, and every little while it moves, a
long indrawn breath, and a drip of warm water falls from the overhang. Stand there with a lamp, and the wall
shifts one thumb's width, left to right, in the time it takes to say a prayer.

**Ketter.** A village the shape of a village that has been pulled slightly out of true. The sundial points
at the wrong hour. The church leans towards the sea, politely. The cheesewife's cheeses roll to the east. 'It's the
wet,' says everyone. A line of old cairns runs across the green, one per generation; the oldest is out at the
edge of the east cliff, where the Gape is now; the newest is by the well. Each cairn was set on the line from the
church door to the first bridge-post, and each is now a little east of it, as if the line had been drawn on a
cloth and the cloth pulled.

**The drum-stone.** Meggan sits at it with a bone drumstick. 'Dusk,' she says. 'Listen.' She strikes, and the
whole ridge answers, a long note from under your feet, and the thing under it turns a little, as a sleeper does
when called by name.

**The Knotting.** Third dusk. Both villages come out on the Plait. The west crew work to the end of what they have,
and the east crew to theirs, and between them are thirty paces, and a plank walk of lashed poles that Pipit and the
Ketter children have run across all afternoon. Everybody knows it is wrong. Everybody sings anyway. Tamsy and Fell
stand at the join with the knot-cord. The company may stand at the join, and say what they know, to everyone at
once. This is the hinge. Or say it to Osbel afterward, or to nobody; or let Varnish strike the first pin at the
very hour of the wedding, which is when he is paid to.

**Dawn.** The Plait parts at first light. Whatever has been done, it is done.

## What the company can do

1. **Learn it.** From Sabra's cord; from the cairn-line (the ranger's trade); from the Seam with a lamp, where the
   bank breathes; from Meggan. Four independent proofs; any two make the truth believable.
2. **Cross.** The Plait, then the guide-line (Dex), then the kite at dusk. Each is a different risk and a
   different thing to carry.
3. **Stop Varnish, or help him.** He is not a villain to be fought. He can be persuaded: let him go down on
   the rope himself and he'll see. Or you can pull the first pin-iron (Str), or take him a better answer. Or let him
   drive the pins, and the Gape groans, and the bank, which had been going gently, goes fast.
4. **Tell the reeve.** Alone, quietly: he is angry. With proof: he counts the village. At the Knotting: he stands on
   the Plait in front of everyone and asks Ketter, one by one, whether they will stay on the hill.
5. **Answer the drum.** At dusk on the third day Meggan will let the company strike the stone; a good answer eases the
   walk, so the going is slow enough for old people to be carried.

## Endings

- **The Harbour at Ketter.** Ketter chooses to ride. The Walker is told, by drum, that it has passengers; goes
  slowly. Six weeks later the bank touches salt water at a place called Wend Strand. A village has arrived at the
  sea. Meggan puts a foot in it. Tarrow Lip, with nine paces become nine miles, sends them rope.
- **Across the Gap.** Ketter crosses, as many as the kite and the guide-line and the carrying-chair can bring. The
  empty bank walks alone toward the sea; the Plait has been cut cleanly by Sabra Yarrow, not parted. The church bell
  is heard at the Lip in the night, going away.
- **Iron in the Shoulder.** The pins were driven. The bank, pinned, tears itself on the iron. The east village's
  well goes dry in a week; it takes eight days to carry Ketter out on foot, and the Lip's toll is worth nothing: there
  is nothing to cross to. Varnish is not seen again.
- **Neither Bank.** Nobody has decided by dawn. The Plait parts under whoever is on it. The ending says who.

## Tone

Mostly sunny. A wedding, a kite, a ten-year-old with a pail on a rope. The dread is in the details: a cheese rolling
uphill the wrong way, a cairn at the cliff edge. One grave scene (the churchyard count). One comic scene (weighing,
in the barn, by chalk).

## Connections

Meggan's grandmother's drum-answer is the same kind of calling that the Brynach drove sings; Ysolt Marrick in
Gosse's Company is Sabra's cousin by letter. Wend Strand is on the coast beyond the Rì's south. The Collegium of
Measures has a standing offer for any surveyor of a moving country; Varnish carries its pinwrights' licence.
