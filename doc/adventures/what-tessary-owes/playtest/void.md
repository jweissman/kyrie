**Ask Nan about Davy** — ‘He came home on the Tuesday with the rest of them,’ says Nan, ‘and he sat on that step out there for a week. Didn’t say much. Ate what I put down. I had to tell him every night of his life to put his boots together and he never once did it, and he set them on the step like a gentleman’s valet. They taught him that.’ She bites the cord. ‘Then he walked into the sea.’

**Ask Nan about the recruiting bill on her shelf** — ‘LAND AND A PENSION FOR EVERY MAN WHO COMES HOME,’ says Nan, without looking at it. ‘They cried that in the Withies with a drum. **To Scriveners' Row** — The Lettered Quarter smells of wet pulp and lamp-black. **Into the Exchequer** — A narrow room at the top of a narrow house, all ledgers. The strongboxes along the wall stand open, and are empty, and have been for some time; dust lies inside them. A cot behind a screen. A bottle. Josiah Quire, a Treasurer of Tessary, is here.

**Read a list in the Treasurer's hand, under a paperweight** — Treasurer Quire keeps a list, in his own hand, of every veteran called since the summer, a hundred and thirty-eight names, each with an address and a sum of money sent. The Exchequer knows the vanished are not deserters.

**Ask the Treasurer about his list of names** — Quire looks at the list for a long time. ‘A hundred and thirty-eight,’ he says. ‘I send their mothers something every month. We went down under the sea, six of us, and they showed us a thing like a ledger the size of a ship, and an old man who could not lie read us the terms, and we signed, because we were going to lose the war, and we won.’ His hands are shaking. ‘The Muster’s oaths. As collateral. I put my name to eight thousand men like a farmer putting up his cows.’

**Ask the Treasurer what the city must do to pay** — ‘Keep a promise,’ says Quire. ‘Of equal weight. Their words.’ He laughs, horribly. ‘Do you know what weighs as much as a king’s ancestral oath, in their scales? A promise of a whole people to a whole people. And there is exactly one of those in the city’s books, and I have it by heart: land and a pension for every man who comes home. It would cost two hundred thousand marks. The treasury has nothing. The Board will vote no on the thirteenth day, because there is no money, and on the fifteenth the note falls due, and then...’ He doesn’t finish. **Pay Pounce six coin for his old draft** — Pounce goes to a chest and comes back with six pages folded small, and counts your coins before he lets go of them. ‘You did not get these from me,’ he says. ‘You got them from a man you met in a tavern who resembled me.’ Ivo gains 1 Pounce’s draft of the War Note.

**Read Pounce's draft of the War Note** — You read it through twice. The city’s half is plain Tessarine legal hand: the city of Tessary, as heir to the Aenean emperors in the matter of oaths, borrows the calling of an oath sworn by the house of Hethra, &c. And at the Feast of Settlement in the twelfth year, the principal, to be paid in a kept promise of equal weight. The Treasurer’s signature. Five councillors’. And a sixth mark that is not a signature at all, only a set of ruled lines. Ivo gains 300 experience: read the terms of the War Note.

## night1

**Wait › An hour** — They come down through the Withies a little after two, twelve of them, in step, though nobody is calling the step. Barefoot. Their eyes are open. At the top of the Watersteps each of them stops, and stoops, and sets his boots side by side on the stone, toes to the water, and straightens, and walks down into the sea. The water is very low. They walk out over the sand toward the walls in the basin, getting smaller, keeping step, until the dark has them. It is a long time since anyone slept. The company is weary, and will fight the worse for it until it sleeps.

**Ask the Valuer where the soldiers you saw walk into the sea have gone** — The Valuer considers whether this is the affair of a debtor, and decides, visibly, that what you saw with your own eyes is your own affair. ‘Into the Holding,’ they say, ‘where pledged things are kept. They are not harmed. They are held. They stand asleep with the rest of what has been pledged and forfeit, and they do not age.’ A pause. ‘You are distressed. I am sorry. It is a lawful collection.’ Ivo gains 250 experience: found out where the called go.

**Tell the Valuer that the city promised its soldiers land and a pension before it pledged them** — The Valuer stops writing. ‘The recruiting bill,’ they say. ‘Eleven years ago. The pledge was twelve.’ They are silent for a while. ‘No. The bill is eleven years old; the Muster was raised eleven years ago; the note was drawn when the war was a year old. The promise to the soldiers was made first.’ They look at the ledger as if it had said something rude. ‘A prior promise, of the same weight, to the same persons, unkept. And the city pledged those persons to us as if they were its own.’ A long pause. ‘That is not on my inventory. I find I cannot stop thinking about it.’

**Ask the Valuer to come with you to the Hall of Oaths** — ‘The Hall of Oaths,’ says the Valuer. ‘The way down.’ They are quiet, and you have the impression of a calculation being done. ‘I am to return below before the Feast in any case, to report. If you are going that way, I will take you through the wall, and note what you do on the other side of it.’ They hold out a hand, ruled like a page. ‘I must ask you to promise not to lie to me. It is a small thing, for you. You will find it is not small below.’

**Ask the Ninth Valuer to join the company** — ‘Our inquiries coincide,’ says the Valuer, and closes the ledger on a ribbon. ‘I will accompany you. I will note anything you do that bears on the collateral.’

## below

**To the head of the Swatch Stair** — At the west end of the Tidewall the wall-walk breaks off at a stair that goes straight down the face of the wall into the basin. A chain hangs beside it for a handrail. Below, the Swatch: the one deep channel through which the outer basin fills and empties twice a day, with a noise like a slow mill. The basin is out. Below the stair lies a city of wet stone: roofs, a colonnade, the dome of something, streets with weed lying flat in them like combed hair. Figures are moving down there, small, bent over, looking for things.

**To the foot of the Swatch Stair** — The stair comes down the face of the Tidewall into a gully of wet stone and kelp. The noise of the Swatch is very loud here: the basin draining past you through the one deep channel, brown and quick. Ahead, through a gap in the old sea-wall, a street begins.

**To the Weed Colonnade** — A street of columns, their tops lost, their shafts furred with weed that lies flat in the direction the water went. Between the columns, doorways, full of sand to the lintel. Anemones the colour of raw meat have opened in the wet, and close if you go near.

**To the Drowned Forum** — The old city’s market square, a hundred paces across, paved in great slabs, most of them still there. The fountain in the middle is a heap of stone with a crab on it. On every side the fronts of buildings stand roofless, their windows full of sky. At the south end, broad steps go down to a building with no windows at all. Mudlarks are working the square with buckets and rakes, children mostly, and a few old men who have been children here for sixty years. They lift their heads to look at you, and go back to work. Wick is up to his knees in a drain at the edge of the square with a rake, a bucket and an expression of total professional absorption.

**Into the Hall of Oaths** — It is pitch dark. Without a light you can see nothing here, and do nothing that needs seeing.

**To the Hall of Clerks** — A long white hall full of desks, and at the desks the Scruples, writing: forty or fifty of them in grey, pale as candles, the ruled lines on their hands and faces moving a little as they move. Nobody speaks above a murmur. The pens make a sound like rain. At the end of the hall a stair goes down; beside it, a door with the word COMPTROLLER cut over it, and nothing else. Here are the Scruples at their desks, who keep the Balance’s books; and the Fourteenth Copyist, a very young Scruple with ink on her ruled fingers and a bad habit of looking at the stair.

**To the Archive of Instruments** — Shelves to the ceiling, and the ceiling out of sight. On them, not books, but instruments: slabs, rolls, tablets, sheets of something like black glass, each with a tag. Every promise the Balance has ever held is here, the Scruples say, from the oath of a king to the bet of a fishwife. In the far wall, a door bound in iron with a single word on it in the ruled script, which you cannot read, and which nobody here looks at. The door of the Room of Defaults is bound in iron and locked.

**Ask the Valuer to open the Room of Defaults in the course of the inventory** — The Valuer looks at the iron door for some time. ‘The compact of the old city is not on my inventory,’ they say. ‘But the city of Tessary claims the old city’s oaths as its heir, and so its defaults are the city’s defaults, and so they bear on the collateral.’ They lay a ruled hand flat on the iron, and the lock turns over inside the door with a sound like a slow bell. ‘I find that I want to see it,’ they say. ‘I note that I want to.’

**To the Room of Defaults** — A small room, which is the surprise. After the archive’s endless shelves, this is a cupboard: one table, one lamp that lights when you come in, one ledger. The ledger is thin. The Scruples do not record many defaults. The iron door of the Room of Defaults stands open.

**Read the thin ledger on the table** — The Room of Defaults records the drowned city’s compact as broken eleven hundred years ago, in the Comptroller’s own hand: ‘The sea had the city. The ledger did not keep. Default entered by the clerk who swore it.’ Beside the entry, in the same hand, a reckoning runs down the margin for a thousand years, and ends in fresh ink: ‘Tessary, heir. War note drawn. Repayment set at a weight the heir cannot meet. At foreclosure, eight thousand oaths fall in, and the hole is closed.’ The War Note was drawn to fail. The Comptroller swore the drowned city’s compact eleven hundred years ago and could not keep it; his default left a hole in the Balance’s ledger. He set Tessary’s repayment at a weight its heir could never meet, so that at foreclosure the Muster’s oaths would fall into the Holding and fill the hole he made. The Valuer reads the ledger over your shoulder, and then reads it again, alone, with their finger on the lines, the way a child reads. When they straighten, the ruled lines on their face have gone faint, as if something had rubbed at them. ‘He swore the compact,’ they say. ‘He did not keep it. And he has drawn the city’s note so that it cannot be paid, so that the Muster will fall in and cover it.’ A long pause. ‘A promise made in order to be broken is not a promise. It is the first thing we are taught. It is the gravest thing one of us can do.’ They close the ledger very gently. ‘I do not know what I am going to do.’ You read the reckoning again with the War Note in your head, and it is not a reckoning. It is a plan. The repayment was never meant to be made. A thousand years of trying to close one hole, and then, at last, a city poor enough and desperate enough to sign.

**Ask the Valuer to indict the Comptroller before the Assembly** — The Valuer is quiet for a long time. ‘Yes,’ they say. ‘It must be one of us, and it must be someone who has read the entry, and I have read it.’ They take out a fresh sheet and write on it, standing, in a hand that does not shake. When they have done they fold it once. ‘The Assembly will sit in the Weighing Chamber on the night of the Settlement, from midnight, and the Notary will come out of the Holding to sit with it. I will be there. You should be there; you read it with me.’ They look at you. ‘I am leaving you here. I have a great deal to prepare and I find I would rather be alone.’ ‘I shall continue the inventory,’ says the Valuer. ‘I am on page nine.’ Ivo and The Ninth Valuer each gain 600 experience: found out why the War Note was drawn.

## assembly

**To the Gallery of Pans** — A gallery cut through the rock with the hum much louder here, a sound you feel in your breastbone. At the far end a door of white metal with no handle stands between two great pans hung on chains from the roof. One pan holds a block of lead cut with nine ruled lines. The other is empty, and the door does not open. Along the wall, on a rack, a set of brass weights, each with its number of lines. Over the door: NINE AGAINST NINE. The white door between the pans has no handle, and does not open. The empty pan hangs high on its chains, the lead’s pan low.

**To the Weighing Chamber** — The Weighing Chamber is a nave, cut out of the bottom of the world. The Balance hangs in it from a beam lost in the dark overhead: two pans each the size of a fishing boat, and a pointer as tall as a mast at rest against a dial of ruled lines. In one pan, very small at the bottom of it, lies a single sheet of black glass. In the other are oaths: not things but the shapes of things, eight thousand faint warm shapes like breath on a cold window, each the size of a hand laid flat. Beyond the Balance, at the end of the nave, is a door with no frame, standing on its own, through which you can see a white floor going on for ever. The pointer leans a hair’s breadth toward the black glass, and the oaths in the other pan stir now and then, like sleepers. The white door stands open, the pans level on their chains.

**Testify before the Assembly to what you read in the Room of Defaults** — You tell them what you read, and where, and when, and who was with you. The floor of the nave registers every word; the ruled lines along the walls brighten and hold. Nobody interrupts. When you have finished the Notary turns its face of lines to the Comptroller. He does not deny it. He cannot. ‘I would draw it again,’ he says, to the Assembly, to the Balance, to the nine black lights at the far edge of everything. ‘It was the only way to close the hole.’ Ivo gains 700 experience: testified before the Assembly of the Scruples.

**Ask the Assembly to put the Comptroller down from his office, and spare him unravelling** — There is a long silence in the nave. Then the Notary speaks. ‘A promise made to be broken is no promise,’ it says. ‘That is the law. What becomes of the one who made it is the Assembly’s.’ It looks at the Valuer. The Valuer looks at the Comptroller for a long time. ‘He has kept the ledger for eleven hundred years,’ they say. ‘Let him keep the archive. Under supervision. Without the black glass.’ And the rows of grey figures bow, all together, which is apparently how the Scruples vote.

**Wait › An hour** — In the Weighing Chamber the Notary lays a line of light through the sheet of black glass in the pan, end to end, and the glass goes grey, and then clear, and then is nothing. ‘Void,’ it says. ‘A promise made to be broken is no promise. The collateral is released.’ The pointer of the Balance swings, a long slow arc, and stops where it stops: not level. There is still a hole in the ledger the size of a city. But it is the Comptroller’s, now, and not the Muster’s.

**Wait › An hour** — Word runs along the Withies faster than the tide: Davy Teazle woke up on the Watersteps in his shirt with his boots beside him, and walked home, and put his boots on the mat inside his mother’s door, side by side. Ivo gains 300 experience: brought Davy Teazle home.

**Wait › A while** — Time passes. The Ninth Valuer sits now at the Comptroller’s desk, which they have had moved into the Hall of Clerks where everyone can see it. They have begun to go through the ledger from the first page, with the old city’s hole in it, and to write in the margin, for each entry, whether it was honest. It will take them, they estimate, four hundred years. They seem, as far as anyone can tell with a Scruple, happy. Sergeant Durrance goes back to the Muffled Drum and drinks one pot, standing, and then goes down to the Watersteps to meet the ones coming home, and calls the roll for them on the top step, every name, in order, until her voice goes. Brother Ancel hangs a weight in Saint Evenhand’s for Josiah Quire: lead, for a debt. It is the only one in the chapel nobody paid for. The note is void and the Muster is home, and the city owes nothing to the Balance and its soldiers everything, as before; the Board will hear the question of the Settlement again in the spring, and this time the public bench will be full. Under the sea the Scruples have a new Comptroller who keeps a hole in the ledger the size of a city in plain sight, and on certain nights, far out past the bar, the sea at Tessary shows other stars. The fishermen have stopped being frightened of it. They say it is good for the eels.
