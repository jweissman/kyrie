**Ask Nan about Davy** — ‘He came home on the Tuesday with the rest of them,’ says Nan, ‘and he sat on that step out there for a week. Didn’t say much. Ate what I put down. Then the spring ebb, the night after the new moon, I wake up and he’s going down the ladder in his shirt. I called him. He didn’t turn round.’ Her needle goes in and out of the net. ‘He set his boots on the top step. Side by side. I had to tell him every night of his life to put his boots together and he never once did it, and he set them on the step like a gentleman’s valet. They taught him that.’ She bites the cord. ‘Then he walked into the sea.’

**Ask Nan what she is offering** — ‘Nine coin,’ says Nan. ‘It’s in a tin. **Ask Nan about the recruiting bill on her shelf** — ‘LAND AND A PENSION FOR EVERY MAN WHO COMES HOME,’ says Nan, without looking at it. ‘They cried that in the Withies with a drum. Davy was seventeen. He came home Tuesday with a red coat and a paper that says he’s owed eleven years’ pay, and the Exchequer has no money to pay it with, and the Board’s to hear the question of the land on the thirteenth day. The question.’ She snorts. ‘They’ve had eleven years to think of the answer.’

**Into the Muffled Drum** — A low taproom built out over the backwater, so that the floor moves a little when the tide is running. Soldiers’ coats on every peg, soldiers’ boots under every bench. **Ask Durrance about the soldiers who have gone into the sea** — ‘Third Company,’ says Durrance. ‘All of them. Pargeter, Haskett, Moll Bryer, the Lucey twins, Davy Teazle. And in order. You know what order? Roll order. Alphabet by platoon, the way the paymaster calls it.’ She drinks. ‘I’ve had nineteen years of roll calls. Somebody is calling the roll, and it isn’t anybody in this city. Next spring ebb it’s Ruddock, Saye, Tamsett, Oddie, Amsel. And then it’s a page turn, and the top of the next page is me.’

**Ask Durrance what the oath on the Stone says** — Durrance doesn’t have to think. ‘’I swear to keep the city of Tessary, its walls and its people, while I live or until it release me.’’ She shrugs. ‘Hand on the Stone, say it once, the recruiting sergeant slaps your back and you’re the city’s. Nobody listens to the words. I listened. I was that kind of recruit.’ A short laugh. ‘Until it release me. It never has. I’ve never heard of anyone it did.’

**Ask Durrance what she means to do when it is her turn** — ‘Go,’ says Durrance. ‘What do you think?’ She puts her hand flat on the table. ‘I put this hand on the Stone in the Yard nineteen years ago and swore. Nobody made me. I swore to keep the city, and I’ve kept it, through two sieges and Sedge Ford and the winter at Galt. If the city’s calling me in, I go in.’ She looks at her hand. ‘I’d like to know who’s doing the calling, mind. That’s all. I’d like to look at them.’

**To Scriveners' Row** — The Lettered Quarter smells of wet pulp and lamp-black. The Row is all stalls: scriveners at sloped desks under awnings, writing letters for those who cannot, contracts for those who can, and arguing with both. Reed-paper hangs drying from every balcony like laundry. At the north end, the Office of Seals has a queue outside it that goes round the corner and, people say, has not been shorter in living memory. Opposite, the Exchequer is a narrow house with barred windows and one lit lamp.

**Into the Exchequer** — A narrow room at the top of a narrow house, all ledgers. The strongboxes along the wall stand open, and are empty, and have been for some time; dust lies inside them. A cot behind a screen. A bottle. Josiah Quire, a Treasurer of Tessary, is here.

**Read a list in the Treasurer's hand, under a paperweight** — Treasurer Quire keeps a list, in his own hand, of every veteran called since the summer, a hundred and thirty-eight names, each with an address and a sum of money sent. The Exchequer knows the vanished are not deserters.

**Ask the Treasurer about his list of names** — Quire looks at the list for a long time. ‘A hundred and thirty-eight,’ he says. ‘I send their mothers something every month. Out of my own salary, which the city also does not pay.’ He takes off his spectacles. ‘I signed it. Twelve years ago. We went down under the sea, six of us, and they showed us a thing like a ledger the size of a ship, and an old man who could not lie read us the terms, and we signed, because we were going to lose the war, and we won.’ His hands are shaking. ‘The Muster’s oaths. As collateral. I put my name to eight thousand men like a farmer putting up his cows.’

**Ask the Treasurer what the city must do to pay** — ‘Keep a promise,’ says Quire. ‘Of equal weight. Their words.’ He laughs, horribly. ‘Do you know what weighs as much as a king’s ancestral oath, in their scales? A promise of a whole people to a whole people. And there is exactly one of those in the city’s books, and I have it by heart: land and a pension for every man who comes home. It would cost two hundred thousand marks. The treasury has nothing. The Board will vote no on the thirteenth day, because there is no money, and on the fifteenth the note falls due, and then...’ He doesn’t finish. **To the Terraces** — The ground rises here to the city’s one hill, and the old houses of the Moieties stand along it in terraces of walled garden, with salt-bitten fig trees and doors painted the colours of their families. The Council House has a portico and a bell. Further along, a tall house with a soldier on its steps is where the city keeps the King of Hethra, courteously, until his ransom is paid. **Into the King's lodging** — Good rooms, kept by a city that wants to be seen to keep its prisoner well: a fire, books, a window on the sea. A board of Hethran draughts is set out by the fire with a game half played. The guard on the steps sits outside the door, not in. Amaury of Hethra, a King of Hethra, a hostage in Tessary, is here.

**Ask the King about the Kneeling at Sedge Ford** — ‘Ah,’ says the King. ‘The famous kneeling.’ He moves a draught. ‘I had won. You understand that? I had your city’s Muster pinned against the river and I rode down to accept its surrender. And in the middle of the ford I felt a hand. Here.’ He touches the back of his neck. ‘Old. Patient. Quite certain of its rights. Nine hundred years old, I would say, if a hand can be old. And my knees went into the water and my sword went out hilt first and I heard my own voice yielding the kingdom.’ He looks at the board. ‘Your saint’s day for it is the twelfth of Haying. I watch the procession from this window. It is the only day of the year I drink before noon.’ You put them side by side: the old oath of Amaury’s line in the War Note, and the hand Amaury felt on his neck at the ford. The miracle the city keeps a saint’s day for was paid for, by the month, in silver and then in soldiers.

**Tell the King how Sedge Ford was won** — The King hears you out without moving a piece. When you have done, he laughs, quite quietly, for a long time. ‘An oath of my house,’ he says, ‘to the Aenean emperors. Bought back off a shelf under the sea by a city of paper-makers, for silver, on the never-never.’ He wipes his eyes. ‘And then paid for in their own soldiers when the silver ran out. Oh, that is good. That is the best thing I have heard in a year.’ He stops laughing. ‘If you want this told to the Board in a voice they cannot dismiss as a stranger’s, I will tell it. They will have to let me out of these rooms to hear it, and I should like to see the inside of their chamber, and the painting.’ He moves a draught at last. ‘I should like very much to see their faces.’

**Into Councillor Marrable's house** — A small house of the Terraces, the smallest, with no garden to speak of. Inside it is a soldier’s house: everything square, everything put away. On a table, a pile of papers weighted down with a pistol-butt with no pistol. Ysbel Marrable, who sits for the Eel Moiety on the Board, a veteran with one sleeve pinned up, is here.

**Ask Councillor Marrable how the vote will go** — ‘Badly,’ says Marrable. ‘Eight chairs. Mine is yes. Old Hallam has buried two sons in Hethra; he’s yes, if he’s awake. Salt is Sault’s and Sault says no, there’s no money, which is true. Rope is Dauntry and Dauntry votes the way Sault tells him. Greaves and Vantage are the furniture and will not give an inch of their land. Paper is Haverick, who cares about nothing but his goose, and Ship is Orme, who will vote for whatever he thinks will be paid for.’ She counts on one hand, which takes no time. ‘I need five. I have two.’

**Ask Councillor Marrable what would change their minds** — ‘Proof,’ says Marrable. ‘They call the vanished deserters because if they’re not deserters, something worse is happening and somebody on the Board knows what. Show them the Treasurer knows. Show them why. Bring them someone they can’t call a liar.’ She looks at the pinned sleeve. ‘And keep the Muster off the Terraces. If soldiers come up that hill shouting the night before, Orme and Hallam will vote no out of fright and I won’t blame them.’

**Wait › A while** — At the spring ebb, twelve veterans of the Long Muster walked barefoot and in step down the Watersteps and out across the drained basin toward the drowned city, as if called, and left their boots paired on the top step. They come down through the Withies a little after two, twelve of them, in step, though nobody is calling the step. Barefoot. Their eyes are open. At the top of the Watersteps each of them stops, and stoops, and sets his boots side by side on the stone, toes to the water, and straightens, and walks down into the sea. The water is very low. They walk out over the sand toward the walls in the basin, getting smaller, keeping step, until the dark has them. It is a long time since anyone slept. The company is weary, and will fight the worse for it until it sleeps.

**Ask the Valuer where the soldiers you saw walk into the sea have gone** — The Valuer considers whether this is the affair of a debtor, and decides, visibly, that what you saw with your own eyes is your own affair. ‘Into the Holding,’ they say, ‘where pledged things are kept. It is a lawful collection.’ Ivo gains 250 experience: found out where the called go.

**Wait › A while** — Time passes. The Ninth Valuer went below at the Settlement with the inventory complete. He is sleeping again. Factor Sault keeps her pans and her ships and loses her chair on the Board, and is seen every evening on the Tidewall eating eels out of a paper cone, as cheerful as ever, and doing sums. In the spring the old estates of the Moieties along the Ossel are surveyed into strips, and eight thousand soldiers and their families go upriver with ploughs bought on the city’s credit, which is now bad, because the city has nothing; but the city kept its word, and the Balance under the sea is level, and the Scruples go back to writing down the small promises of fishwives. The Board meets in a Council House with boarded windows. On the twelfth of Haying there is no procession for the Kneeling. Instead, in the Withies, somebody’s mother puts a recruiting bill in the fire, and everyone stands round and watches it burn.
