# What Tessary Owes — a final artistic judgment

## Where it succeeds

**It has an idea that is also a mechanism.** Obligation is not this adventure's theme so much as its physics. The
city swears casually and the Balance writes it down; the soldiers' oath has a release clause in the city's favour that
no one reads, and the city's promise to the soldiers can be released in theirs; the creditor is himself a debtor; the
most honest person in the story cannot lie and has to learn what that is worth in an institution rotten from its
eldest down. Every resolution is a different answer to the same question (who owes whom, and what is a debt paid
under fraud, or forgiven by the cheated?), and none is the obviously right one. The Release, in which the soldiers save
the city a second time and pay for it with everything they were promised, is the best thing in it, and it does not
tell you how to feel.

**It renews itself by setting.** A city comedy of paper and queues; a drowned city that stands out of the water for
five hours a day; an alien bureaucracy under the sea; a white floor where forfeit things are kept. Each changes the
register, and each follows from the one before by cause: the soldiers walk into the sea, so you follow them; they go
through a wall, so you go down; the debt is owed below, so you go to the creditors; they are held beyond the Balance,
so you go there.

**Its antagonists have reasons and resources and get in each other's way.** The Factor is right about the Board, has
a father on a knee-height plaque, and moves on her own clock whether or not the protagonist interferes. The Comptroller
cannot lie and chooses what to say, and his crime is the kind a decent person could commit for eleven hundred years.
They want the same default for opposite reasons, and a player can set them against each other.

**Some images will stay.** Boots set together on the top step, toes to the water, by men who never set their boots
together in their lives. A coat on a peg nobody wears; hollow people "like coats with nobody in"; the Valuer's grey
coat with nobody in it at the end of the Broken Note. A queue whose oldest member holds his father's place. A goose
eating a king's harvest one grain at a time. A drowned city whose streets lie exactly under the living one, and a
hollow in the drowned court's wall the shape of the stone the city swears its soldiers on. A woman's voice in a jar
singing the ballad of the girl who waited.

**It has people who can change, and change for their own reasons.** The Valuer's turn is a filing, not a feeling,
and it ends four ways. Durrance's belief is tested from four directions. Quire, who signed, and the Comptroller, who
drafted, are the same kind of fool, and Quire is the one who says so.

## Where it is weaker than the ambition

- **It is still, at bottom, an investigation followed by a deadline.** A cold player, reading nothing of the source,
  had the whole mystery by the end of the first night and then waited eleven days for news. The revision that
  followed gave the middle week its scenes back (each of the Factor's moves, and the Treasurer's last hour at the
  Swatch, is now something the Drum hears of a day ahead, with an hour and a place) and made the central reversal
  something the player puts together rather than reads. But the shape is unchanged: the best plays are pre-emptive,
  and a diligent player who pre-empts everything has bought a quiet week.
- **The Board session is a well-written vote.** Its arithmetic is honest, its levers are found in the world, and the
  Valuer can now stand up in it as the one witness nobody can call a liar; but the design's own critique was right:
  a vote is the least dramatic thing in the story, and it is still the hinge of one ending.
- **The Scruples are a hall of clerks with three good lines each, not a society.** The design wanted a culture with
  customs, factions and a politics of its own; what is there is an atmosphere and a law.
- **The Holding is small and mostly seen.** Seven rooms, two of them used. The folded lake, the harvest, the bell and
  the nine lights are marvels to look at; only the Ranks and the Notary's desk are places where something is decided.
  That is defensible (the Holding is a vault, not a country) but it is less than the first treatment imagined.
- **Danger is still mild for an OSR adventure.** Two lethal things (the Forsworn in the plunge-room, guarding the
  drowned city's own drain, and the eel-mother at the foot of the Swatch Stair at the night ebb), each warned of, each
  with ways round that a clever, prepared or kind player can find in the city; everyone else takes prisoners; the tide
  bruises and robs. That suits a story about obligation more than one about survival. The fear of the dark is there
  now, but it is narrow: two places, both optional.
- **The cosmology is asserted more than it is explored.** The wrong stars in the sea are now prepared by fishermen and
  a chandler before the Comptroller confirms them, and the Foreclosure ending pays them off; but the Balance as an
  instrument of the planes' fastening, and the Kiritarun as tallies, remain things people say.

## What I would change with another serious revision

1. **Give the middle week its own plot**: a second, smaller crisis in the city that the Factor and the Comptroller
   both want resolved their way (a Scruple seen on the Tidewall; a riot over the deserter bills; Amaury's escape),
   so that the fortnight has a shape and not only a deadline.
2. **Replace the vote with a scene**: the Board's session as a place with people in it who can be spoken to during it,
   interrupted, shamed; the arithmetic kept underneath.
3. **Give the Scruples a politics**: a faction of clerks who already suspect the Comptroller; the Copyist as the
   youngest of them; the Assembly as a society deciding something about itself, not only about him.
4. **Make the Holding a place to be in**: a night spent there; the forfeit things wanting something; the goose as a
   guide; the bell with consequences.
5. **A Promise construct in Kyr** (see [friction.md](friction.md)): the registered promises are the soul of the
   adventure and each is hand-built from five rules; a first-class promise would make it possible to let the player
   swear far more, and be held to far more, which is what the setting most wants.
