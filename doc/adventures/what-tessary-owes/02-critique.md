# What Tessary Owes — editorial review of the first treatment

*An adversarial reading of `01-treatment.md`, written before the engine was examined. Each finding names the
weakness, why it matters to play, and the decision taken. The decisions are carried into `03-revised-design.md`.*

## 1. Dramatic structure

**1.1 Act IV is a vote, and a vote is a spreadsheet.** The treatment's political act lets the player move
councillors from *no* to *yes* until a threshold is crossed. That is a dialogue mini-game with a number at the
end, and it makes the pension vote the climax of the city plot — but a vote is the least physical, least
dangerous thing in the adventure.

*Worse:* if the vote passes, the debt is simply paid and Act V has no reason to exist. The treatment admits this
by saying "whatever happened at the vote, the final scene is here", which is the itinerary talking.

**Decision.** The promise was *land and pension to every soldier who came home*. A promise is kept when it is
delivered, not when it is voted. The deeds must be sealed and put into the hands of every living veteran of the
Muster before the Feast ends — **including the ones who are held**. They are held because the city did not keep
its promise; the city cannot keep its promise because they are held. The vote passing is an early victory that
reveals a worse difficulty: someone must carry the deeds into the Holding. Act V now follows from Act IV by
cause, not by schedule. The vote itself is demoted from climax to one of several things the city crisis
contains, and the crisis is made physical: the riot, the attempt on Wend, the Seal, the Feast night on the Weir.

**1.2 Every revelation enlarges; none overturns.** Collection → Brack → the terms → the Holding → the cosmos.
Each step adds a room to the house; none makes the player see the earlier rooms differently, except the Kneeling.
An ally who is not what they seemed would do that work.

**Decision.** *The loan was designed to fail.* The Kept's eldest, the Comptroller, made the promise eleven hundred
years ago that the sea would not have Aenean Tessary — and the sea had it. His default left a hole in the ledger
he has spent a millennium trying to fill honestly. Tessary's loan, with a repayment no poor city could meet, was
built so that eight thousand oaths would fall into the Holding and fill it. He opposes Brack — but because her
purchase would take the oaths *out* of the Holding and reopen his hole, not because he cares about the veterans.
In Act III he presents himself as the protagonist's ally against Brack. The discovery that he engineered the
whole arrangement turns the creditors from a neutral system into a second strategic power and turns the
Assessor's arc from sentiment into crisis.

**1.3 The ending list is a menu.** Six endings in the treatment, several with no preparation in earlier acts (the
Commons especially, whose mechanism was hand-waved — a later oath does not void an earlier pledge, by the
treatment's own logic).

**Decision.** Endings must each be *prepared by something the player did in an earlier act* and must leave a
different world. The Commons is replaced by **the Release**: by the Kept's law a promise is also discharged when
its promisee holds the promisor quit. If the living veterans of the Muster stand on the Oath Stone and release
the city from its promise to them, the promise counts as kept and the ledger balances — the soldiers save the city,
again, and get nothing for it. Triumph or injustice is for the player to decide; Rook leads it or refuses it.
"The pledge" becomes a personal act available inside other endings, not an ending. Five resolutions remain (see
the revised design), each with a different Tessary afterward.

## 2. Character

**2.1 The Assessor's arc is "the alien learns to feel".** Watching a mother cry is not a reason for a being of
perfect exactness to change; it is a reason for the *author* to want them to. The arc as written is driven by
affection, which in practice means an affection score.

**Decision.** The Assessor changes for reasons of their own kind: a *legal and moral contradiction they can
prove*. Their pivots are specific discoveries — that the city's promise to its soldiers is prior to and heavier
than its promise to the Kept; that Brack intends dominion, which a note may not lawfully be bought for; and,
decisively, that their own Comptroller drafted a promise in order that it be broken, which in their law is no
promise at all and the gravest offence a Kept can commit. They respond with the obsessive exactness that defines
them: a motion, a filing, an indictment. Personal feeling enters only at the edges (Pim, who names them). They
also act when the player is not present: they take testimony, file with the Comptroller, visit Fenn.

**2.2 Brack is the stock merchant villain.** Salt money, warehouses, smugglers: every fantasy city has her. And
the treatment teeters toward giving her a dead son to make her sympathetic, which the commission explicitly warns
against.

**Decision.** No dead son. Brack is ambitious, competent and *right about the council*. She rose from the pans,
remembers every worker's name, eats eels from a paper cone on the Weir, calls the old houses "the furniture". She
is also the protagonist's **first patron**: she hires them to find out who is taking "her" veterans, because she
wants to look like the veterans' friend and wants to know what the Kept and the council are doing. Work done for
her in Act I feeds her bid. She can be bargained with, and one resolution is a compromise with her.

**2.3 Rook is a type (the tough sergeant).** She needs a belief that events can test.

**Decision.** Rook believes in the oath — not the city, the *oath*: you swore, you go. She is next in roll order
and will be called at the next spring tide. Brack offers to pay Rook's month of interest in silver to keep her off
the roll, in exchange for her service — a real temptation, because it would also save the men after her. Rook's
possible ends: she answers the drum and is held (and can be found); she is kept back and leads the Release; she
becomes Brack's sergeant; or, having followed the evidence to the Comptroller's design, she concludes that an oath
taken under a fraud is not an oath, and leads the veterans against both city and creditor.

**2.4 Fenn, Aldric and Pim have no decisions.** They are information dispensers with good lines.

**Decision.** Fenn can confess before the council (swinging the vote and ruining himself), flee, or — late and
only if the protagonist has given him reason — go into the Holding and pledge his own name for the held. If no one
speaks to him after Brack's pressure, he drowns himself at the Throat, and his list is found. Aldric wants to go
home; he will testify before the council that the miracle at the ford was purchased, which is explosive and only
possible if he has been told the truth. Pim runs a business and has a brother in the Holding; Pim is the
protagonist's guide in Lowtide and sells the tide-tables that make it survivable.

## 3. World

**3.1 Could this happen anywhere?** Mostly no, which is good: a paper city of contracts, built over a drowned
Aenean city, claiming Aenean succession, with an oath-stone salvaged from the drowned court. But two features were
decorative: the Feast of Settlement's origin and the city's casual swearing. Both should *do something*.

**Decision.** The Feast's contract-burning is the vestige of the Aenean practice of discharging registered
promises at the year's last spring ebb — the Kept's cycle; the date of the note's maturity is not arbitrary. The
city's casual swearing becomes a mechanism in the Strongrooms: the weighing floors register what is said on them,
so a careless "I swear I'll be back" spoken there becomes a real binding. Tessary's habit is the player's danger.

**3.2 Tessary's claim to Aenean succession is load-bearing in one direction only.** It let the city call
Aldric's ancestral oath. It should cut both ways.

**Decision.** If Tessary inherits the Aenean city's credits, it inherits its claims: including the Comptroller's
unpaid promise that the sea would not have it. That is the legal basis of **the Offset** — and the reason the
Comptroller needed Tessary in particular.

**3.3 The cosmology is a postcard.** Nine dark points in the Holding that are the hearts of the Varakiiri, seen
and not touched, is spectacle. The commission warns against generic declarations of cosmic importance.

**Decision.** Keep the vision, but give the cosmological layer a *consequence in Tessary*: if the ledger is left
uneven at the end (foreclosure without balance, or Brack calling the oaths out), the fastening at Tessary thins,
and in the world afterward the sea in Lowtide sometimes shows other stars and things come up the Throat that were
not born in Imbar. The hearts are not a resource; they are the measure of what the Kept think they are protecting,
which makes the Comptroller's crime comprehensible without making it right. All cosmological statements are made
by people (the Kept, the Registrar) and are flagged in the design as contributions for canon review.

## 4. Player agency

**4.1 The calling at the first spring tide is witnessed but cannot be affected.** Fine for the opening, but Rook's
calling must be contestable, or the protagonist is watching a film.

**Decision.** Three ways to keep Rook back, each with a price: pay the month's interest (Brack's silver, a theft
from Brack, or a loan the protagonist must promise to repay); obtain an extension from the Comptroller (a
registered promise); or wake her after the drum takes her and hold her on land until the ebb is past (a fight with
a sleepwalking veteran who is stronger than you). Or let her go, and find her later.

**4.2 Can the player play the powers against each other?** The treatment had one adversary.

**Decision.** Two strategic powers with conflicting interests — Brack wants the note sold, the Comptroller wants it
foreclosed — and the Assessor between them. Telling the Comptroller of Brack's bid makes him obstruct her (her
broker is suspended; her Lowtide stair is sealed). Telling Brack of the Comptroller's hole gives her a blackmail
lever she will use. Both reactions change later acts.

**4.3 Delay and failure.** If the protagonist dawdles past the Feast, does the adventure end? It must not.

**Decision.** The Feast passing produces a state, not a game-over: foreclosure (and Brack's purchase, if her
undertakings have succeeded). The Holding remains reachable and the held remain held, so a late protagonist can
still contest Brack's claim or redeem individuals, at a worse price.

## 5. Adventure design

**5.1 Lowtide as written is a timer.** A tide dungeon can be wonderful or a frustration.

**Decision.** The tide is legible (Pim's tables; the water's state described in every Lowtide place; high refuges
on Aenean rooftops), and being caught is a credible setback, not death: swept out through the Throat, washed up on
the Pans, hurt and having lost what was not tied on. There is also a dry route — the lamplighters' cistern from
Brack's Saltgate warehouse — which is always open and always guarded. Different routes, different problems.

**5.2 Too few kinds of problem.** Investigation and negotiation dominate.

**Decision.** Distribute: a sneak/theft (Brack's warehouse ledger), a chase (the riot), a defence (Wend's house), a
mechanism (the weighing floors and the Strongrooms' counterweight doors), a reading puzzle (the drowned-court
inscriptions and the archive), a tactical retreat (the turning tide), a contest of loyalty (the veterans at the
Oath Stone), combat that can be avoided (lamplighters, Forsworn, the Weights).

## 6. Tone

**6.1 It is solemn in the middle.** Comedy lives in the city (Office of Seals, Pim's coins); the Strongrooms and
the Holding are all hush and portent.

**Decision.** The Strongrooms get their own comedy, born of their exactness: a form to request the form; a junior
Kept clerk who secretly writes surface poetry and is very bad at hiding it; the weighing floor registering the
protagonist's idle oaths; the Assessor counting the minutes. The Holding gets one absurdity among the marvels — a
goose, pledged in a wager three hundred years ago and still indignant about it.

## 7. Originality

**7.1 Bank goblins.** A subterranean institution of pale non-humans who hold a city's debts is uncomfortably near a
well-known wizarding bank.

**Decision.** The Kept are human-descended clerks, not a goblin race; they own nothing; they do not want wealth;
they cannot lie; their names are pledged; their fear is unravelling. Their purpose — keeping a cosmic ledger even
— is the opposite of greed.

**7.2 The Holding is Ariosto's moon.** Astolfo's valley of lost things, where Orlando's wits are kept in a jar.

**Decision.** Keep it as homage — it is a good image and an old one — but with its own logic: not lost things,
*pledged and forfeit* things, each tagged with the promise that put it there, and the place has a keeper with
terms.

**7.3 The literal-clause trick (the soldiers swore "to keep the city") is Portia's "no jot of blood".**

**Decision.** Keep it as one tool among several, never the solution: it explains why Brack needs the council's Seal
as well as the note, and gives the protagonist a lever against her in the Holding, but the resolutions rest on
kept promises, releases and exposures, not on a quibble.

## 8. Scope honesty

The first treatment has more characters, wards and endings than a first implementation can make *good*. The
revised design names what is essential (marked *spine*), what deepens (*depth*), and what is cut. The spine is
what must work end to end in every resolution.
