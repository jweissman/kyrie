# What Tessary Owes — a literary review of the built work, before the last revision

*Written after the adventure was complete and tested, at the request to read it critically as literature: what has
real depth, what is pulp, what I am not happy with. The revision it led to is recorded at the end.*

## What is genuinely there

**A real idea, with consequences all the way down.** The adventure is about obligation: what a promise weighs, who
holds it, whether a promise made in bad faith binds, and whether forgiving a debt is a gift or a second injury. That
idea is not a theme painted on; it is the mechanism of every scene. The city's casual swearing is dangerous below; the
soldiers' oath has a release clause in the city's favour that nobody ever reads, and the city's promise to them can be
released in theirs; the creditor is himself a debtor; the most moral person in the story is the one who cannot lie,
and their crisis is learning that the institution built on that incapacity was rotten from its eldest down. The
Release is the best thing in it: the soldiers save the city a second time and pay for it with what they were owed,
and the ending refuses to say whether that is triumph or injustice.

**Some images that are its own.** Boots set side by side on the top step, toes to the water, by men who never set
their boots together in their lives. A coat on a peg that nobody wears. A queue whose oldest member holds his father's
place. Hollow people "like coats with nobody in." A goose eating a king's harvest one grain at a time. A drum with a
black cloth over its head that nobody plays until the last of the Third comes home.

**Two characters who can change.** The Assessor's arc is driven by what they can prove, not by what they feel, and
it ends four genuinely different ways. Durrance's belief (the oath, not the city) is tested from four sides.

## What I am not happy with

### 1. The depth is in the design, not yet in the texture

Most of what is profound in Tessary lives in the plot's arithmetic. The prose, place by place, is concrete but
*local*: each room is well observed and stops at its walls. There is little sense that this city has had other
fortnights. The Atlas asks for a world older and larger than its plot; Tessary has a past (the drowning, the war) but
almost no **history**: no earlier crisis, no previous broken promise, no monuments with wrong inscriptions, no songs
about anything but the plot. The illusion of depth needs references that go *beyond* the story and are never
cashed.

### 2. The theme of doubling is everywhere and nowhere

Reading it again, the adventure is full of doubles it never notices: a city built on the plan of the city drowned
beneath it; two promises of equal weight; two strategic powers who want the same default for opposite reasons; a
scrivener's draft, a copied inventory, a clerk called the Copyist; the Ledger, which writes down every act a second
time; boots standing in for the men who left them; empty coats on pegs, hollow Forsworn, the Assessor's grey coat
with nobody in it at the end of the Broken Note; Saint Evenhand, a Scruple who came up, and the Assessor, who may;
the Comptroller, who swore a promise young and could not keep it, and Quire, who signed one and could not keep it
either, and who both answer it with drowning. None of this is pointed at. It should be: not explained, but *rhymed*,
so that a player half-notices that Lowtide's streets lie under Tessary's, that the Oath Stone stands above a hollow
in the drowned court's wall shaped exactly like it, that the world here keeps a second copy of itself.

### 3. The Comptroller explains himself

His first speech hands the player the cosmology ("You have heard, I suppose, that the planes were fastened apart.
This is one of the places where they were fastened.") and his confession ends on a line ("against the fastening of
the world") that is pulp grandeur reaching for weight it has not earned. The stars in the sea are mentioned for the
first time *by him*, as a claim, when they should be something the player has already heard fishermen say and
half-disbelieved. The cosmic stake should be verified by the world before it is asserted by a speech.

### 4. The default is not foreshadowed

The adventure's central reversal (the creditor drew the note to fail, because he defaulted first) arrives without a
trace before the Room of Defaults. A good reversal makes earlier details mean something new. There should be a saying
of Saint Evenhand's in the chapel, a phrase on the Tideline Steps, a Scruple clerk's unguarded line, so that the
ledger in the Room of Defaults *completes* something rather than introducing it.

### 5. The Factor is offstage

Sault has a fine argument and almost no scenes. She hires the protagonist, makes her case in two topics, and is then
present only through her effects (the whisper, the lightermen, the letter). She never seeks the protagonist out,
never reacts to what they have done to her, and her motive is ambition plus a good argument, which is enough for a
politician and thin for the second-most-important person in the story. She needs one scene in which she spends
something of herself, and a history that rhymes with the plot.

### 6. The most dangerous place has no reason to be entered

The Forsworn in the bath-house are the adventure's only lethal creatures, and there is nothing in the plunge-room a
player needs. A danger nobody has a reason to face is scenery. OSR danger should guard something worth the risk, and
offer a way round it for the clever, the prepared and the kind.

### 7. Too few problems have more than one key

The vote, the Durrance night and the endings are richly multiple. Smaller problems are not: the counterweight door
has one solution (weights); the ebb-runners' lockbox is opened by stealth or force but not by persuasion; the
warehouse lightermen can only be fought; the way into the Strongrooms needs the Assessor or the compact.

### 8. Pulp, specifically

Most of the prose stays on the right side of the line, but these lines reach past what they have earned:
"a nave, cut out of the bottom of the world"; "the nine black lights at the far edge of everything"; "something comes
up the Throat on the flood that was not born in Imbar"; "Something about the nine points makes you want very much to
look somewhere else." The first two are the adventure telling the player to be awed. The third is acceptable OSR
dread if the earlier wrong stars have prepared it. The fourth is a cliché of the uncanny.

## The revision

1. **History beyond the plot**: the Salt War forty years ago and its First Muster, also promised land, also given
   nothing but a plaque and a rake (the city's first broken promise, the Factor's father among them); the Drowning and
   the four hundred years before the Tidewall; the eel-fair, the paper trade, Old Ennelmark, the bell under the basin;
   inscriptions and plaques that are wrong in revealing ways.
2. **Doubling, rhymed not explained**: Lowtide's streets named and placed under Tessary's; a still pool in the Forum
   in which the city hangs upside down; the Stone-shaped hollow in the Hall of Oaths' end wall; Pounce on contracts made
   in two copies and Tessary as the second copy of a city; the empty coat motif sharpened; the two Musters.
3. **Foreshadowing the default**: a saying of Saint Evenhand's ("Not the sea. A promise drowned the old city."), the
   worn letters on the Tideline Steps, a Scruple clerk's slip, fishermen's wrong stars on the Tidewall at night.
4. **The Comptroller rewritten** to say less and imply more; the cosmology moved into what the player has seen.
5. **The Factor given a scene and a past**: she seeks the protagonist out on the Tidewall at dusk, reacts to what they
   have done, and, if shown the Room of Defaults' truth, tells them about her father.
6. **The Forsworn guard the drowned city's own way down**: a drain under the plunge-pool into the Scruples' cistern,
   which bypasses the black wall. Three ways past them: fight; draw them out by swearing aloud in the hall and slip
   through; or give them what they hold out their hands for, the weight of a kept promise from Saint Evenhand's.
7. **More keys**: the Assessor can balance the counterweight door; Bettany Slake will hand over the Factor's letter
   to someone who tells her what her letters are for; the warehouse lightermen let anyone on the Factor's books pass,
   and can be slipped past at the change of the night watch.
8. **The pulp lines cut or earned.**

## What the revision did, and what it did not

All eight items were carried out in the Kyr (two new files, `history.kyr` and `ways.kyr`, and rewrites through
`people.kyr`, `lowtide.kyr`, `strongrooms.kyr`, `voices.kyr` and `feast.kyr`) and each new way through is a journey in
`test/what-tessary-owes.test.ts`. What was added beyond the list, because the list exposed it:

- **The Treasurer doubles the Comptroller, and knows it.** Told that the note was drawn to fail, Quire wants the relief
  of being cheated, and then refuses it: he signed what he did not read, as the Comptroller swore what he could not
  keep, and both of them answered it the same way. With Pounce on contracts drawn in two copies, it is one of only two
  places the adventure points at its own doubling, and both are a character's insight, not the narrator's.
- **The Teazles afterwards.** Davy home asleep in the red coat with his boots on, which his mother has not mentioned;
  later, what he remembers of the Ranks (standing, in a line, white like snow with no cold in it, and a voice singing
  the Sally song) rhymes with the jar in the Holding without saying so.
- **The eel-mother.** A second lethal danger, the only one in the open: a conger as long as a boat at the foot of the
  Throat Stair, at the night ebb only. She warns before she strikes. Old Gudrun, who sells hot eels on the Tidewall by
  day, will tell anyone who asks what the eel-mother likes; a cone thrown into the channel buys the stair. A patient
  company can slip past along the wall; a sensible one goes down by day.
- **Intensifiers pruned.** A pass through the strings for *very*, *somehow*, *strangely* and their kind cut those
  that were doing a noun's work.

What it did not do, and could not in a revision of this size, is recorded in [08-judgment.md](08-judgment.md): the
middle week still waits; the vote is still a vote; the Scruples still have no politics. The doubling is now present
throughout, but it is rhymed in scenery more than in choices: the player sees the second city, the second Muster, the
second fool, and is never asked to *be* a second copy of anyone. The one place that comes near it is the pledge, where
the protagonist's name lies on the Notary's desk in place of a soldier's; I would build outward from there.
