# The Book of Faces: the original treatment

*Stage 1. Written before the engine was read, and before any editor looked at it. It is ambitious on purpose; the
editorial that follows ([01-editorial.md](01-editorial.md)) cuts it. Names here are working names.*

## The premise in one breath

Ombra is a delta city of forty thousand, built on the silt of a river that has never been able to decide where it
ends, where it rains nine days in ten. Three hundred years ago, after a feud between fourteen families had been
fought in its streets by the simple method of knowing who everybody was, the city signed the **Concord of the
Unseen**, and made a religion of not knowing.

Between the third bell of the morning and the ninth of the evening, nobody in Ombra is anybody. Everyone goes
masked. A baker is a Cockle, a ferrywoman a Gull, a clerk of the Weights a Heron, and to name the person under a
mask aloud, in the street, is the one crime the city punishes without mercy: the offender is **Named**, which is to
say their name is cried in every square, and every grudge, debt and feud they have ever escaped attaches to it by
sundown. The nineteen great Offices of the city (the Treasurer, the Warden of Locks, the Assessor of Weights, the
Physician-General, the Serene) are not held by persons but by **masks**, drawn by lot every year at the **Drawing**
from an Urn that holds a tessera for every citizen over sixteen. The wearer serves a year, goes **Spent**, and is
never to speak of it. Which mask sat on which face is written in the **Book of Faces**, kept sealed by the
**Registrar**, in the Chapel of the Bare Face, for ninety years.

Strangers are the **Bare**. They are not persons in the city's law. They are fed, housed in the Hospice at the
Gate of Strangers for nine days, and not spoken to. Nobody who is masked will confide in a bare face, and a bare face
is not allowed to be a name.

On the eve of the **Novena**, the nine days of rain-prayer that end in the Drawing, the Registrar was found dead in
the Chapel in the white mask of her office. A Registrar's mask may be lifted only by her successor-designate, whose
name is in the Book. The Book is gone from its lectern. So nobody may lawfully say who is dead, whether an Office is
vacant, or who is to read the lots; and if the Drawing is not held on the ninth night, the Concord's own text says
that at midnight all nineteen Offices *dissolve*, "being only masks", and Ombra has no government at all, in the
rains, with the sluices unwatched and fourteen palaces cleaning their old swords.

The Masked Watch has one lawful move left. In the law's seventy-first article is the **Stranger's Warrant**: when
the Concord is at an impasse it forbids itself to resolve, a thing no masked person may see or say, it may hire a
Bare to see it and say it. It was last used sixty-two years ago. A sergeant of the Watch goes down to the Gate of
Strangers with the Warrant in her glove and looks along the line of the wet and unwanted.

You are the Bare.

## What you are, and what you are offered

You are a stranger with nine days, an unusual legal personality, and no standing to ask for anything. The Warrant
lets you look at what no mask may look at: the body, the Chapel, the faces under the masks if you dare. It does not
make anyone speak to you. To be spoken to, you must wear a face, and a face is a person, and a person can be
Named.

At the Warrant's end, the law says, the Bare who served it is **offered a face of their own**: citizenship, a
trade-mask, a name that is theirs and unsayable. It is a real offer, and the question of whether to take it is
the question the book ends on.

## The truth

*Ysolde Marrick was a lamp-trimmer's daughter drawn by the Urn, honestly, at nineteen, as Treasurer.* She was
bad at it. In her year the Cullet Sluice failed in the Rains and six hundred people in the low islands drowned. The
Concord's secrecy saved her from the mob, and it fell on the Serene of that year, Absalom Peck, who stood in the
public gallery in the white-and-gold and said nothing, and was Named, and has lived Bare in the Hospice ever since.

She spent the rest of her life making sure it did not happen again. She became the Registrar's clerk, then the
Registrar. And for thirty-one years the Urn has not drawn anything. **The Urn is a sealed bronze jar with a slot and
nothing in it.** The Registrar reads the lots from a palm-card. Every year since the Sluice, Marrick walked the
city in the white mask by night, unrecognised, mending what she could and learning who mended, and chose the
Offices by hand, by character, and let the city believe in chance because a city that believes in chance has nobody
to feud with. The Concord has not been well governed for three hundred years; it has been well governed for
thirty-one.

She was seventy-four. She knew it was her heart. She could not name a successor, since the law says the Urn names
the Registrar, and the Urn is empty, and the only person who could be told the truth without being bound by the
Concord is a Stranger. She left a ledger, the **Rounds**, nine entries for nine nights, initials and a street each:
errands of the kind she had been doing, left unfinished. She arranged, with the one person she trusted in the Watch,
that if anything happened to her the Warrant would be invoked. She did not know who the Bare would be. She meant to
find out by what they did with her list.

She died on the first evening, at the lamp-table in the Chapel, between one office and the next.

## Four hands on the night she died

Nobody killed her. Four people did something to her night, each for a reason that was human and would, in a city
that could say names, have been simple to forgive:

1. **Tomas Pell**, her Clerk of the Book, found her, put the white mask back on a face that had slipped, because the
   sight of a Registrar's bare face is the worst thing a clerk can do, and took the Book away to the one place
   nobody looks: the Dead Letter Office, posted to *Nobody*, to be safe until a successor could be named. The Dead
   Letter Office burns the Nobodies at noon on the ninth day.
2. **The Serene**, this year's, a cooper named Hobb whom she had chosen for his dullness, came to her chapel that
   night to beg to be let off the Mask. He saw her, and ran. He has not been able to take the Mask off since, for
   fear that without it he would be asked what he saw.
3. **Ser Corvin Lacke**, Warden of Locks, a thoroughly corrupt Office, had called on her an hour before she died,
   to threaten her with exposure (he had worked out whom the Fitter's casts say she was) if she did not call off the
   Rounds that were closing on his Lock-tax. He said her name aloud in the Chapel. That is Unfacing. It frightened
   her to death, or it did not; the heart was going anyway. It is the only crime in the whole affair that Ombra's
   law can prosecute, and he committed it.
4. **A lamp-boy**, Pip Tolliver, nine, was under the lamp-table trimming wicks. He saw the face, saw the clerk, the
   cooper and the Warden come and go, understood nothing except that he had seen a Registrar bare, which in his
   religion is the end of him. He is in the cisterns under the Drip.

The mystery is to assemble these four and the Book, and to learn from whose hands what the city is made of.

## The city, in seven sleeves and two undersides

Ombra's islands are called Sleeves, because from the air they are a coat with the arms spread; the water between them
is the Cuff. Seven Sleeves, an under-city, a marsh.

|---|---|---|
| **The Gate of Strangers** | The landward end: customs steps, the Hospice, the Wardrobe Annex that rents masks by the day, the Watch post. | Bare lodgers; Mother Tibb; Moll the clerk; the Masked Watch |
| **Maskers' Lane** | The Guild of Maskers: workshops, fitting-rooms, a theatre called the Wet Stage, and under the Fitter's house the **Wall of Faces**: a life-cast of every citizen, kept since the Concord, because a mask must fit. | Madame Pavane; apprentices; players |
| **Counting Row** | The Exchange, the Weights hall, the offices of the Warden of Locks, the tallow market, the tallymen's coffee-rooms. | Assessors, tallymen, clerks; Lacke; his clerk Fenn |

## The day and the night

The rhythm of the city is the first game. **By day** everyone is masked and masks do not answer the bare: the player
must wear a face to be spoken to, and the face brings the rules of the face. A trade mask opens its guild's doors, an
Office mask opens an Office's, but each is a person's identity and each will be recognised by the *Fit*, which only
sits properly on the face it was made for. **By night**, after the ninth bell, people go home, take off their masks in
private rooms, and will open a door to a knock, but only a bare face is received; a masked one is a burglar. The
daytime city is the city of *saying*; the night city is the city of *telling*. Some secrets are available only in
one, and some doors only open in the other.

## Rounds, and the city's other stories

Marrick's ledger has nine entries. They are the small adventures that matter to the plot, and each is the opening of a
person's story. The player can complete as many as nine days and a body allow; each Round finished leaves a **Mark**
(a small deed, remembered) and each also teaches a fragment of who she was. But the city has more stories than the
Registrar's list, and some of the best are not on it: a feud between umbrella families, a clerk who lost his mask in a
wager, a retainer dusting an empty palace for an heiress who sews hems in the Drip. The Registrar's view of the city
had gaps. The player's is not obliged to share them.

### The Rounds (Marrick's ledger)

1. *R. K., the Gull.* A ferrywoman's boat is sinking by inches and she will not take a gift. (Rill Ketch)
2. *C. D., the Heron.* A Weights-clerk writes love-letters to a Lamp he has never seen. (Cosimo Dray)
3. *A. P., Hospice.* A man who was Named thirty-one years ago and is dying of it. (Absalom Peck)
4. *Sister N.* The soup-kitchen's anonymous donor dies with the Registrar; there is bread for four days. (Sister Nobody)
5. *The short pound.* A copy of the Standard Pound, lighter by an ounce, is in use at the bread-market. (the Weights)
6. *Weights Row, C. L.* Lacke, the Warden of Locks. (the Lock-tax)
7. *P. T., cisterns.* She saw a lamp-boy hiding and had not found him. (Pip)
8. *The Heron-writ.* The Exchange means to drain the Heronry. (the herons)
9. *Gaspar.* An old retainer is dusting for no one. (Gaspar Venn, Lisbet Venn)

### Others of the city's stories, not in the ledger

Eleven more, of which seven are local: the **Umbrella War**, the **Wager of Barnaby Sweet**, the **Mask That Won't
Fit** (an apprentice's made face that remembers its dead wearers), the **Letter-Writer's Dilemma**, the **Duel of
Spoons** (precedence at a Novena dinner), the **Pigeon Strike**, the **Lamp-Trimmers' Strike**, the **Rain-Wives'
Petition** (twelve families have won the Drawing too often), the **Wall of Faces** (a cast of your own face), the
**Hospice Count** (more Bare than beds, a comic structure with a hard core), and the **Burning of the Nobodies**.

## The two ways in, and two more

Everything leads to the Chapel's **Registry** and the **Urn Hall** under it, where on the ninth night the Drawing is
held. Four ways reach it.

**The Warrant** is the way of the bare face and the law. It is procedural: the Warrant lets you inspect the body and
open the Hearing of the Unseen, where masks may speak to a Bare; a signed concurrence of three Guilds (Watch,
Exchange, Maskers) opens the Registry doors by right. It is persuasion, insight, lore, patience and a clean name. It
cannot lie, it cannot hide, and it is slow, and the Watch's patience, **heat**, shrinks each time you name a face.

**The Face** is the way of the borrowed mask. You buy or are lent an Office's face and *are* the Assessor of Weights at
the Registry door. It is deception, performance, stealth, an Office's habits, and the *Fit*, which fails on any face
it was not cut for unless something has been done to it. It is fast, and it is complicit: you take part in the Lie
to do it.

**The Cisterns** are the way of the thief, ranger and warrior. Under the Drip the Ninefold Drain runs to the Urn Hall's
foundations; the way is flooded, trapped, and dangerous, and shortens with the rain; it is closed from the sixth day.

**The Stair** is the way of the Old Names. The Contessa Tarn has a private stair from the Quiet Hill to the Chapel's
vestry that nobody has used in three hundred years. She will lend it for a price that is also a position.

## The seven callings

For each calling there is a submodule of its own, with an institution to belong to, decision points that say what kind
of a warrior or mage or cleric you took yourself for, and a finale at the Drawing.

- **The warrior: the Gauntlet.** The Duelling Stair of Quiet Hill is ninety-nine steps and nine landings, a Second
  at each; the Seconds' Guild holds that a champion is made by what they refuse. Each landing offers a different
  understanding of the sword: the hireling's, the defender's, the breaker's. At the top the Contessa wants a Second
  for a killing. *Finale:* hold the Stair on the Ninth Night against a riot, with or without killing.
- **The mage: the Fit.** A mask sits properly only on the face it was cut for; the Collegium of Weights does not know
  why. The research of a new spell, **Likeness**, in four stages from a failing theory through three sources, each of
  which asks a different thing of a mage's understanding of magic (power, precision, cost), ends in a casting at the
  Urn. *Finale:* show the Urn's truth to the room, or hide it.
- **The thief: the Cast-Room.** The Wall of Faces is the true Book. A heist in five stages, with a crew, three
  approaches and a conscience: take what, and give it to whom. *Finale:* the Book, or the Wall, in the right hands
  or the wrong.
- **The cleric: the Confessional.** In a city of masks the confessional is the one place where masks speak to a
  person as a person. A penitent will confess the whole of the matter and ask for silence. *Finale:* bless the Draw,
  refuse it, or break the seal.
- **The bard: the Lampoon.** The Wet Stage's licence permits masked satire of Offices. The bard composes a
  Lampoon from the city's small stories, chooses a target (Lacke, the Candid, the Contessa, the Urn), a form (farce,
  elegy, ballad), and plays it. *Finale:* the crowd.
- **The ranger: the Heronry.** The city's last wild thing is a reed-garden and an old white heron. Tracking a hidden
  child through a city by wet footprints, pigeon-droppings and rat-runs; a writ to defend. *Finale:* the heron, and
  the child's word.
- **The monk: the Nine Quiets.** The Bare Brothers are the city's only masked-by-vow-bare: they go unmasked by oath
  and are invisible by contempt. The nine quiets are nine temptations to speak, name, strike or turn away. *Finale:*
  hold the Chapel's door by doing nothing.
- **The commoner, and anyone: A Trade of One's Own.** To be a person in Ombra is to have a trade; the commoner takes
  one up (ferry, lamp, chandler) in three beats and wears its mask honestly. *Finale:* the Trades' procession.

## The ends

At midnight on the ninth night, one of seven things happens, and the closing account says what became of the Book,
the Drawing, the Urn, the Masks and each person who mattered:

1. **The Quiet Hand.** The Lie continues. Someone is chosen (you, or Pell) to choose; the city is governed well and
   knows nothing.
2. **The Open Book.** The Book is read aloud. Every Spent and every living wearer is named; Vane's Candid Republic;
   the feuds come back, and so does the Sluice.
3. **The Honest Urn.** The lots are filled for the first time in thirty-one years and drawn by chance, in public:
   a farce, a danger, and, in one place, a marvel.
4. **The Qualified Draw.** A civic compromise, a drawing from among those who have mended something this week; the
   Rounds as a franchise.
5. **The Year of Nobody.** No Office drawn; the city goes unmasked and unruled for a year of carnival; the bard's end.
6. **The Old Names Return.** The Contessa's end; the Fourteen come down off the hill.
7. **The Stranger's Face.** You are drawn, or draw yourself. You are an Office, a year, unsayable.

And independently: whether the Bare Warrant's offer of a face is taken, what happens to Lacke, to Pell, to Pip, to
Rill and Cosimo, to Peck.

## The reward

Seeing the book through gives the company a **Face of its own**: a permanent white domino (the Warrant mask) that grants
the four skills of the four Guilds' trade-masks in turn (a capability worn by day), a share of the Registrar's own
*Rounds* in the form of a lasting endowment of a quality, a companion, and in a generated world the *freedom of the
city* (a place to rest and be supplied). Each calling's finale gives its own gift: a spell, a title, a trained bird, a
vow, a name sung in the Drip.

## What it is for

The adventure is about two goods in tension: that a city be kind to the unknown, and that it be able to know them. Ombra
solves the problem by making everyone unknown and then lying about it, and the lie turns out to be a person's whole life.
The satire is of anonymity as sanctity and transparency as virtue, of lotteries and of those who correct them; the romance
is two people who are loved by their masks; and the horror, such as there is, is the sound of a name cried in a square.
