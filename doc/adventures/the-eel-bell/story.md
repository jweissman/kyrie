# The Eel Bell — story draft (pass 1)

*Quillet lies on the Iscoed sea-bank, where the Water-Wardens' gates still hold the tide and no longer close. It lives by eels. Two winters ago, on the night of the Candlemas flood, its tide-bell, the Grandam, cracked, and seven boats did not come home.*

## Scene: the pit

A woman is shouting at a hole in the ground. It is the founding-pit on the church green, and she is Perpetua Sount, founder, sixty, who has cast the bells of three market towns and one cathedral door, and who has lost every note above the fourth fret to forty years of furnace.

> "Don't speak to me from the side. I take the left ear off a man's mouth and the right ear off his hands. Stand there. Good. I have a crucible, a pit, a mould of fine loam and goat-hair, and the wrong amount of everything else. You have ears. I'll trade you."

She does not say her last bell cracked at its first stroke, in Wenlow, with the bishop watching. Others will.

## Scene: the Grandam

Ansgar Dyce, last Warden of the Quillet gate, keeps the pieces of the old bell in the loft, laid out on sacking like a man laid out. His hands are scarred across the palms in a rope's pattern. The town says he let the Grandam crack by ringing it like a madman. He says nothing.

What he does not say: the bell cracked in the eleventh hour, and he went on ringing it, cracked, until the light, because a cracked bell still tells. Nineteen boats came home to that ugly voice. Seven did not hear it in time, or heard it too late.

Tobias Rushby lost his son Gil in the seven. He has not spoken to Dyce since, and he leans on the weir-rail and calls it "the Warden's racket".

## Scene: the lullaby

The Grandam had two voices: the strike, which carried over the wind; and under it a long low hum, which the silver eels, in their autumn run, come to like cattle to a gate. Quillet has called its eels with that hum for as long as there has been a Quillet. Nobody now alive can sing it except Posy Rushby, nine, Gil's daughter, who was put to sleep to it. She hums it to the cat. She does not want to hum it for the grown-ups. She wants to name the bell.

## The shape of the three days

The spring tide falls on the third night, and it is the night of the Silver Run: the whole fishery is out on the washes in the dark, thirty boats, as they must be, because the eels run once.

Day one: get metal and fuel. Copper from the eel-kettles, tin from the inn's pewter (ring each plate: the dull ones are lead), silver from Maudlin Greave, who will give her wedding spoons if her husband's name is on the shoulder. The Grandam's pieces, which are the best metal in the country, and Dyce will not hand them to a stranger.

Day two: pour at dawn. Cool. Break the mould. Sound it. A bell that is cast flat can be pared sharp; a bell cast sharp cannot be mended. Tune it by ear, with Posy's hum for the hum and your own for the strike.

Day three: haul it to the tower. Hang it. Find the ringers, because a bell is rung for six hours on a tide night, and one man alone cracked the last one.

## What can go wrong, and what can be beautiful

- A bell that is sweet, true and rung through the night brings thirty boats home, and then, at two in the morning, the eels come in over the weir-sills like poured silver, because the hum called them. Nobody has seen that in two winters. The whole town stands in the mud and does not make a sound.
- A bell that is true but wrong in the hum brings the boats home and sends the eels off down the channel. Quillet lives; it is a poor winter.
- A bell cast with too much tin, or with the sea-gate's iron chain in it because it seemed a fine thing to put the Warden's own iron in, cracks at the first stroke. If there are ringers, Dyce rings it anyway. It is hideous. It carries. They keep it.
- A town with no ringers hears its bell stop at three in the morning.
- A company that never cast anything may still light the Warden's cresset and bring some of the boats in by fire.

Perpetua cannot hear any of it. When the bell is hung and rung she stands on the tower stair and puts her palm on the stone. "Well?" she says. "Say it to me." The player has to tell her.
