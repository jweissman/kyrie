# An honest critique

*(The second pass, [11-second-pass.md](11-second-pass.md), answers the largest of these: the safe Commonplace, the
predictable session, the straw readings, the Almanack's middle days. What remains is said there.)*

*Written after playing every journey and reading every transcript end to end, as a reader who did not write it
would read it. What works first, briefly; then what does not, at length, because that is the useful part.*

## What works

- **The festival is the treaty.** The best idea survived every revision: a bonfire, a skipping rhyme, eels, last
  year's almanack thrown on the fire, are ordinary city life *and* the metaphysical stake. The Kindling scene in which
  nobody throws an almanack ("three thousand small voices saying, all at once, very quietly, 'Thank you,' and then,
  because they cannot help it, 'High water at the Hithe, half past six.'") is the moment the adventure exists for,
  and it is earned by eight days of the Almanack being a nuisance.
- **Evidence as persuasion.** The chamber is not a dialogue tree. A vote moves because the company *brought*
  something: a court's ruling, a dying man's letter, a ledger, the first words at the Wellhead, a soldier's oath.
  Reading the order paper before and after a lever is satisfying in a way that a persuasion roll never is.
- **The Wellhead reversal** lands: *WE ASK TO SLEEP* turns the Recall from tyranny into something more like
  bereavement, and makes the Unsewing's case harder without making it wrong.
- **Hester Rhule** is the character who changes, and she changes by what she is *shown*, which makes the player
  responsible for her. The Binder ending (a lie found out, her seal left on a table, a woman Sewing in a doorway on
  Needle Street) is the quietest and best of the three.
- **The comedy has costs.** The treatise suing its printer is funny and is also killing Gil Marrish's sheep; the
  Effusions are dreadful poems and are also the only book in Calamy that consents to the fire.

## What does not, or not yet

### The city is wide, but the middle is thin

Twenty-three small adventures is the right number; but about a third of them are **one or two conversations deep**
(the heron, the cheese, the overdue fine, the ferry licence, the lamplighter). Their *consequences* are real, which
is what the design asked; their *play* is a topic and an answer. The illusion of depth holds on a first pass and
thins on a second. The sluice, the bond, Pask's suit, the Awls and Corvell's library are the ones a player will
remember, because they have a night in them, or a tide, or a court.

### The Commonplace is better described than inhabited

Its places have one good image each (the beams of attention, the Foxing like an old map, marginalia in columns like
ants) and a member to talk to, and a reader moves through them as through a sequence of rooms with a voice in each.
There is **almost no danger** in the Plane: the Scholiasts, the Errata, the Black Letters. A first-time visitor never
feels that the Commonplace could keep them. The design's "wonder" act is wonder at the prose, not at what happens.
The Remainder Market's economy ("all prices in readings") is a sign on a board and one action (read aloud for an
hour); it should be a place you could be cheated in.

### The chamber is a puzzle with the answer written on the order paper

Because the order paper shows the count, and every member tells you its stance and (once asked) what would move it,
the Parliament is legible to the point of being a checklist. That was a deliberate choice against a black-box vote;
but it means the session itself (Kindling eve, noon) has **no surprises**: by the time you call it you know the
result. The counter-proposals the chamber makes when a reading fails are the only moments of resistance. A member
who changes its mind *in the chamber* because of what another member says (the Housebook walking out when the
Lexicon is freed is the one instance) would make it a scene instead of a tally.

### Some readings are straw

Nobody favours *the Censors' discretion*, and nobody can be brought to; *no reading owed* likewise. They exist for
symmetry. A player who notices will feel the shape of the designer's hand. Either a member should want them (the
Lexicon, for its own perverse reasons, could want the Censors back) or they should go.

### The Almanack is the heart, and is under-written in the middle days

Its first hour and its last are its best. Between, it is a gossip on a lead whose topics open leads elsewhere. The
one real choice (keep or tell what it heard in the Imprimatur's kitchen) is good but is made early and then carried.
It needed one more scene alone with the company, after the first Thursday burning, in which it says something it
has not said before.

### Prose

The voice is consistent (plain, concrete, a little dry) and mostly earns its length; but **arrival pages are long**,
because a room's scene, its things' states and its people's greetings are concatenated. The Reading Room arrival
after the reading is about four hundred words in one block. A few joins show: "a drawer slides out" twice in the
sealing; Rhule's name in a scene where she is not looking at you. And Kyrie's generic voice lines sometimes cut
across the adventure's register (the weather in the Commonplace, now fixed).

### Builds are distinguished in the chamber more than in the city

Most craft hooks are *things to say to a member*. In the city, only learning, healing, lockwork and wild change what
a protagonist can *do*. A soldier in Calamy is mostly a person who can swear an oath to an herbal. Ancestry matters
nowhere; spellcasters get nothing from their spells.

### The Manuals are well-earned and a little arbitrary

Corday, in the wild at the Fore-edge's edge, is a proper treasure at the end of a proper expedition. Quillon, handed
over by the chamber at the seal, is a prize for finishing, not for anything in particular. The Primer's lesson is the
most graceful, and the smallest.

### What would most improve it

1. One genuine **danger** in the Commonplace that can cost a member of the company something permanent. The
   Lexicon's ninth page costs the *city* (a freed Lexicon, a lost Advocate); nothing there costs the reader.
2. **One scene of argument between members** in the session, scripted on the state of the vote.
3. **Deepen four of the one-conversation errands** into two-step ones with a complication.
4. Cut or motivate the straw readings.
5. A second Almanack scene after the first burning.

## Against the commission

*A grand, original adventure whose world feels older and larger than its plot*: the world does; nine hundred years of
procedure, Pardoe's knife, the Saint Ide's bestiary and Agnes Hurd's cough are felt behind every scene. *Many small
interconnected adventures*: yes, with the caveat about depth above. *An overarching mystery solved by the player*:
yes, the Colophon is assembled, never recited. *A council worth negotiating with*: legible, varied, fair, and too
predictable on the day. *Different builds*: honoured in the chamber, thinner in the streets. *A character who
changes*: Rhule, yes; the Almanack, almost.
