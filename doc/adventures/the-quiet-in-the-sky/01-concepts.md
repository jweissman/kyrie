# The Quiet in the Sky — Stage 1: five concepts

*Written 31 October 2026, before reading Kyr's syntax closely, after reading the atlas, the repertoire survey
(`../repertoire-2026-10-26.md`) and the opening pages of all eighteen works in `doc/adventures` and of the six
`adventure/*` branches.*

The commission asks for somewhere whose geography, inhabitants, institutions, time, moral assumptions or material laws
are profoundly unlike Imbar's, and which a person would want to come back to. The repertoire asks for the things the
collection has not learned to say: wanting, fault, a person as an end, making, comedy as structure, fear that lasts,
the small that stays small, and above all minds changed by people rather than by exhibits. I drew five premises, and
gave each enough thought to see where its drama is.

---

## A. The Land of Counterpane

*A town of six hundred people living in the bedclothes of a giantess who has lain abed for two hundred years.*

On the shoulder of a fell stands a house the size of a mountain, built by the old kind of giant, a hundred times a
man's height. One of them is still in it, in bed. Refugees from a lowland war came in under the door in a hard winter
and stayed. Now a town lives in her room: the Counterpane, its wards the squares of her quilt; the Bolster, where the
Listeners sit by her ear; the Nightstand mesa with its lake of a water-jug and an observatory in one lens of her
spectacles; the Almshoe, a poorhouse in her left shoe. Her breath, once an hour, turns the mills on the bedposts. She
speaks a word a day at dawn, and her sentences, over two centuries, are the town's scripture: "The light is very yellow
today" has a college of commentators. Her giant cat sleeps by the hearth and hunts at night; the council has debated
belling it for fifty years. Beyond the shut door, the rest of the house is a wilderness: the Kitchen, the Stair, the
Attic with her children's heights notched on the nursery door.

The plot: her new sentence begins *When I* — and the town waits for *die*, and gets *rise*.

**Its promise.** Scale as a material law; Colossal Cave in a kitchen; a theocracy built on an old woman's small talk,
which is satire of anyone who makes doctrine of a parent's remarks; the fable of belling the cat, actually done; a
person, not a mechanism, at the centre.

**Why not.** Read against the collection it collides three ways. *The Banns at Hessel* already has a vast old woman,
a heart giving out, a fortune and a physician quietly doubling her drops. *Half the Hive* already has a town living
inside a host that is about to move away and take half the town with it. *The Lull* already has a dying old woman whose
last wish the household must decide whether to grant. Each idea could be turned; all three at once would make a fourth
work out of the parts of three. The scale law is the one thing here no other work has, and I keep it in a drawer.

## B. The Survey of the Unfixed

*A country where land that nobody has mapped is redrawn every night, and the Board of Survey makes it real by drawing
it.*

East of the settled shires the Margin begins: woods, fens and hills that are not the same two mornings running. What is
drawn on the Great Survey and sealed stays where it was drawn. Farmers need fixed ground; the Board's surveyors walk out
into the Margin with chains and theodolites and fix it, a mile a season. The Marginfolk live by what grows only in
unfixed country: the night-orchards, the wandering wells, the hares that are a different hare each dawn. A protagonist
hired as a chain-boy learns that the line is a kind of violence and that the Margin is also hungry.

**Its promise.** It is the question the atlas leaves open (§18, §26.3: "At what point does a generated possibility
become committed history?") made into a law of nature; it would be the first authored work built around exploration for
its own sake; mapping would be the protagonist's making.

**Why not.** Its drama is a colonial argument with one obvious side, and the people in it would be positions. It is
also a work about the engine, which is a kind of cleverness that admires itself. It could be very good. It would be
cold.

## C. Cloudhallow

*A mountain republic that makes and sells weather.*

Clouds herded on the high pastures like sheep; fog distilled in copper in the valleys and sold by the jar; hail cured in
cellars; the Rain Exchange, where lowland kingdoms buy their summers by contract and the price of a wet May can break a
house. Somebody has stolen a season. Weather is already a thing Kyr can say (a scenery state that other things follow),
and a jar of fog in a thief's pack is a lovely tool: fog to hide in, thunder to scatter a mob, rain to put out a fire.

**Its promise.** Livelihood everywhere; comedy of commerce; a dozen approaches to every problem through what you carry.

**Why not.** A caper with a nice toy. I could not find the person in it whose life it changes.

## D. The Long Sleep of Hethe

*A northern city that sleeps through the winter in a vault, and the stranger hired to keep it.*

Every autumn the whole of Hethe goes down into the Dormitory under the hill and sleeps until the thaw, and a winter-watch
of a dozen strangers, paid in advance, keeps the city: the fires banked, the wolves out, the sleepers turned. Something
in the vault is waking people one at a time. Fear in a bounded place for weeks, the danger partly the people you are
shut in with; scarcity of fuel and food as the pressure.

**Its promise.** It is the repertoire's "fear that lasts" exactly.

**Why not.** The collection has three winters already (Carth, Pellam, the fell in *Fair Weather*'s north), and horror
in a vault is the most familiar shape in the genre. It answers a list rather than a vision.

## E. The Quiet in the Sky

*In Ysgard, where the warrior dead have fought every day for nine hundred years and risen healed every dusk, one man did
not get up. Since then nobody has fought. It has been quiet for forty days, and nobody up there knows how to live.*

On the Imbar side of a high pass lies Larum, a hamlet that for nine centuries has heard, on still days, a sound in the
snow above it like a smithy the size of the sky. It stopped forty days ago, and Larum cannot sleep for the quiet.

Over the pass is the Field: a white plain between two halls, Morrowhall on the eastern height and Evenhall on the west.
Warriors of Imbar who die under arms come in out of the snow at the hour they died, and are sorted by it: before noon,
Morrow; after noon, Even. Comrades who fell in the same battle an hour apart have fought each other ever since. Every
morning the horns, every day the Daily, every dusk the Mending, when what was done to them is undone and the fallen
stand up. What they make does not mend: nine centuries of broken arms lie in the ice of the Field in layers, and you can
look down through clear ice at the swords of wars nobody now remembers. Food is the Ox, eaten every night and standing
whole every morning, because an ox is flesh and flesh mends. Bread does not rise, because yeast is alive and nothing
alive keeps in Ysgard. *Nothing rises in Ysgard but the dead.*

Forty-one days ago Halvard Eld, Evenhall's champion, fell in the Daily and did not get up at dusk. Hild Varley, Marshal of
Morrow, blew the Halt, a call the horns had in them and had never sounded. Since then the Field has been quiet out of
fear: if death has come back to Ysgard, then fighting is fighting again. In the no-man's-land around Halvard's body,
under guard where he fell, a town has grown out of shields and spears in forty days: Halt. A berserker is trying to
bake. A skald who has made nine hundred years of battle-songs cannot make another kind. A pikewoman of Morrow and a
knight of Even who have killed each other some three thousand times do not know how to stand next to each other without
weapons. The despised dead who died running, the Runners, who carry and clear for both halls, have noticed that peace
needs them more than war did.

And the protagonist, who came up the pass alive, is the only person on the Field whose breath smokes in the cold.

**The drama.** Halvard was killed by a living woman, with a knife, for what he did to her family before he died. The
law nobody up there remembers is that a living hand kills truly in Ysgard. If the truth comes out, the dead are safe
from each other again, and the war can resume. Torold Hame, Halvard's sworn brother, has called a Testing: in five
mornings Evenhall goes down onto the Field to fight, and if every man gets up at dusk, the Daily resumes. He wants it
because he believes the Mending stopped when the fighting did, and that Halvard will rise when it starts again. Hild,
who blew the Halt, wants it never to be known, because the peace stands on fear and she has come to love the peace. The
truth here does not save anyone. What the dead do once they know it depends on whether forty days have given them a
life they would rather keep, and whether Torold can be given a way to grieve.

**Its promise.**

- A moral law unlike Imbar's: violence is cheap and undone, so only what is made and what is promised has weight.
- A society of warriors from every age of Imbar's wars, so its deep history is carried by people who can be asked, and
  who contradict each other.
- The demobilisation question the atlas records as the owner's own (§6): how a war machine returns to civic life, its
  songs and crafts and caste and loyalties.
- Courtship between intimate enemies.
- The first funeral in a heaven.
- A protagonist whose nature (alive) changes what every act of theirs means. The protagonist is the most dangerous
  person in heaven, and both sides will want to use them.
- A parish to come back to: Larum, the warm inn at the foot of a cold pass, with an out-and-back loop that has a
  material reason (the cold).

**What could go wrong.** It is another plane, and the repertoire calls a plane an escalation reflex. It is snow again.
It has a shape like *The Lull*'s: a community in a perpetual state that must choose. It risks Norse pastiche. These
are taken up in the critique.

---

## The choice

I chose **E**.

- **A** is the most charming, and it is three other works' parts.
- **B** is the cleverest, and it is about the engine.
- **C** is the most fun, and it is about a toy.
- **D** answers a list.
- **E** is the only one where I could see the scene that would make someone put the game down for a moment: the horn
  not sounding on the morning after the Testing, and who is and is not on the Field. It is also the only one where the
  setting's law *is* its moral question rather than a backdrop to one. Where nothing done to a body lasts, what is
  worth doing? The minor stories answer it: bread, a song, a marriage, a wage, a grave.

It is also what the commission asked for in its plainest form: a place whose moral assumptions are profoundly unlike
Imbar's, situated in Imbar's own canonical cosmology. Ysgard is one of Orsino's nine outer planes, which the canon
already carries, with one sentence of lore ("a frigid, mountainous realm of valor and heroism, where mighty warriors
clash amidst snowstorms") and a law that weakens spells (`plane_ysgard`). No adventure has yet built any of it.
