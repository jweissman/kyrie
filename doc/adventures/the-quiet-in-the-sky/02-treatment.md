# The Quiet in the Sky — Stage 1: the treatment

*Written before accommodating the engine. Ambitious on purpose; the critique (03) cuts it, and the revised design (04)
is what was built.*

## The pitch

For nine hundred years the hamlet of Larum, under the Larum Pass, has lived with a sound. On still days it comes down
out of the snow on the pass: the clangour, iron on iron, ten thousand smithies, faint as a bell three valleys off.
Larum's children are rocked to it. Its chapel rings a bell at dusk when the sound stops for the day, the Mending Bell,
and nobody remembers why. Forty days ago the sound did not start. It has not started since. Larum cannot sleep.

Over the pass, in Ysgard, the dead of Imbar's wars have stopped fighting, because one of them died.

## I. Geography

### The Imbar side: Larum and the Pass

Larum sits at the head of a long valley in a mountain country, where the road from the lowlands gives up. One street
of stone houses with turf on their roofs, a chapel with a squat tower, an inn, a bakehouse, the guide's house, a
smithy that mends Picker's tools, and sheep. Above it the Pass goes up between two shoulders of rock into a snowfield
that is always in cloud, even in August: the White. People who go up into the White without Jory Whitlock come down
lost, or frostbitten, or not at all, and some come down with a story.

Larum lives on four things: sheep; the guide's trade; antique arms (Ysgard's Pickers bring the broken arms of nine
centuries to the head of the pass and swap them for tobacco, needles, salt and news, and a collector from the lowlands
buys them by the cartload); and its ballads. Larum makes the songs that the lowlands sing about the champions of the
Field. *The Ballad of Halvard Eld*, a penny a sheet, has sold in every market town in three countries. Heaven has
celebrities, and Larum is where their fame is printed.

### The Ysgard side: the Field

The White thins, and you come out on a height above a plain under a sky the colour of a knife. The light is low and
does not move much. It is cold enough to hurt your teeth. You are in Ysgard.

- **The Coming-in.** At the head of the pass on the Ysgard side the snow comes down in a curtain, and out of it the new
  dead walk, one or two an hour, bewildered, still bloody, at the hour they died. A booth of planks and banners stands
  there with a brazier nobody needs and a rack of hourglasses: Cleeve, the Teller, a dead herald with a ledger, asks
  each one the hour of their death and sends them east or west. He is extremely surprised by people who are breathing.
- **The Field.** A plain five miles across between two heights, white, wind-scoured, littered as far as you can see
  with what nine hundred years of daily battle leave behind: swords, shield-bosses, helms, spear-heads, banners frozen
  stiff, a siege-tower with snow on its top platform, the ribs of a war-cart. Things that are made do not mend.
  - Where the snow has been scoured off, the Field is ice, and in places it is clear: **the Clear**. Looking down
    through it is like looking down through deep water at the floor of history. Near the top, crossbow bolts and pikes
    of the last century; lower, mail and long axes; lower, bronze; at the bottom, so deep they are green shadows,
    shapes nobody can name.
  - A frozen river crosses the Field from north to south, **the Lathe**. The Daily always began with the two hosts
    racing for its ford.
- **Halt.** In the middle of the Field, where Halvard fell, a town forty days old. Its walls are shields stuck rim-down
  in the snow. Its houses are tents of banners on spear-poles, the banners of a hundred dead wars. There is a market at
  a crossing of two lanes (Trophy Row, where the dead swap the things they carried), an alehouse in a siege-tower lying
  on its side (**the Truce**), Grimbold's bakehouse in a war-mantlet (**the Oven**), and in the centre a ring of
  planted spears, and inside it, on a bier of shields, Halvard Eld, under a frost that does not melt, with four of
  Evenhall's men standing over him day and night. People walk round the ring on their way through the town, and lower
  their voices, the way people do past a church.
- **Morrowhall.** On the eastern height, a long hall of black timber roofed with shields, the dawn behind it. Its door
  faces west, down onto the Field. Inside: the long fire; the benches; the Tally-boards on which every kill of nine
  hundred years is cut; the Ox, Brindle, and its spit; the Marshal's room behind the high seat; the barracks lofts.
  Morrow's dead are the ones who fell before noon: dawn raids, morning assaults, men who died at first light.
- **Evenhall.** On the western height, its mirror: grey stone, a hall that the sunset fills. Inside: the same, but its
  ox is a dun one, and it has the Field's great forge, where Gorm Hallett has mended Even's mail every night for four
  hundred years.
- **The Scrapes.** Under the Field's southern edge, where the ice overhangs, the Runners have dug and built a warren of
  shields and broken carts and banners, warm with nothing, lit with fat-lamps: where those who died running live.
  They clear the Field for both halls each night (dragging the broken off the battle-line so the next day's fighting has
  footing), mend nothing, are owed nothing, and are spat on by both. They keep the Picking: the trade with Larum.
- **The Old Ice.** North, where the Field rises into a glacier: blue, cracked, slow. A stair cut in the ice by nobody
  knows whom goes up to a cave, and in the cave sit **the Sitters**: ten of the oldest dead in Ysgard, from before the
  halls were built, who refused the Daily seven hundred years ago and have sat under the ice playing a game of stones
  ever since, forgotten by everyone. Their bear, a war-bear of a dwarven host, dead for nine centuries, wanders the
  ice below them.
- **The Teeth.** South-west, a line of black crags. Frost wolves; a cairn on the highest tooth from which on a clear
  day you can see, impossibly, the lowland of Imbar lying like a map under glass.

### Weather, light and sound

Ysgard here has no seasons: a low white sun, long blue dusks, snow out of a clear sky. The dead do not feel cold and do
not steam when they breathe, which is how anyone can tell the living. The Mending comes at dusk. The light goes blue
all at once, as if somebody had put a glass over the sun, and every wound closes. For nine hundred years that was when
the fallen stood up off the Field.

Sound carries strangely. In Ysgard you can hear Larum's chapel bell, very faint, at dusk, from the head of the pass.

## II. History (most of which nobody in it agrees on)

- **The First Host.** The Sitters remember a time before the halls, when the dead came in out of the snow and there
  were few of them, and they built a fire and sat round it. Their account of how the war began: the dawn-comers and the
  dusk-comers quarrelled over a game of stones (Tabula, the Aenean board game), over whether a man who came in at noon
  should play first or last. Morrowhall's account: Even refused the dawn its due. Evenhall's: Morrow struck first, at
  dawn, as it always does. Cleeve's ledger begins in the middle of a sentence.
- **The Sorting.** Since the war began, the dead have been sent to the halls by the hour they died. It is not a law of
  the plane, only a custom so old nobody can imagine it otherwise. Brothers who died in one battle an hour apart, one
  before noon and one after, have fought each other every day for centuries. The halls have a word for it: *hour-kin*.
- **The Daily.** Every morning at the horn the hosts came down from the heights, met on the Field, and fought until
  dusk. Every kill was cut on the Tally-boards. Champions rose and fell in fame; Larum printed their ballads. Nobody
  stayed dead. Nobody won.
- **Ithel the Smith.** The Sitters, and only they, remember that three hundred years ago a living man came up the pass, a
  smith looking for his son among the dead, found him in Morrow, quarrelled with an Even man, and killed him with a
  hammer. The Even man did not rise. The Sitters buried him in the Old Ice. Ithel went home and died of grief in Larum,
  and Larum began ringing a bell at dusk without knowing why. Nobody else remembers. The Sitters told nobody, because
  nobody asked, because nobody has asked the Sitters anything in seven hundred years.
- **Forty-one days ago.** Halvard Eld, the most famous champion of the age, was killed in the Daily by a knife at the
  nape, and lay in the snow at dusk while the Mending went over the Field and everyone else stood up. Hild Varley, who
  was nearest, blew the Halt. Torold Hame held Even back. Since then the horns have been silent.

## III. Cultures and institutions

- **The halls** are war-cultures without a war: ranks by Tally; feasts; boasting-rites; oaths. Every man and woman in
  them is a professional killer of the first rank, with centuries of practice, and most are bored out of their minds.
  Morrow is brisk, loud, dawn-minded and practical; Even is formal, melancholy and proud of its manners. They despise each
  other with the intimacy of an old marriage.
- **Hour-kin**, the dead's own words for kinship across the line. Mab and Ansel are not hour-kin; they are worse:
  *old partners*, the halls' term for two who always find each other on the Field.
- **The Runners** are the dead who died fleeing. Ysgard took them anyway, and the halls made them a caste. They wear no
  arms, sit at no bench, are owed nothing, carry the broken off the Field each night, and have run the Picking trade
  for centuries. Their foreman, Cuddy Wardle, died at sixteen running from a battle he did not choose to be in.
- **Halt** is a new institution with no institutions: it has a market, an alehouse and a bakehouse, and none of them has
  a law. Everyone in it is from somewhere else, and everyone is improvising a life.
- **Cleeve's Office of the Hour** at the Coming-in is Ysgard's only bureaucracy, a man and a ledger.
- **The Mending Bell** at Larum: a custom whose reason is lost.
- **The ballad trade** at Larum: heaven's fame, printed on Imbar.

### Food, craft and art

The dead eat the Ox, and drink meltwater and a mead whose honey comes from nobody knows where (the Runners know: they
trade for it at the pass). Under the Truce they want bread, and grain must come up from Larum, and bread will not rise.
Their arts are war-arts: the skalds' Tally-songs, the boast, the shield-painting. In forty days of quiet a few of them
have begun to make other things, badly: a chair, a doll, a drawing of a face. Things made stay made.

## IV. People

### The Field

- **Torold Hame**, Warden of Evenhall. A big quiet man two hundred years dead, Halvard's sworn brother. He took Halvard
  in when Halvard came in out of the snow twelve years ago, and loved him, whatever word you use. He stands at the ring
  most of every day. He has a theory, and he holds it as a drowning man holds wood: the Mending came with the Daily, and
  when the Daily stopped, the Mending stopped for Halvard. Start the fighting again and Halvard will stand up. He has
  called the **Testing**, in five mornings. He does not want war. He wants his friend back. Changed by: a way to grieve
  (a funeral); the truth (it destroys his theory and leaves him nothing but grief); Edith's account of what Halvard
  was.
- **Hild Varley**, Marshal of Morrowhall. Four hundred years dead, a bridge-defender, careful, dry, humane. She blew the
  Halt because she was afraid, and has never told anyone that the bravest thing she has done in four centuries was an
  act of cowardice. She has come to love the peace: the sound of people talking on the Field instead of dying on it.
  She does not want to know what killed Halvard. If she learns that only the living kill, she will see at once that the
  Testing will end the peace, and that one more true death at it would save the peace; and that there is now a living
  person on the Field. Changed by: being told she need not be afraid to choose peace; being refused; being shown what
  Halt is.
- **Edith Marl**, living, a fuller's widow from a lowland town that Halvard Eld's free company burned twenty years ago
  in life, when he was a mercenary captain. Her husband and her daughter died in it. Halvard died twelve years ago in
  another war and went, she learned from a penny ballad, to glory in Ysgard. She came up the pass with Larum's pickers'
  carts, dressed as a Runner, and killed him in the Daily with a fuller's knife. She is hiding in the Scrapes. She
  shivers. Her breath steams. Cuddy Wardle knows what she is and hides her, because she is the first person in Ysgard
  who ever did a Runner's work and then did something else. She is not sorry. She would like to go home.
- **Mab Treloar**, pikewoman of Morrow, two hundred years dead, laughing, coarse, kind. **Ansel Haverlock**, knight of
  Even, six hundred years dead, grave, courtly, shy. They have killed each other something like three thousand times.
  Each always looked for the other on the Field. In the Truce they cannot be in the same room. Each believes the other
  wants the war back, because it was the only way they had of meeting.
- **Grimbold Ure**, berserker of Morrow, five hundred years dead, a mountain of a man with hands like loaves, who
  wanted to bake when he was alive, and was conscripted instead. His bread will not rise.
- **Wystan Hollins**, skald of Evenhall, eight hundred years dead, who has made nine hundred years of battle-songs and
  is asked now to make a song for the peace, and cannot, because he does not remember what anything is for except
  fighting. He drinks in the Truce and is rude to everybody.
- **Cuddy Wardle**, foreman of the Runners. Died at sixteen, a hundred and fifty years ago, running. Shrewd, sour,
  funny, a born organiser who has never been allowed to organise. Has noticed that the halls cannot fight without the
  Runners clearing the Field, and that nobody has noticed.
- **Gorm Hallett**, Evenhall's armourer. Four hundred years of mending mail every night. The peace is killing his trade
  and his reason for being. He wants the war back and says so, and is secretly writing to Larum for living hired
  swords, because a rumour has reached him that the living are different.
- **Cleeve**, the Teller at the Coming-in, officious and kind, who has sorted a quarter of a million dead by their hour.
- **Ned Noone**, a newcomer, a carter's boy from the lowlands killed yesterday in a skirmish, who came in out of the
  snow at noon exactly, so that Cleeve cannot sort him. Neither hall will have him. He is frightened, funny, and
  anxious to know whether his sister got home. He can come with the protagonist anywhere in Ysgard. He cannot go down the
  pass: the dead who go down into the White lie down in it.
- **Aulus**, eldest of the Sitters, a legionary of the Aenean world, who speaks the old tongue and a little of the new,
  and plays Tabula.

### Larum

- **Bet Garside**, who keeps the Hark, Larum's inn. Slept to the clangour for forty years and cannot sleep without it.
  Remembers a woman who came through two months ago asking about Halvard Eld.
- **Jory Whitlock**, the guide. Laconic, honest, expensive. Carries letters up for the dead's families. Knows the White.
- **Master Ambler**, a collector of heaven-iron from the lowlands, lodging at the Hark: cheerful, greedy, learned, buys
  anything from the Field.
- **Nance Pellett**, Larum's baker, whose sourdough mother is two hundred years old.

## V. The smaller stories

1. **Nothing Rises.** Grimbold's bread will not rise. The protagonist can find out why (the yeast dies in the cold:
   nothing alive keeps in Ysgard), fetch Nance's sourdough mother from Larum, and carry it up the pass inside their coat
   against their own warm body, the only warm body for miles, so that it lives. The first leavened loaf in Ysgard.
   Grimbold weeps. Bread becomes Halt's sign.
2. **Three Thousand Times.** Mab and Ansel. Get them into one room; untangle the misunderstanding; teach them to talk
   without weapons (Ansel's idea of courtship is a formal challenge); the halls' disapproval; the first wedding in
   Ysgard, in Halt, with Grimbold's bread and Wystan's song if there is one.
3. **The Unwritten Song.** Wystan cannot write peace. He needs things he has forgotten: what a market sounds like,
   what a child is for, what a field is when it is not a battlefield. The protagonist brings him Larum (its bell, its
   bread, its sheep), and the song is made, or Wystan writes instead the great elegy for Halvard that will start the war.
4. **The Runners' Due.** Cuddy Wardle sees that the halls cannot fight on a Field nobody clears. A strike, a wage, a seat
   on a bench: the protagonist can help them bargain, threaten, or win their due through the Picking's trade.
5. **The Noonman.** Ned Noone, unsortable. Neither hall will take him; he is no Runner. Find where he belongs, and
   take word of him to his sister in the lowlands.
6. **The Sitters' Game.** Find the Sitters; play Aulus at Tabula for what he knows; learn the origin of the war, the
   story of Ithel the Smith, and the Old Ice burial; bring the Sitters down onto the Field for the first time in seven
   hundred years.
7. **Letters from the Field.** A carrier's trade: the dead give a living carrier letters for their families in Imbar,
   and Jory pays by the letter. Some letters are terrible. One is for Bet Garside.
8. **Heaven-iron.** Picking in the Field and the Clear for Ambler's money: the history of Imbar in the ice, each piece
   a story the dead will tell if you show it to them.
9. **Swords into Ploughs.** Gorm, given a reason, can make the Field's first plough out of nine centuries of swords;
   or he brings up the living mercenaries.

## VI. The principal story

**Act I: The Quiet.** The protagonist hears in Larum that the war in the sky has stopped, goes up the pass, is sorted
by an astonished Cleeve ("Hour of death? You're breathing"), and finds Halt. Wonder and comedy: the dead learning to
be at peace. The body in the ring. Torold's grief. At dusk of the first day Torold names the Testing: in five
mornings.

**Act II: The Breather.** The protagonist's living state becomes known. Investigation of Halvard's death runs through
the minor stories: his wound (a knife's, at the nape, its edges as they were made, while every old wound on him is
closed without a scar); the Runners (a new Runner that day who never came back to the Scrapes); the breath that steams
in the warren; the Sitters' story of Ithel. The midpoint is not a solution but a dread: *a living hand kills truly here*,
and the protagonist is living. The truth will end the peace. Hild, if she learns it, asks the protagonist to kill one
man at the Testing.

**Act III: The Testing.** At dawn of the fifth day Even comes down onto the Field to fight itself. What happens
depends on who knows what, and what each has become:

- Torold given a grave to stand at may not come down at all.
- The Runners on strike leave a Field nobody can fight on.
- A living hand at the Testing makes the fear true again.
- If they fight and all rise, the question is what the morning after holds.

**Act IV: The Morning After.** The horn, or no horn. Who is on the Field.

## VII. Endings

1. **The Daily.** They fought, they rose, and in the morning the horns blew. Halt is trampled. In Larum, the clangour is
   back, and Bet Garside sleeps.
2. **Halt.** They fought, they rose, they know, and in the morning they stay at their benches and their ovens: peace
   chosen without fear. In Larum, silence; the Mending Bell still rings, and now everyone knows why.
3. **The Kept Fear.** A living hand killed at the Testing, or Torold vanished before dusk: the peace stands on fear,
   and the protagonist knows what it stands on.
4. **Half a Field.** Even goes back to the Daily and fights itself; Morrow keeps Halt. A war with one side.
5. **The Breathers' War.** The truth sold to Gorm: the halls hire the living, and Ysgard learns real war.

And Edith: home, handed over (killed, and coming in out of the snow at the hour she died, to be sorted among her
enemy's comrades forever), confessing at the ring, or hidden.

## VIII. Themes

- **What lasts.** Where nothing done to a body is permanent, only what is made, promised or mourned has weight. Every
  minor story is something made: bread, a song, a marriage, a wage, a grave.
- **The truth that does not save.** The collection's minds change when shown things. Here showing the truth endangers
  the peace, and what saves it is grief given a form, love given a room, and work given a wage.
- **Demobilisation.** How a war becomes a civic life; who it needed and never paid; what its songs were for.
- **The arbitrary line.** The hour of death decides a side forever. Satire of nations, birth and every other sorting.
- **Celebrity of killers.** Larum prints the ballads; Edith read one.

## IX. Why go

Because a town of the dead from every century of Imbar's wars, forty days old, built of their shields, improvising
bread and courtship and wages under a knife-coloured sky, is a place you want to walk through slowly. Because you can
look down through the ice at the swords of forgotten wars and then ask a man who carried one. Because the living
protagonist's breath smokes and every dead face turns. Because Larum, below, has a fire and a bell and a woman who
cannot sleep, and you will come down cold and want it.
