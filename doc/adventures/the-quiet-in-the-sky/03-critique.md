# The Quiet in the Sky — Stage 2: the editor's attack

*The treatment (02) read as a demanding literary editor and an old-school designer would read it, before a line of
Kyr. The verdicts are blunt on purpose. What survived, and how, is in the revised design (04).*

## Is the setting distinctive?

Mostly. A heaven of warriors is old furniture: Valhalla, the einherjar, the boar eaten every night. What the treatment
adds is better than its furniture:

- **sorting by the hour of death**, which makes the war arbitrary and gives the halls a word, *hour-kin*;
- the **Runners**, a caste of those who died fleeing;
- **the Clear**, history visible as strata of arms;
- the **Coming-in** and its bureaucrat;
- **Halt**, a town built of shields in forty days;
- the **moral law**: wounds mend, makings do not.

**Cut the Valhalla residue.** No mead-hall cliché, no ravens, no "Allfather". The ox stays, because it can be made
particular. Brindle is a dead siege-ox who died hauling a ram, so technically a warrior, and the joke earns it. The halls
must not feel like one hall with two names. Morrow is timber, dawn and briskness; Even is stone, sunset and manners. They
should be told apart by a player who has stood in both for a minute.

**Snow again.** Ysgard's cold is canonical, so it cannot be changed, but it must not be a third Pellam or Carth. Make
it a cold that matters only to the living. The dead stand about in it in shirtsleeves. The protagonist's cold is a
material pressure (it drives them back to the Oven, or down to Larum) and a sign: the steaming breath. Carth's cold is
a penance and Pellam's is a festival. This one marks who is alive.

## Does it feel inhabited apart from the plot?

Halt does, on paper. The halls do not yet: they are described as war-cultures and given no ordinary day. What does a
dead pikewoman do at ten in the morning in the fortieth day of a truce? Give each hall a daily shape: the Tally-boards
being argued over, sparring in the yard because nobody can stop, the Ox, the boast-rite at night. And give the dead
something to do with their hands in Halt: Trophy Row, where nothing is bought because nothing can be, and everything is
swapped.

## Are the small stories substantial and varied?

Five are. Four are not.

- **Nothing Rises**: good. It has a reason, a physical act (carrying the yeast against the skin), a joke, a wonder and a
  consequence. Keep it exactly.
- **Three Thousand Times**: good premise, thin plot. "Untangle the misunderstanding" is a sitcom summary. It needs a
  complication and a reversal. Ansel's idea of courtship is a formal challenge to single combat, which Mab, who has
  fought him three thousand times, takes as an insult. And the halls forbid it: a wedding across the line is
  desertion. What does it cost them? Mab's place at Morrow's bench.
- **The Unwritten Song**: good premise, no play. "Bring him things" is a fetch. It must be *making*: the protagonist
  answers Wystan's questions, and what they choose to tell him is what goes into the song. A song made of a sheep, a
  bell and a boy is different from a song made of Halvard's death.
- **The Runners' Due**: promising. It needs one decisive lever, not a negotiation menu. The lever is that nobody has
  cleared the Field for forty days, because there has been nothing to clear. A Testing on an uncleared Field cannot be
  fought: nine hundred years of iron under the snow. If the Runners will not clear, there is no Testing. That puts a
  caste's strike at the centre of the main plot, which is right.
- **The Noonman**: thin as written. A frightened boy whom nobody will sort is a premise, not a story. **The revision
  this needed is the best idea in the critique**: Ned Noone is from Larum. He is Jory Whitlock's son. Jory, the guide
  who takes the protagonist up the pass, is waiting in Larum for a boy who is three days overdue from the lowland
  market. The protagonist meets the boy dead at the Coming-in an hour after Jory has turned back at the head of the pass.
  - The earlier scene changes meaning.
  - Larum is joined to the Field by a person.
  - Grief is lived through, not described: what does the protagonist say to Jory?
  - And it rhymes with Ithel the Smith, who came up the pass three hundred years ago looking for his son, killed a man,
    and went home to die. The Mending Bell is Ithel's bell. Jory will want to go up. What will Jory do?
- **The Sitters' Game**: good, but in danger of being the collection's old woman with the key (the repertoire's
  warning). Aulus must not be wise. He is petty, competitive, very funny about a seven-hundred-year-old grudge over a
  board game, and the Ithel story comes out of him sideways, as gossip, while he is losing.
- **Letters from the Field**: fold into Ned. A general postal trade is a nice idea and an errand. Keep one letter.
- **Heaven-iron**: keep as livelihood, not story. Picking pays, and each find can be shown to the dead, who remember.
- **Swords into Ploughs**: cut as a story; keep Gorm as a voice of the war party and a smith who can be turned. Cut his
  living mercenaries and the *Breathers' War* ending entirely. They are an escalation with a single villain's
  motivation, and they make the law a weapon in a way that pulls the work toward a war thriller.

## Is the principal story compelling, and does it grow from the setting?

Yes, for one reason: **the truth makes things worse**. That inverts the collection's grain, in which minds change when
shown things, and it grows straight out of the law. But Act II as written is *again an investigation*, the
collection's habit: a wound, a witness, a clue, a lore-keeper, a confession. Four of the five steps are exhibits.

**Revision.** The law can be learned three ways, and one of them is by fault.

- The protagonist spars with a dead fighter (Morrow's yard does it all day out of boredom, and asks the breather in), or
  is drawn into a brawl. A cut they give does not close at dusk. The dead fighter carries the first scar in Ysgard for
  the rest of eternity.
- The protagonist learns what they are here by what they have done. Not by reading.
- The other two routes are the old ones, kept because a careful player who never fights must also be able to find it:
  Halvard's wound read by someone who can read the dead, and the Sitters' story of Ithel; or Edith herself.

**Hild's request** (kill one man at the Testing so the fear holds) is the best scene in the treatment and must not be
softened. Give it a real price, Torold, and real alternatives: refuse; agree and not do it; stage a vanishing.

## Characters: conflicting motives, inner lives

- **Torold**: excellent. His war-wish is grief-denial. Keep him off-stage-wise: he should speak little.
- **Hild**: excellent. Her secret is that her bravest act was cowardice. Do not let her explain it. Let it come out
  when the protagonist tells her the truth and she goes white, because her first thought is *then it can be done again*.
- **Edith**: needs a scene with Torold, or the work flinches from its own question. Halvard burned a town. Torold's
  friend was a murderer in life. Torold knows (he must; the ballads say it, cheerfully). What does he say to the woman?
- **Mab and Ansel**: see above.
- **Grimbold**: a gentle giant who wants to bake is close to a stock type. Earn him: he was conscripted at fifteen out
  of his father's bakehouse; he has not touched dough in five hundred years; he does not believe it will work, and does
  it every morning anyway, which is a kind of faith the work can respect.
- **Wystan**: rude, drunk, blocked. Stock again. Give him one surprise: he does remember a song that is not about war,
  and is ashamed of it, a lullaby he sang to nobody, from before he died. It is the seed of the peace song.
- **Cuddy**: good. Make sure she wants something for herself: a seat at a bench, once. Just to see what it's like.
- **Jory**: now the work's quiet centre. He must not be consoled.
- **The protagonist**: still wants nothing. Partly fixed by being alive (they are the most dangerous person in heaven,
  and are courted for it). Fixed further by Ned and Jory: they are carrying news no one should carry, and they can be
  at fault (the scar).

## Generic furniture

- An inn called "the Ringing Ear" was a bad joke; the treatment's "the Hark" is better.
- A bear wandering the Old Ice is decoration unless it does something. Give it the Sitters: it is theirs, and
  killing it with a living hand would be a cruelty they never forgive.
- The Teeth: frost wolves, and a view. That is what a crag should be; keep it modest.
- The Tellers: one Teller.

## Alternatives and consequences

The endings table has five entries; one is cut. The remaining four must come from causes, not from a menu at the end.
What decides each hall is a person:

- Even follows **Torold**: a funeral given him, or the truth with Edith before him, and he stands down.
- Morrow follows **Hild**: afraid, she wants the fear kept, and if she cannot have it, she goes back to the war she
  understands; given courage, she keeps the peace without fear.
- The Field is fightable only if **Cuddy** has it cleared.

The town's forty days of life (bread, a song, a marriage, a wage) should not be a counter that unlocks the best
ending. They should be *what the Morning After shows*: who is at the Oven, who is singing, who is married, who is on a
bench. A thin Halt makes a thin peace, and a full one makes a full one. The page must say which.

## Tonal contrast

The treatment is grave throughout, with comedy as garnish. Comedy needs structure:

- **Cleeve's bureaucracy** meeting a breather, again and again: every time the protagonist comes in by the pass he asks
  their hour of death, and grows more distressed that they have none.
- **The wedding** as a comedy of complications, with two halls' protocols, a ceremony nobody has ever seen, and a baker
  with dough on his hands as the only witness who will stand for both.
- **Larum's insomnia**: Bet Garside has taken to banging two pans at the window at night.

Horror needs vulnerability. The protagonist has it once the law is known: in a hall of killers, you are the one who
cannot get up. The Testing, if the protagonist goes onto the Field, is a fight that cannot safely be fought.

## Themes through action

"What lasts" is carried by makings. Good. "The truth that does not save" is carried by Hild and Torold. Good. "The
arbitrary line" is carried by the sorting and by Ned, who has no hour. Good. "Celebrity of killers" is carried by
Larum's ballad, if the ballad is in the player's hands: Bet sells it, Edith bought it, Torold knows it by heart and
does not sing it.

## Would I want to go there knowing nothing of its plot?

Yes:

- for the Clear;
- for Halt's lanes of banners;
- for a pikewoman and a knight who cannot be in a room together;
- for the warm Oven in a cold heaven;
- for Larum at dusk with a bell.

I would want to go back down to Larum and come up again, which is the loop the game's README promises and no authored
work has been built around.

## The escalation reflex

It is a plane. It is not a descent, not a machine, not a Kiritarun. It is one field, two halls and a town, and the work
begins and ends at a turf-roofed inn in a hamlet on Imbar. Its stakes are not the world: they are whether a few thousand
dead people go back to killing each other every day, and whether one father is told about his son. That is the right
scale.

## Verdict

Build it, with these revisions:

1. Ned is Jory's son.
2. The law can be learned by fault.
3. Cuddy's strike decides whether a Testing can be fought.
4. Edith faces Torold.
5. Halt's life is shown, not counted.
6. Comedy is structural.
7. Cut Gorm's mercenaries and the *Breathers' War*.
