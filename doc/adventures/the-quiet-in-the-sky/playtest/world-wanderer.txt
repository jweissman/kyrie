You go out of the march-stone of Larum and the Pass into the dark, by what light there is. Somewhere in the wood someone is cutting timber; you hear the axe a long time and never see who. At the head of a long bare valley, a hamlet of turf-roofed houses, and above it a high pass full of a white cloud that does not move. It is very quiet. The road comes up beside a beck going white over stones and stops being a road at the first wall, as if it had set the hamlet down there and gone back. Above the roofs two shoulders of black rock stand up into the cloud, and between them a snowfield goes up into it and does not come out. Sheep. A chapel tower. Smoke going straight up.

It is a long time since you slept. You are weary, and will fight the worse for it until you sleep. Larum’s one street, under the Pass: an inn with a hand cupped to an ear on its sign, a bakehouse breathing warmth out of its door, a squat chapel, and at the top a house with a rope and a long pole hung by the door, which is the guide’s. The houses are stone, turf-roofed, with their backs to the weather. Above everything, the Pass goes up into its cloud. From an upper window of the Hark comes a slow steady clanging, two pans struck together and struck together again. Bet Garside, who has not slept properly in forty nights, is trying to. The folk of Larum, shepherds and their wives, standing in the street, are here.

A bare clean room with a table laid for two, and two cups on it, and only one of them used. Ropes coiled on pegs, ice-poles in a rack, a row of sheepskin coats hanging like a row of tired men. Jory Whitlock, the guide, who takes the living up through the White, is here.

Jory takes a pole from the rack and a coil of rope, and you go up the street and up the path between the black shoulders, and into the white. He walks a yard ahead with the rope between you, and does not talk, and every so often he stops and puts his hand flat on a cairn with a ram’s skull on top, the way a man touches a doorpost. ‘Keep the last at your back,’ he says, once, ‘and walk till the next comes.’ Then the white thins, and goes blue, and you are standing on a height in a light like the inside of a knife, and the cold has teeth. Jory looks out over it for a moment. Then he turns round without a word and goes back down into the white, and is gone. From the booth by the curtain a thin old man in a faded tabard comes out with a ledger under his arm and a pen behind his ear. ‘Name and hour of death,’ he says briskly, and then sees your breath go up white in the cold, and stops. ‘Ah,’ he says. ‘Breathing.’ He looks at the ledger as if it had let him down. ‘There is no column for breathing. The pickers from Larum come up as far as the booth, and the guide, but they never stay.’ He looks down at the Field, and back at you. ‘If you’re going down there, go carefully. They’ve not seen a breather close in a long time, and they’re frightened enough as it is.’ Aud gains 100 experience: found the cairns through the White. Aud gains 100 experience: stood on the Field alive. You come out of the white onto a height over a white plain five miles across, strewn as far as you can see with nine hundred years of broken arms, between two halls on two dark heights; and the cold goes into your teeth. The sky is the colour of a knife-blade, with a low white sun in it that does not seem to move. Below you the plain is strewn with things: spears standing up, shields, the ribs of a cart, a siege-tower with snow on its top. In the middle of it smoke goes up from a huddle of coloured tents. Behind you, the snow comes down out of a clear sky in a curtain, and does not come any further.

Rain comes over in grey curtains, one after another, and between them the country shines. White, and in the white, if you look for it, a darker shape a dozen yards on: a cairn, rimed thick, with a ram’s skull on top. And beyond it, when you get there, the next.

Above the last wall of the last field the path goes up between two shoulders of black rock, steep, through snow that is trodden in a line by sledge-runners. A little way above you it goes into the cloud, and the cloud is not grey, as cloud is, but white, bright, as if lit from inside. It does not move. A post stands at the edge of it with a bell on top and a board: LIVING, GO NO FURTHER WITHOUT THE GUIDE.

Larum’s one street, under the Pass: an inn with a hand cupped to an ear on its sign, a bakehouse breathing warmth out of its door, a squat chapel, and at the top a house with a rope and a long pole hung by the door, which is the guide’s. The folk of Larum, shepherds and their wives, standing in the street, are here. Everyone in Larum, sooner or later, stops in the street and looks up at the Pass and listens. There is nothing to hear. People stand like that for a while with their heads on one side, and then go on.

A low smoky taproom with a peat fire, and over the fire, newest and biggest of a hundred penny ballads pinned to the beams, a woodcut of a man with a sword in each fist and a town burning behind him: THE BALLAD OF HALVARD ELD, CHAMPION OF EVENHALL. The others curl along the beams, armoured men with their names in big letters underneath: BRAND OF THE RED HAND. MAB TRELOAR AND THE SIX FROM EVEN. A shelf of old iron by the door, ticketed for sale. Two pans hang by the stair with their bottoms dented. Here are Bet Garside, who keeps the Hark; and Master Ambler, a collector of heaven-iron, from the lowlands.

First light in Larum. Everyone in the street is standing with their heads on one side, looking up at the Pass.

Down off the Pass, out of the standing cloud, faint as bells three valleys off, it comes: iron on iron, thousands of it. Larum stops in the street. A woman puts her hand to her mouth. Then somebody laughs, and somebody else, and the shepherd with the walnut face says, ‘There. There, now,’ as if to a ewe; and by noon nobody is listening to it, and that night Bet Garside sleeps until ten. Somewhere over the Pass, on the fourth morning, the men of Even went down to the ford and fought each other, and at the Hour every one of them got up, and so nothing had come back; and the next morning the horns sounded on both heights. Whatever the quiet was, it is over. In Larum the shepherds do not look up any more. You stood once at the head of the Pass, in a light like the inside of a knife, and looked down at a huddle of coloured tents in the middle of a white plain, and did not go down to see what they were.

Larum’s one street, under the Pass: an inn with a hand cupped to an ear on its sign, a bakehouse breathing warmth out of its door, a squat chapel, and at the top a house with a rope and a long pole hung by the door, which is the guide’s. The folk of Larum, shepherds and their wives, standing in the street, are here. Down off the Pass, faint as bells three valleys off, comes the clangour: iron on iron, thousands of it, like a smithy the size of the sky. Nobody in the street looks up. A woman beating a rug on a wall is beating it in time.

You set out from Larum in the full of the afternoon, the path pale with dust. Above the last wall of the last field the path goes up between two shoulders of black rock, steep, through snow that is trodden in a line by sledge-runners.

White, and in the white, if you look for it, a darker shape a dozen yards on: a cairn, rimed thick, with a ram’s skull on top. Somewhere above you in the white, iron on iron, a long way off and all around.

First light on the Field. Everyone on it, the living and the dead, is listening for the same thing. Cleeve comes out of his booth with the ledger, and opens it, and dips his pen. ‘Name and hour of—‘ He sees your breath. ‘Oh, it’s you,’ he says. He shuts the ledger. Word goes over the Field from mouth to mouth, faster than anyone could walk it: at the Hour, at the ring, Torold Hame named a Testing. On the fourth morning Evenhall goes down to the ford and fights itself, and if every man of them gets up at the Hour, death has not come back to Ysgard, and the Daily begins again. At the third horn they come down off both heights, Morrow from the east and Even from the west, as they have every morning for nine hundred years, and meet on the ford, and the noise goes up off the Field like a flock of birds. It goes over Halt. Halt does not slow it. By noon the shield-walls are down and the banner lanes are trodden into the snow, and the Oven’s mantlet is on its side, and the Daily goes on over the place where the town was as if it had never been there. The Coming-in again. From the Field below comes the roar of the Daily, and in the middle of it, where Halt was, nothing. The Coming-in: the height over the Field, the curtain of snow behind you coming down out of a clear sky and stopping, and Cleeve’s booth of planks and banners with its brazier smoking. Far off below, Halt’s smoke. Up from the Field comes the noise of the Daily: thousands of men at it, iron on iron, a roar like surf with screaming in it.

Your breath goes up white in front of you and hangs. Nobody else’s does. A man going past with a spear on his shoulder stops dead and stares at the little cloud as if you had taken out a knife. The southern Field, and the Daily far off to the north of it, by the ford: a great dark smudge of people on the white, moving, and a noise like surf.

Trophy Row is churned snow, and shields lying flat, and banners trodden into the ice. The Oven’s mantlet is on its side with the oven cold in it. The Truce has been dragged off somewhere for timber. Nobody is here. The Daily goes on all round: iron on iron, and shouting, and the long noise of a great many people doing the only thing they know.
