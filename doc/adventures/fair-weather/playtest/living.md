## dog

**To Fore Street**

Skeel is built of ships. The house-beams are ships’ beams, tarred black, with the treenail holes still in them; the doors are hatch-covers; a garden fence is a row of oars stuck in the ground. And on every gable, leaning out over the street, there is a figurehead: a woman with a lyre, a lion, a bishop, a mermaid with her nose gone, a man in a cocked hat, a horse, a child holding a dove. The town calls them the Wooden Saints and knows the name of every one. Fore Street runs along the top of the town, with the church at one end and an inn at the other, and a steep stepped lane, the Drang, falls away between the houses toward the harbour. Over the inn door is the biggest of the saints: an admiral in a gilt coat with a telescope under his arm and most of his nose shot away. Under him, the sign: THE ADMIRAL. Carts come up the Drang loaded with casks and wet bales, and men walk beside them with their hands on the load, the way you walk beside a coffin.

**To the quay**

A curved stone quay with a harbour inside it no bigger than a field. Herring boats lie in it with their sails furled, and the ferry lugger, and a long, narrow, very old rowing-gig pulled up on the slip with a girl working on her. On the quay: barrows, nets spread to dry, the Wreck-House with its iron-bound doors, and the fish cellars with a loft over them. On the bow of the blue gig, newly cut and gilded, a name: JACOB. Jinny Rowse’s Patience comes and goes round high water, while there is water at the quay, for whoever has paid her for the day. On the harbour wall a black tide-board, chalked fresh this morning: LOW WATER 11 AND 11. SANDS FROM 9. Under the chalk, painted and peeling: KEEP THE WITHIES. THE STRAND GIVES FOUR HOURS. THE SEA TAKES THE FIFTH. Here are Jinny Rowse, who sails the Patience; and Kerensa Brask, the Reeve’s daughter, with tar to the elbows.

**To the Slip**

A cobbled slipway goes down off the end of the harbour wall onto the Strand. At its foot a post is driven into the sand with a bell on it, and beside the post the first of the withies: a laurel branch stuck upright, then another, and another, away across the sand. The Strand is under the sea. The withies stick up out of the water in a line, a hand’s breadth showing, and the tide-board says when they will stand on sand again.

**Pay Cuddy Pascoe a coin to walk you over the sands**

Cuddy takes the coin and rings his bell at the sand, as if to warn it. ‘Right side going out,’ he says. ‘Left side coming in. Walk where I walk. If I stop, stop. If I run, run.’

**To the open Strand**

You leave Skeel in the full of the morning, with the sun climbing and the path dry underfoot. Ribbed sand to every side, hard as a road where the withies are and softer off them, shining wet. Each ripple holds a thread of water. A line of laurel branches stuck upright leads away across it, one every twenty paces, toward the Holm, toward Scarth Bank, toward the Gut, toward the Craigs. A big black curly dog is trotting along the wrack line a hundred yards off, nose down, ribs showing. When you stop, he stops, and watches you, one forefoot lifted. He has a rope collar. When you take a step toward him he takes two away.

**Into the Admiral**

A low room built into the hold of a ship, they say, and it feels it: the beams curve, the floor tilts a little toward the fire, and the lamps swing when the door opens. Fishermen with nothing to fish sit at the long table over small beer. On the wall, a painted board with the names of ships that have come to Skeel, and the years. The last name, freshly painted: CONSTANCY. Up in the rafters, in among the hams, a small yellow bird hops about and sings, as if it had always lived here. Mother Jago says it came in at the door the morning after the wreck and she has not the heart to put it out. Mother Jago, who keeps the Admiral, is here.

**Into the fish cellars**

Long low vaults with the herring-presses in them, great beams with stones hung on the ends, standing idle. The salt is stacked waiting for fish that did not come. It is very cold.

**Into the net-loft over the cellars**

A long loft under the roof, where the nets are mended in winter. It has been swept, and benches set out in rows, and at the far end, a table with a cloth on it and one chair behind it: for the Wreck Court. Here are Mother Jago, who keeps the Admiral; Parson Hosken, the priest of Lir Who Gives; Big Jack Cawse, the Reeve’s man; Hender Brask, the Wreck-Reeve of the Gannet Reach; Loveday Kitto, who keeps the Holm Lantern; Kerensa Brask, the Reeve’s daughter, with tar to the elbows; and Constance Ferrar, who keeps the books of Ferrar and Daughters, shipowners.

**Wait › A while**

The net-loft is packed to the walls: Skeel on the benches in its Sunday black, men standing at the back with their hats in their hands, children on the beams. The air smells of tar and wet wool. At the far end Hender Brask sits at the table with the book open in front of him and the charter board, brought from the church, propped against the wall behind him, shining. Loveday Kitto sits alone on a stool in front of the table, in her good shawl, with her hands in her lap. Constance Ferrar stands at the side, by the window, with her ledger, very upright. Nobody stands near her. Time passes. Hender Brask stands. ‘The Wreck Court of the Gannet Reach,’ he says, ‘under the Admiral’s charter. The ship Constancy, Ferrar’s, struck on Scarth Bank on the night of Saturday last, in fair weather. If any man here knows of a living thing that came ashore of her, neither man nor dog nor cat, let him say so now.’ He waits, with the pen in his hand, and the loft is so quiet you can hear the tide coming in under the floor.

**Bring Admiral forward to the Reeve's table**

You walk up the loft between the benches with the black dog at your heel, and the whole of Skeel watches him go by, and he goes up to the Reeve’s table and puts his front paws on it, on the open book, and looks into Hender Brask’s face, and wags. Somewhere at the back a man begins to laugh, the helpless kind, and then a woman, and then the children on the beams, and Mother Jago puts her cloth over her face. ‘His name’s Admiral,’ you say, and that does it: the loft roars. Hender Brask does not laugh. He looks at the dog. Then he dips his pen and writes, in the voice he uses for numbers: ‘A dog came ashore of her, living. The Constancy is no wreck.’ The laughter stops as if it had been cut. ‘The second matter,’ says the Reeve, without looking up. ‘The Holm Lantern was dark on the night of the wreck. Its keeper, Loveday Kitto, asks no excuse.’ Loveday sits very straight on her stool. ‘I shall ask the Court to relieve her of the keeping, with thanks for forty-one years, and give the Lantern to John Cawse.’ Big Jack Cawse goes red to the ears. Tamar gains 300 experience: showed the Court a living thing.
