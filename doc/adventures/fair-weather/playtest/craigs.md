## road

**To the cliff road round the Reach**

You leave the coast road above Skeel with the sun low behind you and the dew still on the grass. The cliff road goes round the head of the Reach through gorse and thrift, with the sea below on your right hand, for miles. Ahead the land rises into a long ridge of cliffs, the Craigs, and the noise of them comes to you before anything else: tens of thousands of birds, all talking at once.

**To the climbers' camp on the Craigs**

A camp on the turf at the top of the Craigs: three tents of old sailcloth, a fire-pit, a cart with the shafts up, and a row of iron stakes driven into the cliff edge with ropes going over and down. The noise of the gannets comes up from below like weather. Baskets of eggs, greenish-white and as big as a fist, stand in rows in the grass, and there is a smell of guano and woodsmoke and the sea. The way out to the Pulpit along the cliff edge is a sheep-track a foot wide over two hundred feet of air, and the wind comes up the face in gusts that lift your coat. Along the edge to the west, where the cliff steps out highest over the sea, is a notch in the rock like a window: the Pulpit. You can see the ledges below it, and nobody on them. Here are Ebrel Penhallow, who leads the egg-climbers; and Piran Penhallow, an egg-climber’s son, fifteen.

**Talk to Ebrel Penhallow**

A broad, weathered woman with a rope coiled over her shoulder and her sleeves rolled up to show forearms like a man’s thighs, eating an egg raw out of the shell. ‘You came round by the road or over the sands?’ she says. ‘Either way you’re a long way from anywhere. Sit. Have an egg. Mind the birds, they’ve no manners.’

**Ask Ebrel Penhallow about the egg-climbing**

‘Gannets lay one egg.’ She holds one up. ‘You take the first, and she lays another, and you leave her that. Twenty days in the spring, three hundred eggs a day, pickled, salted, smoked, sold up and down the coast. The Reeve gives the licence, and takes a penny in the shilling.’ She cracks the egg on her knee. ‘My father’s father climbed these, and his. My man climbed them.’ A pause. ‘He fell off the Pulpit eight years ago, reaching for a nest he didn’t need. So now I climb them.’

**Talk to Piran Penhallow**

A thin boy with his hair in his eyes, sitting at the edge of the cliff with his legs over it, looking west along the face toward the Pulpit. He looks round at you, and back.

**Ask Piran what he saw on the night of the wreck**

He does not look round. ‘A light. In the Pulpit. I was out on the edge here, I come out here at night, Mam doesn’t know. And there was a light in the Pulpit where there’s never anything, a lamp, hung up, and a man beside it, all dark. I thought it was my da.’ He swallows. ‘And then the ship hit, out on the sand, I heard it, like a door slammed a mile off. And the man put his hand over the lamp. And it was dark.’ Now he looks at you. ‘Ghosts don’t put their hands over lamps. Do they.’

**Ask Piran what he means to do**

‘Go up.’ He says it quite calmly. ‘Tomorrow night, when they’re all at the Supper and drunk. Up the fixed rope to the ledges, and up the face. I’ve done the face in my head every night since I was seven. I’ll see what’s up there.’ He looks at you. ‘If you tell Mam, I’ll go tonight instead.’

**Ask Piran to climb the Pulpit with you, now**

His face goes very bright and then very serious. ‘Now?’ He gets up. ‘Mam’s at the far stakes. Come on. I’ll show you where the rope is good.’ Piran is on his feet before you have finished asking.

Piran comes up into the Pulpit beside you on his hands and knees, and stands, holding the rock, and looks. He looks at the sea, and at the Holm, and at Skeel. Then he looks at the back of the niche, and you watch him find the letters there, and the ring, and the new hook. He puts his hand over the hook and holds it, as if it were warm. ‘Somebody,’ he says, and stops. ‘It wasn’t him. It was somebody.’ He is quiet. **Examine — Look closely**

You look closely at the iron in the Pulpit wall. The new hook has been driven into the crack this month: the rock-dust is still pale in the crack round it. On the rock under it, a fan of tallow drips, white, from a lantern hung there for some hours. On the hook itself, caught in a burr of the iron, a scrap of scorched horn: a pane from a horn lantern, the kind every boat in Skeel carries. Whoever hung it knew to hang it beside J.B.’s ring, where the old pilots’ lamp hung.

## supper

**Sit down at the Gannet Supper**
