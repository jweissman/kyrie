## day1

**To Fore Street**

Skeel is built of ships. The house-beams are ships’ beams, tarred black, with the treenail holes still in them; the doors are hatch-covers; a garden fence is a row of oars stuck in the ground. And on every gable, leaning out over the street, there is a figurehead: a woman with a lyre, a lion, a bishop, a mermaid with her nose gone, a man in a cocked hat, a horse, a child holding a dove. The town calls them the Wooden Saints and knows the name of every one. Fore Street runs along the top of the town, with the church at one end and an inn at the other, and a steep stepped lane, the Drang, falls away between the houses toward the harbour. Over the inn door is the biggest of the saints: an admiral in a gilt coat with a telescope under his arm and most of his nose shot away. Under him, the sign: THE ADMIRAL. Carts come up the Drang loaded with casks and wet bales, and men walk beside them with their hands on the load, the way you walk beside a coffin.

**To Tonkin's ropewalk**

A long straight alley of beaten earth behind the houses, two hundred yards of it, roofed with old sails on posts. At one end a great wheel with a handle; down its length, strands of hemp stretched between hooks, being twisted into rope as a man walks backward away from the wheel feeding fibre from a bundle at his waist. It smells of tar and hay. Bartle Tonkin, a rope-maker of Skeel, is here.

**Turn Bartle Tonkin's rope-wheel for the afternoon, for two coin**

You turn the wheel for three hours while Bartle walks backward away from you with the hemp, and back, and away, and the strands twist and tighten and become rope. He does not speak once. At the end he counts two coins into your hand and says, ‘You’ll do,’ which from Bartle Tonkin is a speech.

**To the quay**

A curved stone quay with a harbour inside it no bigger than a field. Herring boats lie in it with their sails furled, and the ferry lugger, and a long, narrow, very old rowing-gig pulled up on the slip with a girl working on her. On the quay: barrows, nets spread to dry, the Wreck-House with its iron-bound doors, and the fish cellars with a loft over them. On the bow of the blue gig, newly cut and gilded, a name: JACOB. Jinny Rowse’s Patience comes and goes round high water, while there is water at the quay, for whoever has paid her for the day. On the harbour wall a black tide-board, chalked fresh this morning: LOW WATER 11 AND 11. SANDS FROM 9. Under the chalk, painted and peeling: KEEP THE WITHIES. THE STRAND GIVES FOUR HOURS. THE SEA TAKES THE FIFTH. Here are Jinny Rowse, who sails the Patience; and Kerensa Brask, the Reeve’s daughter, with tar to the elbows.

**Talk to Kerensa Brask**

A tall girl in an old guernsey with her hair tied up in a rag is caulking the seams of a long narrow rowing-gig on the slip, with a mallet and a bucket of pitch. The gig is painted blue, quite new, over very old wood. Without looking up: ‘If you’re looking for my father he’s in the Wreck-House counting. If you’re looking for the wreck it’s that way. If you’re looking for me, nobody is.’

**Ask Kerensa Brask about the gig**

She stops caulking. ‘My grandfather’s. Jacob Brask’s. She lay on the shingle on Inish for thirty-eight years with the grass growing through her, and two summers ago I rowed over in Jinny’s punt and dug her out and towed her home.’ She slaps the gunwale. ‘New garboards, new stem, eleven new planks, and every rib but three. She’s more mine than his now. Don’t tell my father; he thinks I bought her off a man in Callowport.’ She goes back to the seam. ‘He’s never once come down the quay to look.’

**Ask Kerensa Brask what she means to do**

‘Go.’ She drives a strand of oakum into a seam. ‘There’s a packet coming tomorrow from the south for the Constancy, the owners’ packet. It goes back on the fourth tide. I’m going on it if I have to stow away in the bread.’ Mallet. ‘There’s nothing here. There’s wreck and there’s waiting for wreck. There’s my father in the Wreck-House counting the dead’s grain, and the whole town hoping the sea’ll be kind to it and cruel to somebody else.’ She looks out at the Reach. ‘I want to go where there’s ships coming in, not ships coming apart.’

**Ask Kerensa Brask what she has been sounding the bar for**

She goes very still with the mallet in the air. Then she laughs, angrily. ‘Bartle. Bartle Tonkin and his one lead-line a year.’ She puts the mallet down and wipes her hands and takes out of her guernsey a little notebook bound in sailcloth, and holds it, not giving it. ‘Two summers. Every state of the tide, every cast, the whole bar from the Craigs to Inish. I don’t know why. Because he did it. Because it’s there to be known and nobody knows it any more.’ She turns the notebook over. ‘Because if anybody ever wanted pilots on the Reach again, somebody would have to know the bar.’ She almost holds it out. ‘It’s no use to anybody. There’s no station.’

**Ask Kerensa Brask about the night of the wreck**

She does not answer for a long time. She is looking at the gig’s name. ‘He came in at three,’ she says at last. ‘I wasn’t asleep. I heard the door. He was wet to here.’ She touches her thigh. ‘There was red clay on his boots. There’s no red clay in Skeel. There’s only red clay on the Craigs path.’ She swallows. ‘He lit the copper in the wash-house at four in the morning, and burned his good black coat in it, and stood watching it burn. In the morning he had his other coat on and he went out with the carts and he’s not slept since. Not one night. I hear him walking.’ Now she looks at you. ‘I’m going on the packet. I’m telling you because I’m going. I’ll not stand in the loft and say it to his face. I’ll not. Don’t ask me.’
