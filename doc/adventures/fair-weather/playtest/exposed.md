## skerry

**To Fore Street**

Skeel is built of ships. The house-beams are ships’ beams, tarred black, with the treenail holes still in them; the doors are hatch-covers; a garden fence is a row of oars stuck in the ground. And on every gable, leaning out over the street, there is a figurehead: a woman with a lyre, a lion, a bishop, a mermaid with her nose gone, a man in a cocked hat, a horse, a child holding a dove. The town calls them the Wooden Saints and knows the name of every one. Fore Street runs along the top of the town, with the church at one end and an inn at the other, and a steep stepped lane, the Drang, falls away between the houses toward the harbour. Over the inn door is the biggest of the saints: an admiral in a gilt coat with a telescope under his arm and most of his nose shot away. Under him, the sign: THE ADMIRAL. Carts come up the Drang loaded with casks and wet bales, and men walk beside them with their hands on the load, the way you walk beside a coffin.

**To the quay**

A curved stone quay with a harbour inside it no bigger than a field. Herring boats lie in it with their sails furled, and the ferry lugger, and a long, narrow, very old rowing-gig pulled up on the slip with a girl working on her. On the quay: barrows, nets spread to dry, the Wreck-House with its iron-bound doors, and the fish cellars with a loft over them. On the bow of the blue gig, newly cut and gilded, a name: JACOB. Jinny Rowse’s Patience comes and goes round high water, while there is water at the quay, for whoever has paid her for the day. On the harbour wall a black tide-board, chalked fresh this morning: LOW WATER 11 AND 11. SANDS FROM 9. Under the chalk, painted and peeling: KEEP THE WITHIES. THE STRAND GIVES FOUR HOURS. THE SEA TAKES THE FIFTH. Here are Jinny Rowse, who sails the Patience; and Kerensa Brask, the Reeve’s daughter, with tar to the elbows.

**Wait › A while**

The harbour has emptied. The boats lie on the mud, each on its side or propped on its legs, and you can walk round them. It smells of weed and fish and the inside of things. Time passes.

**Wait › A while**

The harbour is full and the boats ride in it, knocking gently against the wall. Time passes.

**Pay Jinny Rowse three coin for the day's sailing**

Jinny Rowse puts the coins in her boot. ‘When there’s water,’ she says, and looks at the harbour, and at the tide-board.

**To the Skerry landing**

You set out from Skeel in the full of the afternoon, the road pale with dust. A shelf of black rock with weed on it, slippery, and a ring in the rock to tie up to. Behind it the Skerry humps up, all rock and sea-pinks, with a turf bothy crouched in the lee of it, smoke going sideways from a hole in its roof. Seal-skins are pegged out to dry on the rock, and a coracle lies upside down like a black beetle. Ewan Marrock’s coracle is a black hide bowl on the Skerry rocks, a mile off. It does not come for anyone he has not asked. Ewan Marrock, the sealer of Seal Skerry, is here.

**Talk to Ewan Marrock**

A big man with a black beard going grey and a sealing-club through his belt is waiting on the rock with his arms folded before your boat has touched. ‘There’s nothing here for Skeel,’ he says. ‘No wreck. No dog. No anything. Go home.’

**Ask Ewan Marrock what he has against Skeel**

‘Against it?’ He looks across the water at the town on its headland. ‘Nothing. It’s a fine town. It buries what the sea sends it under stones that say A SAILOR, and it sings a hymn asking for more.’ He spits into the sea. ‘And when my wife went, it said she was a seal and had gone back to her own, and laughed at me in the Admiral for seven years. I’ve nothing against Skeel. I just don’t go there.’

**Ask Ewan Marrock about the seals**

His arms come unfolded. ‘Greys. They come in at the back end to pup. Fifty on the slab this year, more than last.’ He looks at them as you might look at a field of your own. ‘I take some. Oil and skins. Not the pups, not the cows. Old bulls, past it.’ A pause. ‘They sing, at night. Did they tell you that in Skeel? Skeel says it’s the seal-women singing for their men.’ He looks at the haul-out. ‘I don’t know what it is. I sit out and listen to it.’ Ewan Marrock jerks his head toward the bothy. ‘There’s a boy in there,’ he says. ‘Off your wreck. I found him on a hatch in the weed at the haul-out the morning after, more dead than not. I’ve kept him.’ He looks at you hard. ‘If you take him to Skeel, you take him over my body. That’s all.’

**Into Ewan Marrock's bothy**

Tamar gains 250 experience: found the boy on the Skerry. Inside the bothy: smoke, a peat fire, a box bed, a barrel of seal oil, skins, a lamp, and a great deal of order. On a shelf by itself, a woman’s comb, and a shell, and a blue ribbon, laid out the way things are laid out in a church. Nye Gallow, the Constancy’s boy, fourteen, with a bandaged head, is here.

**Talk to Nye Gallow**

A boy is sitting up in the box bed in the corner with a blanket round him and a bandage round his head, very white, holding Ewan’s sealing-knife in both hands pointed at you. ‘I’m not going,’ he says. ‘I’m not going to Skeel. They’ll do me like they want to do the dog.’

**Ask Nye Gallow what happened to the Constancy**

He tells it with the knife still in his hands, not fast. ‘Master Dunn had the Holm light on the chart. And there it was, high up, south, and he said good, that’s her, and brought her round for it.’ He swallows. ‘Mister Ferrar said it was too high. He said it twice. And then she hit, and the whole ship stood up on end, and I went over.’ His voice goes very flat. ‘And the light went out. Like somebody put their hand over it.’ He looks at the fire. ‘After, it was low water, and I was on a hatch, on the sand, in the dark, and all the others were — and a man came walking. With a lantern. A horn one. Tall, black coat. Marks under his eyes. He held it up and he saw me. He looked right at me.’ The boy’s hands are white on the knife. ‘And he turned round and walked away.’

**Ask Nye Gallow what he wants**

‘Home.’ At once. ‘My mam’s in Callowport. And Admiral. He’s mine; Mister Ferrar gave him me when he was a pup, for the ship.’ He looks at the door. ‘And I want — ‘ He stops. ‘I want to see that man’s face again. I want to look at him. That’s all. I want to see if he looks back.’

**Ask Nye Gallow to come with you**

He looks at Ewan, and Ewan shrugs. The boy gets out of the box bed, slowly, with the blanket round him, and puts the knife in his belt. ‘All right,’ he says. ‘But I keep the knife.’ Nye Gallow comes, with the blanket round him and the knife in his belt, keeping close.

**Out to the Skerry landing**

A shelf of black rock with weed on it, slippery, and a ring in the rock to tie up to. Ewan Marrock, the sealer of Seal Skerry, is here.

You set out from the Skerry landing. A curved stone quay with a harbour inside it no bigger than a field. Here are Jinny Rowse, who sails the Patience; and Kerensa Brask, the Reeve’s daughter, with tar to the elbows.

**Into the Admiral**

A low room built into the hold of a ship, they say, and it feels it: the beams curve, the floor tilts a little toward the fire, and the lamps swing when the door opens. Fishermen with nothing to fish sit at the long table over small beer. On the wall, a painted board with the names of ships that have come to Skeel, and the years. The last name, freshly painted: CONSTANCY. Up in the rafters, in among the hams, a small yellow bird hops about and sings, as if it had always lived here. Mother Jago says it came in at the door the morning after the wreck and she has not the heart to put it out. Here are Mother Jago, who keeps the Admiral; and Cuddy Pascoe, the Sands-Guide.

**To the quay**

**Into the fish cellars**

Long low vaults with the herring-presses in them, great beams with stones hung on the ends, standing idle. The salt is stacked waiting for fish that did not come. It is very cold.

**Into the net-loft over the cellars**

A long loft under the roof, where the nets are mended in winter. It has been swept, and benches set out in rows, and at the far end, a table with a cloth on it and one chair behind it: for the Wreck Court. Here are Mother Jago, who keeps the Admiral; Parson Hosken, the priest of Lir Who Gives; Big Jack Cawse, the Reeve’s man; Hender Brask, the Wreck-Reeve of the Gannet Reach; Loveday Kitto, who keeps the Holm Lantern; Kerensa Brask, the Reeve’s daughter, with tar to the elbows; and Constance Ferrar, who keeps the books of Ferrar and Daughters, shipowners.

**Wait › A while**

The net-loft is packed to the walls: Skeel on the benches in its Sunday black, men standing at the back with their hats in their hands, children on the beams. The air smells of tar and wet wool. At the far end Hender Brask sits at the table with the book open in front of him and the charter board, brought from the church, propped against the wall behind him, shining. Loveday Kitto sits alone on a stool in front of the table, in her good shawl, with her hands in her lap. Constance Ferrar stands at the side, by the window, with her ledger, very upright. Nobody stands near her. Time passes. Hender Brask stands. ‘The Wreck Court of the Gannet Reach,’ he says, ‘under the Admiral’s charter. The ship Constancy, Ferrar’s, struck on Scarth Bank on the night of Saturday last, in fair weather. If any man here knows of a living thing that came ashore of her, neither man nor dog nor cat, let him say so now.’ He waits, with the pen in his hand, and the loft is so quiet you can hear the tide coming in under the floor.

**Bring Nye Gallow forward to the Reeve's table**

The boy walks up the loft to the table on his own, with his bandage and his blanket and Ewan Marrock’s knife in his belt, and the whole of Skeel draws its breath in at once. He stands in front of the Reeve. ‘Nye Gallow,’ he says. ‘Ship’s boy, Constancy. I came ashore.’ And then he does the thing he said he wanted to do: he looks at Hender Brask’s face. Hender Brask looks back at him. Nobody in the loft can see what passes between them; you can, a little. The Reeve dips his pen. ‘A boy came ashore of her, living,’ he says, ‘and the Constancy is no wreck,’ and his hand shakes so that the ink runs. ‘The second matter,’ says the Reeve, without looking up. ‘The Holm Lantern was dark on the night of the wreck. Its keeper, Loveday Kitto, asks no excuse.’ Loveday sits very straight on her stool. ‘I shall ask the Court to relieve her of the keeping, with thanks for forty-one years, and give the Lantern to John Cawse.’ Big Jack Cawse goes red to the ears. Tamar gains 300 experience: showed the Court a living thing.

**Ask Nye Gallow whether the man with the lantern is in this room**

The boy does not answer you. He has not stopped looking at the Reeve since he came up the stair. He lifts his arm and points, across the table, and his voice when it comes is high and cracked and carries to the back wall. ‘Him. He held his lamp up and he saw me on the hatch and he went away.’ For a moment nothing at all happens. Then Loveday Kitto, on her stool, turns her head and looks at Hender Brask, and he looks at her, and you see his ears go red, slowly, from the lobes up, like a boy’s; and that is answer enough for everyone in Skeel who ever knew him as a boy, which is everyone. ‘The last matter,’ says the Reeve, and his voice is not quite steady, ‘is the winter.’ He looks at the book. Tamar gains 200 experience: kept Loveday Kitto her Lantern.

**Wait › A while**

Time passes. There is nothing to say about the winter, and nobody says it. Constance Ferrar, by the window, opens her ledger and closes it again. On the benches people are looking at their hands.

**Wait › A while**

Time passes. The Reeve closes the book. The loft empties down the stair in silence, onto a quay where the tide is in and the boats are knocking gently against the wall. Nye Gallow goes his own way.

**Wait › An hour** *(chapter: The Third Ebb)*

On the third ebb, in the dark, the Reach gives back what it took.

**Wait › A while**

Time passes. They do not hang him in Skeel. They do not know how. Jack Cawse and two of his brothers walk Hender Brask down the Drang between them to the quay, gently, as if he were drunk, and lock him in the fish cellars among the idle presses until the packet sails, and Constance Ferrar writes out the information against him in her ledger on her knee by the light of the cellar lamp, and has him sign it, and he does. The grain goes down to the quay for Ferrar’s. In the night somebody chalks on the charter board by the church door, under NEITHER MAN NOR DOG NOR CAT, the words NOR BOY, and in the morning Parson Hosken, after thinking about it for a long time, leaves it there.
