## morning

**To Fore Street**

Skeel is built of ships. The house-beams are ships’ beams, tarred black, with the treenail holes still in them; the doors are hatch-covers; a garden fence is a row of oars stuck in the ground. And on every gable, leaning out over the street, there is a figurehead: a woman with a lyre, a lion, a bishop, a mermaid with her nose gone, a man in a cocked hat, a horse, a child holding a dove. The town calls them the Wooden Saints and knows the name of every one. Fore Street runs along the top of the town, with the church at one end and an inn at the other, and a steep stepped lane, the Drang, falls away between the houses toward the harbour. Over the inn door is the biggest of the saints: an admiral in a gilt coat with a telescope under his arm and most of his nose shot away. Under him, the sign: THE ADMIRAL. Carts come up the Drang loaded with casks and wet bales, and men walk beside them with their hands on the load, the way you walk beside a coffin.

**To the quay**

A curved stone quay with a harbour inside it no bigger than a field. Herring boats lie in it with their sails furled, and the ferry lugger, and a long, narrow, very old rowing-gig pulled up on the slip with a girl working on her. On the quay: barrows, nets spread to dry, the Wreck-House with its iron-bound doors, and the fish cellars with a loft over them. On the bow of the blue gig, newly cut and gilded, a name: JACOB. Jinny Rowse’s Patience comes and goes round high water, while there is water at the quay, for whoever has paid her for the day. On the harbour wall a black tide-board, chalked fresh this morning: LOW WATER 11 AND 11. SANDS FROM 9. Under the chalk, painted and peeling: KEEP THE WITHIES. THE STRAND GIVES FOUR HOURS. THE SEA TAKES THE FIFTH. Here are Jinny Rowse, who sails the Patience; and Kerensa Brask, the Reeve’s daughter, with tar to the elbows.

**Talk to Jinny Rowse**

A small woman in a man’s sea-boots, sitting on a bollard eating an apple with a knife. ‘If you want the Patience,’ she says, ‘it’s three coin for the day, anywhere she’ll go, as many times as you like, while there’s water. Holm, Skerry, Inish. Not the Craigs, there’s no landing.’

**Ask Jinny Rowse about the night of the wreck**

She cuts a slice of apple and eats it off the knife. ‘I was out. Lines, off the Skerry. Nothing biting.’ Another slice. ‘I saw the Constancy’s lights come in from the south, a big ship, going careful. Then there was a light on the Craigs, high up, where there’s never a light. And she turned for it.’ She stops chewing. ‘You know why she’d turn for it? Because a light up there is where the Holm’s light would be if you were further south than you are. A stranger reads it as the Holm. And then she struck, and the light on the Craigs went out, like that.’ She closes her hand. ‘And the Lantern was dark the whole time. I sat in my boat and watched it, and I’ve not told the Reeve, and I’ll tell you why: he never asked.’

**Talk to Kerensa Brask**

A tall girl in an old guernsey with her hair tied up in a rag is caulking the seams of a long narrow rowing-gig on the slip, with a mallet and a bucket of pitch. The gig is painted blue, quite new, over very old wood. Without looking up: ‘If you’re looking for my father he’s in the Wreck-House counting. If you’re looking for the wreck it’s that way. If you’re looking for me, nobody is.’

**Ask Kerensa Brask about the gig**

She stops caulking. ‘My grandfather’s. Jacob Brask’s. She lay on the shingle on Inish for thirty-eight years with the grass growing through her, and two summers ago I rowed over in Jinny’s punt and dug her out and towed her home.’ She slaps the gunwale. ‘New garboards, new stem, eleven new planks, and every rib but three. She’s more mine than his now. Don’t tell my father; he thinks I bought her off a man in Callowport.’ She goes back to the seam. ‘He’s never once come down the quay to look.’

**Ask Kerensa Brask what she means to do**

**Into the Wreck-House**

A stone barn with iron-bound doors, built for salvage. It is full to the rafters: grain in sacks stacked to the beams, casks of wine in rows on their sides, wet bales of cloth, a ship’s bell, coils of cable, a binnacle, chairs, a harmonium, a crate of something stencilled WITH CARE in a southern hand. Everything is chalked with a number. On a table by the door is a great book, and a lamp, and the Reeve. The smell of the grain is sweet and heavy and fills the place. There is enough in here, you think, to feed a town through a winter. That is what it is for. A pen of hurdles lashed together with cord and a padlocked chain. Here are Big Jack Cawse, the Reeve’s man; and Hender Brask, the Wreck-Reeve of the Gannet Reach.

**Talk to Hender Brask**

A tall man of fifty in a good black coat, with the book open in front of him and a pen in his hand. He has a very still, careful face, and has not slept; the skin under his eyes is the colour of a bruise. ‘Hender Brask, the Admiral’s Reeve,’ he says, and writes a number before he looks up. ‘If you have come to buy, the sale is the day after tomorrow. If you have come to sell, I do not buy. If you have come off the coast road to look at the wreck, everyone does, and you are welcome.’

**Ask Hender Brask about the wreck**

‘The Constancy, Ferrar’s of the south, master Abel Dunn, four hundred tons, grain and wine and sundries.’ He says it like an entry. ‘She stood into the Reach in the night on a false reckoning and struck Scarth Bank. The Lantern was not lit.’ His pen does not move. ‘Loveday Kitto is seventy-three. She fell asleep. It is nobody’s fault, and it is the end of her keeping, and I am sorry for it; she was good to me when I was a boy.’

**Ask Hender Brask for the Constancy's mailbag, for the families of the drowned**

He looks at the bag on its hook. ‘The mails are not wreck,’ he says, almost to himself. ‘The charter does not reach to letters. They are nobody’s.’ He gets up and takes it down and holds it a moment. ‘I read them, you know. Everybody’s letters, all my life, for those who can’t. I have not opened this one.’ He holds it out. ‘Take it. Do what is right with them. I find I cannot.’ You take the Constancy’s mailbag.

**Go through the Constancy's mailbag**

## inish

**Pay Jinny Rowse three coin for the day's sailing**

Jinny Rowse puts the coins in her boot. ‘When there’s water,’ she says, and looks at the harbour, and at the tide-board.

**To the Inish strand**

You set out from Skeel. A strand of shingle under a low brown island. Three stone houses stand above it in a row, facing the sea: two have no roofs, and grass growing in their doorways; the third has a roof of tarred canvas and a goat on it. Drawn up on the shingle, rotting gently, are the bones of boats: long, narrow gigs, six of them, their paint gone to a ghost of blue and red and green. Five gigs, and a gap at the end of the row where a sixth lay: the shingle there is pressed into the shape of a long hull, and two drag-marks run down from it to the sea, grassed over, a summer or two old. The Gut is running through the Sill like a river in spate, green and fast and full of sand. It runs slack only in the middle of low water, for an hour or two.

**Into the pilot-house**

One room, crammed: a bed, a stove, nets, a telescope on a tripod at the window, a goat-harness, two hundred books, and on every wall, in chalk and charcoal and paint, the Reach. Channels, banks, depths in fathoms, bearings, marks: drawn and corrected and drawn again over forty years, so that the walls are dark with it, like a cave painted by a man who has never seen anything else. On the table under the window, a book in a canvas cover, burned along one edge, its pages stiff and brown. On the cover, in old gilt: THE PILOTS’ RUTTER OF THE GANNET REACH. Old Malachy Sennen, the last pilot of the Reach, is here.

**Talk to Old Malachy Sennen**

A very old man, very small, very upright, in a blue coat with brass buttons gone green, is looking at you through a telescope from across the room. ‘You came over the Sill,’ he says, ‘or Jinny Rowse brought you. If it was the Sill, you’re a fool, and if it was Jinny, you’re a fool with three coin less. Sit down. Nobody’s come to Inish in a year but the goat.’

**Ask Malachy Sennen about the pilots**

‘Twelve gigs.’ He holds up both hands and two fingers. ‘Twelve crews of six, racing out over the bar to every sail that showed, first aboard got the job. Brought her in up the channel, Pulpit over the Holm, and took her fee, and the Reach lived on it. Eighty years of that.’ He lowers the hands. ‘Then the old Admiral bought the wreck-right from the crown. He never forbade us. He was cleverer than that. He just let the station’s lease run out, and kept the Lantern, and let it be known a ship that came in without a pilot came in at her own risk. And they did. And they struck. And the wreck paid better than the pilotage.’ He laughs, a dry small sound. ‘In one generation Skeel went from bringing ships in to taking them to pieces. Never noticed it happening. Nobody does.’

**Ask Malachy Sennen to teach you to read the tide**

He is delighted. He takes a stick of chalk and goes to the wall. ‘Four hours dry at each low water. Low water an hour later every day, and back round with the springs. The Gut runs through the Sill like a mill-race, except the hour either side of dead low, when it stops to think, and that’s when a man can wade it, if he’s a fool. Your harbour dries when your sands do; so you walk out on the ebb and sail home on the flood, and never the other way about.’ He draws as he talks, fast, a tide like a heartbeat. ‘And the fifth hour, when the sea comes over the Strand, you are somewhere high, or you are dead. That’s all of it. Fifty years to learn it properly. Now go away and do it.’ Tamar gains 100 experience: learned the tide of the Reach.

**Ask Malachy Sennen about Jacob Brask**

He is quiet for a while. ‘My partner. The best of us. Blue gig.’ He points out of the window at the rotting boats, at the gap at the end of the row. ‘She lay there. JACOB, she was called. Somebody took her, two summers back. I never asked who. He could take a ship up the channel on a black night with no Lantern, by the feel of the water and the smell of the Holm sheep. When the station went, he had nothing to do with it all.’ He taps his head. ‘He drank. He burned the Rutter in the stove one night, roaring, and I pulled it out, half of it. Next week he took his gig out over the bar alone and the sea brought it back without him.’ He looks at you. ‘His boy’s the Reeve now. Hender. Brought up by Loveday Kitto on the Holm. Fine boy. Never set foot on Inish since. I don’t blame him.’

**Ask Malachy Sennen about the burned book**

‘The Rutter.’ He lays a hand on it. ‘Every depth, every mark, every bottom, every danger on the Reach, sounded and written down by eighty years of pilots. Half of it’s ash.’ He opens it: the burned edge crumbles. ‘The bar’s gone. The channel by Scarth Bank’s gone. The Sill. The Holm channel. The leading mark itself, the page with the bearing on it, gone.’ He shuts it. ‘I could write it again, if I had the soundings. I’m too old to take them. You’d want a lead-line, and the tide-hours, and the sense not to drown.’ He looks at you sideways. ‘Not that anybody needs a Rutter. There’s no pilots.’

## holm

**To the Holm strand**

You set out from Skeel in the full of the afternoon, the road pale with dust. A hump of turf and grey rock a quarter-mile long, with sheep on it, and the Lantern on its crown: a squat round tower of whitewashed stone, with a glazed lamp-room on top like a lantern on a stick. At the tower’s foot, a low cell of older stone half sunk in the turf, its doorway facing the sea. A well with a stone lid. Washing on a line: one woman’s. On the well-lid, scratched deep and old: HOBB. And under it, newer, in chalk, the islanders’ joke: SALT SINCE THE WRECK YEAR. The Holm’s well went brackish forty years ago, the year the pilots went, and Skeel says the island is ashamed. The sea lies between the Strand and the Holm, a mile of it, and a boat would be the way. Ewan Marrock’s coracle is a black hide bowl on the Skerry rocks, a mile off. It does not come for anyone he has not asked.

**Sound the Holm channel from the Holm strand with the lead-line**

You walk the Holm’s edge and cast into the channel that runs past it toward Skeel: five fathoms, six, steady, a good clean channel with a shell bottom, deep enough for anything. This is the way in. This is what a pilot would bring a ship up.

**Into the Lantern**

The tower’s one room: a bed, a chair, a table with a Bible of Lir and a teapot on it, a fire of driftwood. Everything is very clean and set exactly where it is, the way things are in a house where someone has learned to find them without looking. A stair goes up round the wall.

**Into the lamp-room**

The lamp-room: glass on every side, salt-dimmed, and in the middle on its iron stand the light, a great brass oil-lamp with eight wicks in a ring and a polished reflector behind them like a sunflower. The whole Reach is round you. To the south, across the water, the black line of the Craigs, and the high notch in them they call the Pulpit. Loveday Kitto, who keeps the Holm Lantern, is here.

**Talk to Loveday Kitto**

A small old woman in a grey shawl is polishing the great reflector with a rag, slowly, by touch, her face very close to the brass. She turns her head toward you, not quite at you. ‘If you’re from the Reeve,’ she says, ‘you can tell him I’ve polished it. If you’re not, you’ve walked a long way to see an old woman who let a ship go on the bank, and here she is.’

**Ask Loveday Kitto about the night of the wreck**

She folds the rag very small. ‘I lit at sunset. I’ve lit at sunset fourteen thousand nights. I trimmed her and I went down and made my tea, and I sat, and I must have slept.’ Her hands keep folding. ‘When I came up she was out. All eight wicks out, and cold. I don’t know how long. I didn’t hear the ship. You’d think you’d hear a ship.’ She looks toward the glass, not seeing it. ‘I’ve never slept at the Lantern. Never once. I don’t know how I came to.’

**Ask Loveday Kitto about Hender Brask**

Her face softens. ‘Hender. I had him from twelve, when Jacob went. He was a quiet boy. He’d sit up here with me all night and never say a word, only watch the light go out across the water.’ She smiles. ‘He brings my oil every Saturday in his own boat. Forty years. Never once missed.’ The smile goes. ‘He’ll turn me off at the Court. He has to. He came and told me himself, and cried, which I’ve not seen him do since he was twelve. I told him he must do his duty. He always does.’

**Ask Loveday Kitto about the pilots**

‘Oh, the pilots.’ She sits down on the iron step of the lamp-stand. ‘Twelve gigs on the Reach when I was a girl, painted all colours, racing out to the ships. Jacob Brask’s was blue. He used to say to me, Loveday, keep the Lantern lit and I’ll keep the Pulpit, and between us we’ll bring them home.’ She laughs, a little. ‘The leading mark. The Pulpit over the Holm. A ship coming in from the south keeps the notch in the Craigs right over this light, one on top of the other, and comes straight up the channel to Skeel in the dark without touching. Jacob used to light a lamp up on the Pulpit on the black nights, so they’d have both.’ She is quiet. ‘Nobody’s done that in forty years.’ Tamar gains 100 experience: learned the leading mark.

**Help Loveday Kitto light the Lantern**

She hands you the taper and talks you through it: trim each wick square with the scissors, so; turn it up a hair; light from the sea side round. You do the first two. Then she takes the scissors back to show you the third, and you watch her find the wick with her fingertips, and the scissors with her other hand, and bring them together very slowly, an inch from her face, and miss. She tries again, and finds it. She does not look at you. ‘There,’ she says. ‘Nothing to it.’ And you understand that she has not seen the flame of her own Lantern for a long time; that she keeps it by the warmth on her face.

**Ask Loveday Kitto how long she has not been able to see**

She sits very still. ‘Two winters,’ she says at last. ‘I can see the sun. I can see the lamp, a little, like a candle through a blanket. I can see the Craigs at sunset, a black line.’ Her hands are folded. ‘If I’d told anyone, they’d have given the Lantern to Jack Cawse, and he’d have let it go out the first night it rained. I thought: I’ll keep it till I can’t.’ Then, very low: ‘And then I couldn’t. And eleven men.’

Skeel does not know how to feel about any of it. It has never been paid to keep a ship whole.
