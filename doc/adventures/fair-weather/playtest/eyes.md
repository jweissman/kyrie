## day1

**To Fore Street**

Skeel is built of ships. The house-beams are ships’ beams, tarred black, with the treenail holes still in them; the doors are hatch-covers; a garden fence is a row of oars stuck in the ground. And on every gable, leaning out over the street, there is a figurehead: a woman with a lyre, a lion, a bishop, a mermaid with her nose gone, a man in a cocked hat, a horse, a child holding a dove. The town calls them the Wooden Saints and knows the name of every one. Fore Street runs along the top of the town, with the church at one end and an inn at the other, and a steep stepped lane, the Drang, falls away between the houses toward the harbour. Over the inn door is the biggest of the saints: an admiral in a gilt coat with a telescope under his arm and most of his nose shot away. Under him, the sign: THE ADMIRAL. Carts come up the Drang loaded with casks and wet bales, and men walk beside them with their hands on the load, the way you walk beside a coffin.

**To the quay**

A curved stone quay with a harbour inside it no bigger than a field. Herring boats lie in it with their sails furled, and the ferry lugger, and a long, narrow, very old rowing-gig pulled up on the slip with a girl working on her. On the quay: barrows, nets spread to dry, the Wreck-House with its iron-bound doors, and the fish cellars with a loft over them. On the bow of the blue gig, newly cut and gilded, a name: JACOB. Jinny Rowse’s Patience comes and goes round high water, while there is water at the quay, for whoever has paid her for the day. On the harbour wall a black tide-board, chalked fresh this morning: LOW WATER 11 AND 11. SANDS FROM 9. Under the chalk, painted and peeling: KEEP THE WITHIES. THE STRAND GIVES FOUR HOURS. THE SEA TAKES THE FIFTH. Here are Jinny Rowse, who sails the Patience; and Kerensa Brask, the Reeve’s daughter, with tar to the elbows.

**To the Slip**

A cobbled slipway goes down off the end of the harbour wall onto the Strand. At its foot a post is driven into the sand with a bell on it, and beside the post the first of the withies: a laurel branch stuck upright, then another, and another, away across the sand. The Strand is under the sea. The withies stick up out of the water in a line, a hand’s breadth showing, and the tide-board says when they will stand on sand again.

The sea has gone out of the Reach. The Strand lies ribbed and shining for miles, cut by the dark line of the Gut, with the Holm and its Lantern standing up out of it, and the wreck on Scarth Bank, and far off the Craigs. Gulls walk on it like people. Time passes.

**Pay Cuddy Pascoe a coin to walk you over the sands**

**To the open Strand**

You leave Skeel in the full of the morning, with the sun climbing and the path dry underfoot. Ribbed sand to every side, hard as a road where the withies are and softer off them, shining wet. Each ripple holds a thread of water. A line of laurel branches stuck upright leads away across it, one every twenty paces, toward the Holm, toward Scarth Bank, toward the Gut, toward the Craigs. A big black curly dog is trotting along the wrack line a hundred yards off, nose down, ribs showing. When you stop, he stops, and watches you, one forefoot lifted. He has a rope collar. When you take a step toward him he takes two away.

**To Scarth Bank, under the wreck**

The Constancy lies on her side on Scarth Bank with her back broken, her bow half buried and her stern cocked up in the air higher than a house, so that you walk under her counter as under a cliff. Her planking has sprung along the turn of the bilge and you can see into her: the ribs, the hold, the black inside of a ship. Weed has started on her already. The sand round her is trodden to porridge by boots and cartwheels. Down at the buried end, with the sand to her waist, is her figurehead: a girl of nine or ten, carved and painted, in a blue dress, holding an open book against her chest. The paint on her face is quite fresh. Somebody has tied a rope round her, ready, and not pulled. Twenty people of Skeel are at work on her: men in the hold passing up casks, women with barrows, boys on the yards cutting down what is left of her rigging. They work fast and do not talk much, and every so often somebody looks at the sea. Big Jack Cawse, the Reeve’s man, is here.

**Into the Admiral**

**To the Holm strand**

A hump of turf and grey rock a quarter-mile long, with sheep on it, and the Lantern on its crown: a squat round tower of whitewashed stone, with a glazed lamp-room on top like a lantern on a stick. At the tower’s foot, a low cell of older stone half sunk in the turf, its doorway facing the sea. A well with a stone lid. Washing on a line: one woman’s. On the well-lid, scratched deep and old: HOBB. And under it, newer, in chalk, the islanders’ joke: SALT SINCE THE WRECK YEAR. The Holm’s well went brackish forty years ago, the year the pilots went, and Skeel says the island is ashamed. Ewan Marrock’s coracle is a black hide bowl on the Skerry rocks, a mile off. It does not come for anyone he has not asked.

**Into the Lantern**

The tower’s one room: a bed, a chair, a table with a Bible of Lir and a teapot on it, a fire of driftwood. Everything is very clean and set exactly where it is, the way things are in a house where someone has learned to find them without looking. A stair goes up round the wall.

**Into the lamp-room**

The lamp-room: glass on every side, salt-dimmed, and in the middle on its iron stand the light, a great brass oil-lamp with eight wicks in a ring and a polished reflector behind them like a sunflower. The whole Reach is round you. To the south, across the water, the black line of the Craigs, and the high notch in them they call the Pulpit. Loveday Kitto, who keeps the Holm Lantern, is here.

**Talk to Loveday Kitto**

A small old woman in a grey shawl is polishing the great reflector with a rag, slowly, by touch, her face very close to the brass. She turns her head toward you, not quite at you. ‘If you’re from the Reeve,’ she says, ‘you can tell him I’ve polished it. If you’re not, you’ve walked a long way to see an old woman who let a ship go on the bank, and here she is.’

**Give Loveday Kitto a pair of spectacles from the Constancy**

She turns the tin case over in her hands, and opens it, and feels the spectacles inside, and puts them on, very carefully, hooking them over each ear. Then she is quite still. She looks at her hands. She looks at you, properly, for the first time. She looks out through the salt-dim glass at the whole Reach in the light, and the Craigs, and the Pulpit, and a long time passes. ‘Oh,’ she says. ‘Oh, there it all is.’ Then, because she has been a lamp-keeper fifty years and the first thing a lamp-keeper does with her eyes is look at her lamp, she goes to the stand and draws off a cup of oil from the reservoir and holds it up to the window. It is cloudy. At the bottom of the cup there are beads, clear as glass, that will not mix. She tips the cup, and they roll. ‘That’s water,’ she says. ‘That’s sea in my oil.’ She puts the cup down very gently on the iron. ‘Somebody put the sea in my oil.’ Tamar gains 200 experience: gave Loveday Kitto her eyes.

**Ask Loveday Kitto who filled the barrel**

‘You know who fills it.’ She is looking at the chalk on the barrel-head, SAT. — H.B., which she can read now, or has always known by heart. ‘Every Saturday for forty years.’ Her voice does not change. ‘I’ll not have you say it. Not you, and not the Court, and not anybody. If it was him, he’ll tell me. He never could lie to me. He used to try, when he was twelve, and his ears went red.’

**Ask Loveday Kitto to come with you to Skeel, to ask him**

She takes off her apron and folds it and puts on her other shawl, the good one. ‘I’ve not been in Skeel since Lir’s Day,’ she says. ‘They’ll stare.’ She takes your arm. ‘Let them.’ Loveday Kitto takes your arm, and holds it, and you go at her pace.
