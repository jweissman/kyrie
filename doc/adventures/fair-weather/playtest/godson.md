## skerry

**To Fore Street**

Skeel is built of ships. The house-beams are ships’ beams, tarred black, with the treenail holes still in them; the doors are hatch-covers; a garden fence is a row of oars stuck in the ground. And on every gable, leaning out over the street, there is a figurehead: a woman with a lyre, a lion, a bishop, a mermaid with her nose gone, a man in a cocked hat, a horse, a child holding a dove. The town calls them the Wooden Saints and knows the name of every one. Fore Street runs along the top of the town, with the church at one end and an inn at the other, and a steep stepped lane, the Drang, falls away between the houses toward the harbour. Over the inn door is the biggest of the saints: an admiral in a gilt coat with a telescope under his arm and most of his nose shot away. Under him, the sign: THE ADMIRAL. Carts come up the Drang loaded with casks and wet bales, and men walk beside them with their hands on the load, the way you walk beside a coffin.

**To the quay**

A curved stone quay with a harbour inside it no bigger than a field. Herring boats lie in it with their sails furled, and the ferry lugger, and a long, narrow, very old rowing-gig pulled up on the slip with a girl working on her. On the quay: barrows, nets spread to dry, the Wreck-House with its iron-bound doors, and the fish cellars with a loft over them. On the bow of the blue gig, newly cut and gilded, a name: JACOB. Jinny Rowse’s Patience comes and goes round high water, while there is water at the quay, for whoever has paid her for the day. On the harbour wall a black tide-board, chalked fresh this morning: LOW WATER 11 AND 11. SANDS FROM 9. Under the chalk, painted and peeling: KEEP THE WITHIES. THE STRAND GIVES FOUR HOURS. THE SEA TAKES THE FIFTH. Here are Jinny Rowse, who sails the Patience; and Kerensa Brask, the Reeve’s daughter, with tar to the elbows.

**To the Skerry landing**

You set out from Skeel in the full of the afternoon, the road pale with dust. A shelf of black rock with weed on it, slippery, and a ring in the rock to tie up to. Behind it the Skerry humps up, all rock and sea-pinks, with a turf bothy crouched in the lee of it, smoke going sideways from a hole in its roof. Seal-skins are pegged out to dry on the rock, and a coracle lies upside down like a black beetle. Ewan Marrock’s coracle is a black hide bowl on the Skerry rocks, a mile off. It does not come for anyone he has not asked. Ewan Marrock, the sealer of Seal Skerry, is here.

**Talk to Ewan Marrock**

A big man with a black beard going grey and a sealing-club through his belt is waiting on the rock with his arms folded before your boat has touched. ‘There’s nothing here for Skeel,’ he says. ‘No wreck. No dog. No anything. Go home.’

**Ask Ewan Marrock what he has against Skeel**

‘Against it?’ He looks across the water at the town on its headland. ‘Nothing. It’s a fine town. It buries what the sea sends it under stones that say A SAILOR, and it sings a hymn asking for more.’ He spits into the sea. ‘And when my wife went, it said she was a seal and had gone back to her own, and laughed at me in the Admiral for seven years. I’ve nothing against Skeel. I just don’t go there.’

**Ask Ewan Marrock about the seals**

His arms come unfolded. ‘Greys. They come in at the back end to pup. Fifty on the slab this year, more than last.’ He looks at them as you might look at a field of your own. ‘I take some. Oil and skins. Not the pups, not the cows. Old bulls, past it.’ A pause. ‘They sing, at night. Did they tell you that in Skeel? Skeel says it’s the seal-women singing for their men.’ He looks at the haul-out. ‘I don’t know what it is. I sit out and listen to it.’ Ewan Marrock jerks his head toward the bothy. ‘There’s a boy in there,’ he says. ‘Off your wreck. I found him on a hatch in the weed at the haul-out the morning after, more dead than not. I’ve kept him.’ He looks at you hard. ‘If you take him to Skeel, you take him over my body. That’s all.’

**Into Ewan Marrock's bothy**

Tamar gains 250 experience: found the boy on the Skerry. Inside the bothy: smoke, a peat fire, a box bed, a barrel of seal oil, skins, a lamp, and a great deal of order. On a shelf by itself, a woman’s comb, and a shell, and a blue ribbon, laid out the way things are laid out in a church. Nye Gallow, the Constancy’s boy, fourteen, with a bandaged head, is here.

**Talk to Nye Gallow**

A boy is sitting up in the box bed in the corner with a blanket round him and a bandage round his head, very white, holding Ewan’s sealing-knife in both hands pointed at you. ‘I’m not going,’ he says. ‘I’m not going to Skeel. They’ll do me like they want to do the dog.’

**Ask Nye Gallow what happened to the Constancy**

He tells it with the knife still in his hands, not fast. ‘Master Dunn had the Holm light on the chart. And there it was, high up, south, and he said good, that’s her, and brought her round for it.’ He swallows. ‘Mister Ferrar said it was too high. He said it twice. And then she hit, and the whole ship stood up on end, and I went over.’ His voice goes very flat. ‘And the light went out. Like somebody put their hand over it.’ He looks at the fire. ‘After, it was low water, and I was on a hatch, on the sand, in the dark, and all the others were — and a man came walking. With a lantern. A horn one. Tall, black coat. Marks under his eyes. He held it up and he saw me. He looked right at me.’ The boy’s hands are white on the knife. ‘And he turned round and walked away.’

## holm

**To the Holm strand**

A hump of turf and grey rock a quarter-mile long, with sheep on it, and the Lantern on its crown: a squat round tower of whitewashed stone, with a glazed lamp-room on top like a lantern on a stick. At the tower’s foot, a low cell of older stone half sunk in the turf, its doorway facing the sea. A well with a stone lid. Washing on a line: one woman’s. On the well-lid, scratched deep and old: HOBB. And under it, newer, in chalk, the islanders’ joke: SALT SINCE THE WRECK YEAR. The Holm’s well went brackish forty years ago, the year the pilots went, and Skeel says the island is ashamed. The sea lies between the Strand and the Holm, a mile of it, and a boat would be the way.

**Into the hermit's cell**

The oldest building on the Reach: a beehive of unmortared stone, so low you stoop, with a stone bench along one wall worn hollow by somebody’s sitting a very long time ago. Carved over the bench, a fish and a flame. It is the Lantern’s store now: a pile of coal, wicks in a box, cans, and a big iron-hooped oil barrel on a trestle with a tap. An oil barrel on a trestle, half full, with a brass tap and a tin cup hung on it. Chalked on the head: SAT. — H.B.

**Examine — Look closely**

You look closely at the oil barrel. You draw a cup from the tap and hold it to the doorway. Lamp oil should be clear and yellow as honey. This is cloudy, and at the bottom of the cup, beads, clear as glass, that roll and will not mix. You touch one and taste your finger. Salt.

**Out to the Holm strand**

A hump of turf and grey rock a quarter-mile long, with sheep on it, and the Lantern on its crown: a squat round tower of whitewashed stone, with a glazed lamp-room on top like a lantern on a stick. The sea is round the Holm, and laps at the turf.

**Into the Lantern**

The tower’s one room: a bed, a chair, a table with a Bible of Lir and a teapot on it, a fire of driftwood. Everything is very clean and set exactly where it is, the way things are in a house where someone has learned to find them without looking. A stair goes up round the wall.

**Into the lamp-room**

The lamp-room: glass on every side, salt-dimmed, and in the middle on its iron stand the light, a great brass oil-lamp with eight wicks in a ring and a polished reflector behind them like a sunflower. The whole Reach is round you. To the south, across the water, the black line of the Craigs, and the high notch in them they call the Pulpit. Loveday Kitto, who keeps the Holm Lantern, is here.

**Talk to Loveday Kitto**

A small old woman in a grey shawl is polishing the great reflector with a rag, slowly, by touch, her face very close to the brass. She turns her head toward you, not quite at you. ‘If you’re from the Reeve,’ she says, ‘you can tell him I’ve polished it. If you’re not, you’ve walked a long way to see an old woman who let a ship go on the bank, and here she is.’

**Ask Loveday Kitto about the night of the wreck**

She folds the rag very small. ‘I lit at sunset. I’ve lit at sunset fourteen thousand nights. I trimmed her and I went down and made my tea, and I sat, and I must have slept.’ Her hands keep folding. ‘When I came up she was out. All eight wicks out, and cold. I don’t know how long. I didn’t hear the ship. You’d think you’d hear a ship.’ She looks toward the glass, not seeing it. ‘I’ve never slept at the Lantern. Never once. I don’t know how I came to.’

**Ask Loveday Kitto about Hender Brask**

Her face softens. ‘Hender. I had him from twelve, when Jacob went. He was a quiet boy. He’d sit up here with me all night and never say a word, only watch the light go out across the water.’ She smiles. ‘He brings my oil every Saturday in his own boat. Forty years. Never once missed.’ The smile goes. ‘He’ll turn me off at the Court. He has to. He came and told me himself, and cried, which I’ve not seen him do since he was twelve. I told him he must do his duty. He always does.’ A tall man in a black coat, with marks under his eyes like bruises, who walked away from a boy in the sea. You have met him. He oils the charter board himself, every week.
