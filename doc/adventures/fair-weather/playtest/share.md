## dog

**To Fore Street**

Skeel is built of ships. The house-beams are ships’ beams, tarred black, with the treenail holes still in them; the doors are hatch-covers; a garden fence is a row of oars stuck in the ground. And on every gable, leaning out over the street, there is a figurehead: a woman with a lyre, a lion, a bishop, a mermaid with her nose gone, a man in a cocked hat, a horse, a child holding a dove. The town calls them the Wooden Saints and knows the name of every one. Fore Street runs along the top of the town, with the church at one end and an inn at the other, and a steep stepped lane, the Drang, falls away between the houses toward the harbour. Over the inn door is the biggest of the saints: an admiral in a gilt coat with a telescope under his arm and most of his nose shot away. Under him, the sign: THE ADMIRAL. Carts come up the Drang loaded with casks and wet bales, and men walk beside them with their hands on the load, the way you walk beside a coffin.

**To the quay**

A curved stone quay with a harbour inside it no bigger than a field. Herring boats lie in it with their sails furled, and the ferry lugger, and a long, narrow, very old rowing-gig pulled up on the slip with a girl working on her. On the quay: barrows, nets spread to dry, the Wreck-House with its iron-bound doors, and the fish cellars with a loft over them. On the bow of the blue gig, newly cut and gilded, a name: JACOB. Jinny Rowse’s Patience comes and goes round high water, while there is water at the quay, for whoever has paid her for the day. On the harbour wall a black tide-board, chalked fresh this morning: LOW WATER 11 AND 11. SANDS FROM 9. Under the chalk, painted and peeling: KEEP THE WITHIES. THE STRAND GIVES FOUR HOURS. THE SEA TAKES THE FIFTH. Here are Jinny Rowse, who sails the Patience; and Kerensa Brask, the Reeve’s daughter, with tar to the elbows.

**To the Slip**

A cobbled slipway goes down off the end of the harbour wall onto the Strand. At its foot a post is driven into the sand with a bell on it, and beside the post the first of the withies: a laurel branch stuck upright, then another, and another, away across the sand. The Strand is under the sea. The withies stick up out of the water in a line, a hand’s breadth showing, and the tide-board says when they will stand on sand again.

**Into the Wreck-House**

A stone barn with iron-bound doors, built for salvage. It is full to the rafters: grain in sacks stacked to the beams, casks of wine in rows on their sides, wet bales of cloth, a ship’s bell, coils of cable, a binnacle, chairs, a harmonium, a crate of something stencilled WITH CARE in a southern hand. Everything is chalked with a number. On a table by the door is a great book, and a lamp, and the Reeve. The smell of the grain is sweet and heavy and fills the place. There is enough in here, you think, to feed a town through a winter. That is what it is for. A pen of hurdles lashed together with cord and a padlocked chain. Here are Big Jack Cawse, the Reeve’s man; and Hender Brask, the Wreck-Reeve of the Gannet Reach.

**Talk to Hender Brask**

A tall man of fifty in a good black coat, with the book open in front of him and a pen in his hand. He has a very still, careful face, and has not slept; the skin under his eyes is the colour of a bruise. ‘Hender Brask, the Admiral’s Reeve,’ he says, and writes a number before he looks up. ‘If you have come to buy, the sale is the day after tomorrow. If you have come to sell, I do not buy. If you have come off the coast road to look at the wreck, everyone does, and you are welcome.’

**Ask Hender Brask whether there is work for a stranger**

He looks at you for a long moment, as if adding you up. ‘You have been out on the Strand,’ he says. ‘You walk it well enough. The dog that has been worrying the Holm sheep is out there, and the town’s boys cannot get near it.’ He takes a shilling from the drawer and lays it on the book, and beside it, slowly, four more coins. ‘A shilling for the dog, at the Wreck-House, before the Court sits. And a stranger’s portion of the Admiral’s third, which is mine to give, at the Court. Twelve coin, perhaps more.’ He does not look away. ‘It is a sheep-worrier. You understand me.’

**Tell Hender Brask you will see to the dog**

He nods, once, and puts the coins back in the drawer, all but the shilling. ‘Before the Court sits,’ he says. ‘The Gut is deep, off the Sill.’ He goes back to his writing, and you see that his hand is not steady, and that he sees you see it.

## court

**Wait › A while** *(chapter: The Wreck Court)*

At three the tide turns on the Strand, and in Skeel the shutters go up on the shops, and people put on their black and go down the Drang to the fish cellars, and climb the stair to the net-loft in silence. Time passes.

**Out to the quay**

A curved stone quay with a harbour inside it no bigger than a field. The harbour is full and the boats ride in it, knocking gently against the wall. Jinny Rowse, who sails the Patience, is here. On the harbour wall the tide-board, chalked fresh this morning: LOW WATER 1 AND 1. SANDS FROM 11.

**Into the fish cellars**

Long low vaults with the herring-presses in them, great beams with stones hung on the ends, standing idle. The salt is stacked waiting for fish that did not come. It is very cold.

**Into the net-loft over the cellars**

A long loft under the roof, where the nets are mended in winter. It has been swept, and benches set out in rows, and at the far end, a table with a cloth on it and one chair behind it: for the Wreck Court. Here are Mother Jago, who keeps the Admiral; Parson Hosken, the priest of Lir Who Gives; Big Jack Cawse, the Reeve’s man; Hender Brask, the Wreck-Reeve of the Gannet Reach; Loveday Kitto, who keeps the Holm Lantern; Kerensa Brask, the Reeve’s daughter, with tar to the elbows; and Constance Ferrar, who keeps the books of Ferrar and Daughters, shipowners.

**Wait › A while**

The net-loft is packed to the walls: Skeel on the benches in its Sunday black, men standing at the back with their hats in their hands, children on the beams. The air smells of tar and wet wool. At the far end Hender Brask sits at the table with the book open in front of him and the charter board, brought from the church, propped against the wall behind him, shining. Loveday Kitto sits alone on a stool in front of the table, in her good shawl, with her hands in her lap. Constance Ferrar stands at the side, by the window, with her ledger, very upright. Nobody stands near her. Time passes. Hender Brask stands. ‘The Wreck Court of the Gannet Reach,’ he says, ‘under the Admiral’s charter. The ship Constancy, Ferrar’s, struck on Scarth Bank on the night of Saturday last, in fair weather. If any man here knows of a living thing that came ashore of her, neither man nor dog nor cat, let him say so now.’ He waits, with the pen in his hand, and the loft is so quiet you can hear the tide coming in under the floor.

**Wait › A while**

Time passes. ‘No living thing came ashore of her,’ says the Reeve, ‘and the Constancy is wreck: two parts to Skeel, one to the Lord Admiral.’ He writes it. The loft lets out its breath, all at once, like a sail emptying.

**Wait › A while**

Time passes. ‘The second matter,’ says the Reeve, without looking up. ‘The Holm Lantern was dark on the night of the wreck. Its keeper, Loveday Kitto, asks no excuse.’ Loveday sits very straight on her stool. ‘I shall ask the Court to relieve her of the keeping, with thanks for forty-one years, and give the Lantern to John Cawse.’ Big Jack Cawse goes red to the ears.

**Wait › A while**

Time passes. ‘The keeper is relieved,’ says the Reeve, ‘with the thanks of the Reach.’ Loveday Kitto gets up off her stool and goes down the stair without looking at anybody, and Jack Cawse does not look at her either.

**Wait › A while**

Time passes. ‘The last matter,’ says the Reeve, and his voice is not quite steady, ‘is the winter.’ He looks at the book. He reads out the shares, house by house, two parts in three, and Skeel sits and listens to it like a congregation to a blessing. The grain will see them to the spring. The Admiral’s third will see to the factor. ‘The Admiral’s third,’ he says at the end, ‘is in my gift, and I give a stranger’s portion from it to the strangers who worked her,’ and looks at you. Jack Cawse comes along the bench to you with a little bag, and puts it in your hand without looking at you: the stranger’s portion. Six coin.

**Wait › A while**

Time passes. The Reeve closes the book. The loft empties down the stair in silence, onto a quay where the tide is in and the boats are knocking gently against the wall.

**Wait › An hour** *(chapter: The Third Ebb)*

On the third ebb, in the dark, the Reach gives back what it took.

**Wait › A while**

Time passes. Skeel eats that winter. The grain is shared out house by house, two parts in three, and the factor is paid at Martinmas out of the Admiral’s third, and the salt company does not come. The Constancy’s saint goes up on the Reeve’s gable beside the Lady with the Lyre, the girl with the open book, CONSTANT AND TRUE. On the first black night of the winter it goes out, and he does not notice.
