# The Pitch of the Glass: the story, before the code

*Pass 1. Fiction first; the Kyr that follows is a reading of it.*

## 1. The crate

Stairfoot is where the carriers' road gives up and the Organ Stair begins: seven turns, a cairn, a cut in the rock, a shelf, a saddle.
In the yard of the Mule and Mirror stands a crate the size of a cart-bed, packed in sixty fleeces and braced with ash poles.
Inside it is a mirror five feet across, ground by hand over four years, for the lower tower of the Star-Readers at Dun Rìerdan.
A mirror like that lets one lamp do the work of nine. The Readers have been burning whale-oil by the barrel, and the whalers stopped coming.

"Eleven mules," says Marta Vellacott, who is sixty-three and has driven the Stair since she was nineteen. "Alidade leads. Gnomon sulks.
The rest do as they are told by one or the other. You will be paid at the Saddle, and you will not touch the crate."

The crate hums.

Not loudly. Put a palm to the poles and it is there, low, in the bones of your wrist, like a cart passing in the next street.
"It does that," says Nesta Wennick, the grinder, sitting on the tail-board with her knees up. "It's the cold. It's the poles."
She has hummed to it every night for four years. She does not say this.

## 2. The man with the money

Dunstan Lathe sells lamp-oil to nine towers and four lighthouses and does not hate anyone. He buys the company a drink at the Mule and Mirror.
"A mirror that makes one lamp do nine lamps' work," he says, pleasantly, "is a very fine thing for the Readers. I'd like to know what it does for me.
Forty crowns, and a slip on the Shelf. Mules do slip. I would only ask that it be thorough."
He will take it well if you refuse. He is a man who has always taken things well.

## 3. The Cut

Above the cairn the Stair goes into a crack in the mountain, forty yards long, wide enough for two mules and a bad temper.
In the wall of the crack, seen from the cairn, are four stone pipes, each as big as a chimney, and when the wind gets up after ten in the morning
the Cut plays. Tobiah Lintern has kept its stops for forty-one years. He opens and shuts them with a pole and a mallet and keeps the Cut in one honest, dull key,
because mules can stand a dull key, and because carriers in a fog steer by it. Marta's husband took nine mules into the Cut one winter when the stops were choked with snow
and the Cut was dumb, and went off the Shelf in the white, and did not hear the edge coming.

Lintern is seventy-eight. His hands will not close on the high stops. What he wants, and does not say to anyone,
is to hear the Cut played once as it was built to be played: all four pipes, a chord, from one wind. He has never had a note to build it on.

A mirror that hums is a note.

## 4. What the glass does

The wind and the glass share a note. In the Cut, at midday, with the mirror on a mule's back and the pipes in their dull key,
the crate rings in sympathy, the ring grows, and somewhere in the crossing the ash poles start to buzz against the glass like a wasp in a jar.
Nesta knows what comes next. It has happened to a small mirror in her master's shop. She says nothing, because saying it means saying that she ground the mirror to a tune,
by ear, humming, and that the Readers' inspectors will want to know on whose authority.

Ways through: cross at dusk or before ten, when the Cut is quiet. Pack the crate in more fleece and take the ring out of it. Let Lintern shut the stops, which the Stair will not forgive for a year.
Or sing the glass's note to Lintern, and let him set the pipes to it, so that the wind and the glass hold the same note and neither can shake the other.
Or take Lathe's forty crowns.

## 5. The Saddle

At the Cold Saddle the mule train stops and the crate comes down and the fleeces come off. The mirror is held up, at dawn, to the sun.
A disc of light goes out over the valley, thirty miles, to a tower no one on the Saddle can see.
If the Cut has been tuned, the pipes are still sounding behind you, and the light and the chord go out together.
In the lower tower, a man who has trimmed wicks for nine years looks up from the lamp he is carrying, and puts it down.

## What I want a player to carry away

That a crossing can be made beautiful; that the wind and the glass can be brought to agree; that an old man's one wish and a young woman's one secret are the same note.
