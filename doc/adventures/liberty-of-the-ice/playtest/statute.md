# The verger, Gallowmoor, and the Statute of Servants

Seed `lisk`, the bard of the title page; fixture `statute`; 106 steps; ended at watch 420, at the ending "Landed"; 850 experience in all. Written by `bun scripts/liberty-of-the-ice-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The North Road comes down off the fells between walls of snow higher than your head, trodden to a trench by a month of feet. Below, in a white bowl of hills, Pellam: smoke going straight up from a thousand chimneys, a grey cathedral tower with a gilded pair of scales on its spire, a long bridge on many arches, and under the bridge, where the river ought to be, a street. A street of tents and smoke and pennants and people, on the river, a mile long. Faintly, all the way up here, a sound like a market and a wedding at once. The North Road goes up out of Pellam into the snow.

**By the North Road, to the North Bar**

You set out from the North Road, above Pellam early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. THE FIRST DAY OF THE FAIR. A hard blue frost, the sky like a glazed dish, not a cloud from the fells to the downs; the smoke of a thousand chimneys going straight up and flattening at a height, as if against a ceiling. Your breath freezes in your scarf. Sound carries: you can hear a hammer on the far side of the river, and a girl laughing on the bridge, and under everything, from the ice, the noise of the Fair, like a hive. The North Bar: a gate-tower with its gates open and snowed into the open position, and a toll-house nobody is in. On the gate somebody has nailed a broadside, half torn: THE GREAT FROST — A FAIR UPON THE ICE — ALL WELCOME — NO LAW BUT THE GLOVE. Beyond it the city goes down to the river in steep white streets. The North Road goes up out of Pellam into the snow.

**By Northgate Street, to the Cheaping**

The Cheaping, Pellam’s wool market: a great square with a market-cross and a covered hall on stone legs where the fleeces are sold in summer, empty now, snowed knee-deep, with paths trodden across it in every direction to the Exchange, to the Scale Church, to Fleece Row, and above all down Swan Lane to the river. Nobody is buying wool here. Everybody is going to the ice.

**By Swan Lane, to Swan Lane**

Swan Lane runs down steep between warehouses to the river and stops at the top of Swan Stairs, the watermen’s stairs, where in any other winter you would hail a boat. Below, at the foot of the stairs, there is no water: there is the Fair. Two watermen sit on the top of Swan Stairs with a plank across it between them, and a tin cup. ‘A penny a stair,’ says one of them. ‘Shake on it.’ A bailiff, a man in brown with a copy of the writ, at the head of Swan Stairs, is here.

**Shake on a penny a stair with the watermen**

You put out your hand, and the waterman spits on his and shakes it, very hard, and lifts the plank. ‘Twelve stairs,’ he says. ‘Twelve pennies. Pay at the bottom or pay at the Court, it’s all one to us.’ He grins. ‘Mind the ninth. It’s slippy.’ The watermen’s plank is lifted aside for you; they nod as you go by.

**By Swan Stairs, to the foot of Swan Stairs, on the ice**

The foot of Swan Stairs, on the ice. The river is white and hard from bank to bank, scarred grey with sledge-runners and skates, and up it, from the bridge to as far down as you can see, runs a street of booths made of oars and sailcloth, with smoke going straight up from every one of them into a sky the colour of pewter. There is a noise like a market and a wedding at once. A boy goes past on bones strapped to his boots, very fast, shrieking. The watermen’s plank is lifted aside for you; they nod as you go by.

**By the ice, to Freezeland Street, by the Glove Post**

Somebody knocks into you in the crush by the Glove Post, a woman in a caped coat, who says ‘Beg pardon, my love,’ and goes on, and when you put your hand to your purse it is not there. You turn round. She has stopped ten feet away and is holding it up by its strings for you to see, and laughing. ‘Never shook on it,’ she calls. ‘Did I? Did you?’ A few people nearby laugh too. Nobody calls the watch. There is no watch. A bill is pasted on the Glove Post, new this morning, the ink smudged with frost: THE GREAT FROST FAIR, FIRST DAY. — HIRING at the RING, TEN till NOON, by Master J. HARKER, for a farm in the hills. — The LIBERTY CRIED at NOON. — The COURT of the ICE sits at FOUR. — The LIARS’ BENCH at the FROZEN LAMB from SEVEN. — A BEAR. AN OX. FROST-CREAM. — NO LAW BUT THE GLOVE. Beside the stocks stands a pillory nobody is in, with snow on the board where the head goes, and a placard hung on it ready, blank, for whatever the Court writes there. At the top of Freezeland Street a ship’s mast has been stepped in the ice and guyed with ropes, and nailed to the head of it, stiff with frost, is a leather glove as big as a sail-bag: the sign of the Liberty. A board at the foot of the mast says in red: SAY WHAT YOU WILL. KEEP WHAT YOU SHAKE ON. C. BROOM, REEVE. Freezeland Street, at its top end, by the Glove Post: the thick of the Fair. Booths of sailcloth on oars, gin and gingerbread, hot codlings, a skittle alley where men bowl a frozen sheep’s head, a swing-boat on a frame, a man selling slices of the ox, a man selling knowledge of the future, a man selling nothing anyone can see with great conviction. Everyone is shouting the price of something. Nobody is saying what anything is worth. In the lee of the Glove Post, a lean-to of sailcloth on two oars, with a brazier, a folding chair, a bottle and a gentleman in it. At the foot of the Glove Post, on an upturned keg, a thin old man in a green-black gown is preaching to a crowd that laughs and shouts back. ‘Look under your feet!’ he cries. ‘This is a river! Yesterday it ran; tomorrow it will run; today you have built a street on it and called it a country and made a law for it. Nothing stays, my friends. Not this ice, not that glove, not the man who shook hands with you on Monday. He is gone! He went out with the river! Why should you pay his debts?’ A waterman throws a snowball at him. He catches it, and eats it. At the foot of the Glove Post stand the Court’s stocks, empty. Here are Ursel Cobb, a woman in a man’s caped coat, with a laugh like a door; Sir Lionel Gant, a gentleman in a waistcoat that was magnificent ten years ago, living on the ice; and Magister Edrin Callow, a thin old preacher in a black gown gone green, coughing, with a crowd.

**Ask her for your purse back**

‘Course you can have it.’ She tosses it to you. ‘Lesson’s free.’ She watches you count it. ‘All there. I’m not a thief, I’m a teacher. On the ice a word’s free and a hand’s bound, and I never gave you my hand, and you never asked for it.’ She grins. ‘Ursel Cobb. And you’ve a face nobody would ever suspect of anything, which is worth more than what was in there.’

**By the ice, to the foot of Swan Stairs, on the ice**

The foot of Swan Stairs, on the ice.

**By Swan Stairs, to Swan Lane**

The watermen’s plank is lifted aside for you; they nod as you go by. A bailiff, a man in brown with a copy of the writ, at the head of Swan Stairs, is here. The top of Swan Stairs, and the Fair below.

**By Swan Lane, to the Cheaping**

The Cheaping: the empty wool-hall, the snowed cross, the paths to everywhere and down to the ice.

**By the Scale Church door, to the Scale Church**

The Scale Church, Saint Evenhand’s in the Cheaping: a tall grey church, cold as a well, and in the crossing under the tower, where another church would have its altar, the great honest scales of the saint, brass, taller than a man, on which the wool of Pellam has been weighed since there was wool. A board beside them in gold: A FALSE BALANCE IS ABOMINATION. Somebody has left a single snowdrop in one pan, and the beam has gone down a hair on that side. Amos Tallis, the verger of the Scale Church, very old, with a broom and a cold, is here.

**Ask the verger about the scales**

‘The Saint’s.’ He puts his hand flat on the great brass pan, as a man puts his hand on a horse. ‘Every fleece Pellam ever sold went over these, once. The factors weigh their own now, in their own houses; they say the Saint’s are slow.’ He sniffs. ‘They are slow. They’re honest, and honest is slow.’ He nods at the board in gold beside them. ‘A false balance is abomination. And they bring their own weights to church on Sundays, and sit under that, and sing.’

**Ask the verger about the board in the porch with the writing worn off it**

‘The Statute of Servants.’ He goes with you to look at it, slowly. You can make out a word or two under the soot: HIRED, LIBERTIES, CARRIED. ‘The city made it after the Black Lambing, when the fell farms were sold, and the factors were hiring the fell children in the Cheaping and carrying them off to the dye-works over the hills and selling them on. No one hired within the liberties of Pellam shall be carried out of them, but to the place they were hired for. That’s the gist.’ He looks at it. ‘It’s never been repealed. It’s only been gone round.’ He nods down the hill, toward the river. ‘The ice isn’t the city. It’s the Watermen’s, bank to bank. The Statute doesn’t run on it. Why do you think the hiring’s on the ice?’

**Ask the verger about Master Harker, the churchwarden**

‘A good churchwarden.’ He sweeps a little round the scales. ‘Pays for the candles. Every spring after the hiring he pays for a great candle, a pound of wax, to burn a week before the Saint. I asked him once what it was for.’ He stops sweeping. ‘He said, for the weight. I didn’t ask him what he meant. You don’t, with a churchwarden.’

**Wait › An hour**

THE SECOND DAY OF THE FAIR. Fog, in the night, off the Reach: the watermen call it the drowned weather. It stands on the ice to the height of a house, white and wet and very still, so that from the bridge the Fair is a roof of sound with nothing under it, and in Freezeland Street the booths come at you out of it one at a time like ships. The cathedral bell sounds as if it were being rung in a cupboard. People speak low without knowing why.

**Wait › An hour**

Word comes down Freezeland Street at a run: the bear’s off his chain at the bear-stake, and gone to the ox roast, and is eating the Butchers’ Company’s ox off the spit with his muzzle torn off, and nobody will go near him.

**Wait › An hour**

All over the Fair this morning: another hat on the stake at the Black Reach. Jem Rudd’s, the carter’s lad from Garthwaite, hired yesterday at the Ring; he was in Kemble’s line at noon and not in the bondhouse at dark. The river has taken him, everyone says, and touches the ice with a knuckle.

**By the way to the ox roast, to the ox roast**

The ox roast: a whole ox on an iron spit over a fire of sea-coal laid on a hearth of bricks, so that the ice will not melt under it, which it is doing all the same, slowly; the hearth has sunk a hand’s breadth into a pool of its own making and the Butchers’ Company’s men stand round it arguing. The smell is extraordinary. Slices are a penny, on bread. Absolute is at the ox. He has pulled the spit down and is lying on the sunken hearth in the steam with the ox between his paws, eating it, and making a sound like a cart going over cobbles. The Butchers’ Company’s men stand in a ring well back with poles and cleavers, saying what they will do. **By the ice past the last booth, to the head of the Reach, past the last booth**

Past the last booth the Fair stops as if at a wall, and there is only ice: grey, wide, and very quiet, with the river showing black through it in places like a vein. A line of sledge-marks goes away down the middle of the Reach, and people keep to it. Far down, a hulk sits locked in the ice at an angle, with her masts gone.

**By the grey ice of the Reach, to the Black Reach**

On a stake of the rope round the black hole a hat has been hung, a carter’s felt hat with a whipcord round the crown, very neatly, as if on a peg. Under it, in the snow, someone has put a penny. In the fog the Black Reach has no other side. The ice creaks, close, and then far, and then close again, as if something were walking on it. The Black Reach: the deep channel, where the Lisk still runs under the ice, and the ice over it is grey, and creaks when you stand still. Off to one side, near the bank, a ring of stakes with a rope round them marks a hole that has never frozen over, black water breathing a little steam. Someone has put a penny on one of the stakes. The sledge marks run on south over hard ice.

**By the ice out to the hulk, to the deck of the Patience**

The deck of the Patience: a collier-brig frozen in on her way up to the city a month ago and left, her masts struck, her deck white, listing to starboard in the ice. A hatch, closed. Her name is still on her stern in flaking gold.

**By the Patience's hatch, to the hold of the Patience**

The hold of the Patience, sloping with her list: coal-dust, ballast stone, a brazier in a bucket of sand, the hull sweating frost. It smells of people.

**Search here**

You search the hold of the Patience with care, a little at a time. Behind the ballast, where the hull curves in, there is a breath of warmth that is not the brazier’s, and blankets, and in the blankets people.

**Ask Dickon Cobb how he came back**

‘Walked.’ He coughs. ‘Gallowmoor’s a hole in a hill with a town on top. You go down at five and you come up at dark, and the lead gets in your chest, and nobody’s on a farm in the hills.’ He sits on the ballast. ‘Three of us came out in September. Two got here.’ He looks at the others. ‘I can’t stop the hiring. I can get some of them out of the line on the ice, and drown them, and when the river’s open I can get them down it on a barge. The ice was a gift: I can walk them down the Reach on the sledge road, nights.’ He coughs again. ‘The searchers walk it too, now.’ Tamsin gains 300 experience: found that the drowned were not drowned. Tamsin gains 300 experience: found Dickon Cobb.

**Ask Dickon what Gallowmoor is like**

He is a while answering. ‘Dark,’ he says. ‘You’d think it’d be the dark you minded. It isn’t. It’s the counting.’ He coughs. ‘There’s a man called Corder, the overman, stands at the top of the ladder at five with a slate, and you put your hand in his as you go down past him, so he can feel it’s a hand, and he chalks it. And at night again, coming up. Hand, chalk. Hand, chalk. He doesn’t look at your face; he’s never once looked at my face.’ He looks at his own palm. ‘There’s eleven hundred hands at Gallowmoor. Some of them have been there since the Black Lambing, they say, father to son. There’s a chapel. There’s a song they sing going down, about the hills.’ He does not sing it. ‘They’re very deep hills.’

**Tell Alderman Gosling that Dickon Cobb was hired in the Cheaping last spring and carried to Gallowmoor, against the Statute of Servants**

He puts his pen down. ‘In the Cheaping. Not on the ice.’ He gets up and takes a book down from the shelf behind him, a fat one in calf with a chain on its spine, and turns it to a page he does not need to look for. ‘On the ice I am nobody, any more than any of us. The fire settles twice. ‘Harker,’ he says at last, to the book, ‘is going to be my father-in-law, or was.’ He writes, carefully, a long time, and sands it, and folds it. ‘At the Lady Day sessions,’ says Alderman Gosling, ‘on dry ground. Where a lie is a crime.’ Tamsin gains 250 experience: laid the Statute of Servants before the city.

In the night, from down the Reach, a long sound of saws, a dozen of them, and men singing to keep the time.
