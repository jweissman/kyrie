# The second night on foot: Kemble's gin, the watermen, and the wolves on the sledge road

Seed `lisk`, a warrior (laborer); fixture `wolves`; 112 steps; ended at watch 420, at the ending "Not Landed"; 1550 experience in all. Written by `bun scripts/liberty-of-the-ice-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The North Road comes down off the fells between walls of snow higher than your head, trodden to a trench by a month of feet. Below, in a white bowl of hills, Pellam: smoke going straight up from a thousand chimneys, a grey cathedral tower with a gilded pair of scales on its spire, a long bridge on many arches, and under the bridge, where the river ought to be, a street. A street of tents and smoke and pennants and people, on the river, a mile long. Faintly, all the way up here, a sound like a market and a wedding at once. The North Road goes up out of Pellam into the snow.

**By the North Road, to the North Bar**

You set out from the North Road, above Pellam early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. THE FIRST DAY OF THE FAIR. A hard blue frost, the sky like a glazed dish, not a cloud from the fells to the downs, and the sun on the snow so bright it hurts to look at. Your breath freezes in your scarf. Sound carries: you can hear a hammer on the far side of the river, and a girl laughing on the bridge, and under everything, from the ice, the noise of the Fair, like a hive. The North Bar: a gate-tower with its gates open and snowed into the open position, and a toll-house nobody is in. On the gate somebody has nailed a broadside, half torn: THE GREAT FROST — A FAIR UPON THE ICE — ALL WELCOME — NO LAW BUT THE GLOVE. Beyond it the city goes down to the river in steep white streets. The North Road goes up out of Pellam into the snow.

**By Northgate Street, to the Cheaping**

The Cheaping, Pellam’s wool market: a great square with a market-cross and a covered hall on stone legs where the fleeces are sold in summer, empty now, snowed knee-deep, with paths trodden across it in every direction to the Exchange, to the Scale Church, to Fleece Row, and above all down Swan Lane to the river. Nobody is buying wool here. Everybody is going to the ice.

**By Swan Lane, to Swan Lane**

Swan Lane runs down steep between warehouses to the river and stops at the top of Swan Stairs, the watermen’s stairs, where in any other winter you would hail a boat. Below, at the foot of the stairs, there is no water: there is the Fair. Two watermen sit on the top of Swan Stairs with a plank across it between them, and a tin cup. ‘A penny a stair,’ says one of them. ‘Shake on it.’ A bailiff, a man in brown with a copy of the writ, at the head of Swan Stairs, is here.

**Step over the plank and go down without paying · an even chance**

You step over the plank and the bigger waterman stands up and puts you back on the top step with one hand, not roughly. ‘A penny a stair,’ he says. ‘Or the bridge, if you know the way, which you don’t.’ Sam fails the brawn check (d20 9 + strength 1 + brawn 2 = 12 against 13).

**Shake on a penny a stair with the watermen**

You put out your hand, and the waterman spits on his and shakes it, very hard, and lifts the plank. ‘Twelve stairs,’ he says. ‘Twelve pennies. Pay at the bottom or pay at the Court, it’s all one to us.’ He grins. ‘Mind the ninth. It’s slippy.’ The watermen’s plank is lifted aside for you; they nod as you go by.

The foot of Swan Stairs. Up the river the booths of Freezeland Street are lit from inside like paper lanterns, and every one of them is making a noise. The watermen’s plank is lifted aside for you; they nod as you go by.

**By Freezeland Street, to Freezeland Street, the lower end**

The lower end of Freezeland Street, where the booths are older and the jokes worse. A printing booth with a press in it, clanking. A gin-tent with a painted sign of a lamb on skates. **By the Frozen Lamb's flap, to the Frozen Lamb**

The Frozen Lamb: a gin-tent the size of a barn, sailcloth on a frame of oars, a brazier in a tub of sand at either end and a bar made of a ship’s hatch-cover. Benches. Hot gin with nutmeg on it. At the far end, under a lantern, one bench by itself has LIARS painted on its back. The tent’s keeper is a big handsome woman in her fifties with her sleeves rolled up, who looks at everyone who comes in as if she had been expecting someone else. On the Liars’ Bench under the lantern sits Justice Broom with her ear-trumpet and a glass of hot gin, and round her a crowd, waiting. Whoever has a lie stands on the bench opposite and tells it, and the crowd shouts, and Broom listens with her eyes shut. Alderman Gosling sits at the end of the Frozen Lamb’s bar over a hot gin he does not drink, and does not look at Bel Ashby, so carefully that everyone else in the tent does. Here are Justice Clemency Broom, the Reeve of the Ice, a waterman’s widow of seventy with an ear-trumpet; Bel Ashby, the keeper of the Frozen Lamb; Ursel Cobb, a woman in a man’s caped coat, with a laugh like a door; Silas Kemble, a clerk, thin, with a red nose and a quill behind his ear; and Alderman Hamnet Gosling, an alderman of Pellam, stout and anxious, with ink on his cuffs.

**Stand Silas Kemble hot gin at the Lamb, one after another, and listen**

Hot gin with Bel’s honey in it, and then another, and Kemble’s red nose goes redder and his voice goes up, thin and confiding, a clerk’s voice, all figures. ‘Eight a head,’ he says, at the fourth, to his glass. ‘Eight a head, landed, and it costs him a farthing a day to keep them in that barn. Gallowmoor. Gallowmoor’s the hills, I suppose, in a manner of speaking. Very deep hills.’ He laughs at that, a long time, and then puts his head on his arms on the table, and the key on his waistcoat chain swings and taps against the bench.

**Unhook the bondhouse key from Kemble's waistcoat chain while he sleeps on the table**

The chain is a cheap one and the hook comes undone with a thumbnail. Kemble says ‘Seven, eight,’ into his sleeve, and does not wake. Bel, behind her plank, watches you do it, and goes on wiping a cup.

The foot of Hay Stairs, on the Bankside shore of the ice. **By Hay Stairs, to the head of Hay Stairs**

The head of Hay Stairs, on the Bankside shore, with the bondhouse on Hay Wharf beside it, and below, the ice. The waterman lifts the plank aside for you. The bailiffs, a pair of bailiffs in brown with a writ, stamping in the snow at the head of Hay Stairs, are here.

**By the lane to Hay Stairs, to Bankside**

Bankside: the other Pellam, across the river from the respectable one. Inns, a shut playhouse with snow on its roof, cookshops, a chandler’s, the Watermen’s Hall with its oars crossed over the door, and the long wall of the Clink, which is a prison for debtors and a liberty of its own, so that a man inside it owes nobody anything until he comes out.

**By the Watermen's Hall door, to the Watermen's Hall**

The Watermen’s Hall: a long room with the Company’s oars on the walls, a brazier, and thirty men with nothing to do sitting on benches doing it. Ice-saws hang by the door, long two-handled saws with teeth like a pike’s. At a table under the window an old waterman with a face like a clenched fist is writing in the Company’s book. Here are Gaffer Tredgold, the master of the Watermen’s Company, idle a month; and the watermen.

**Tell Gaffer Tredgold what Harker does with his hired, on the Watermen's ice**

He hears it without moving. Then he gets up, slowly, and goes to the window, and looks across at Hay Wharf, at the long blind wall with J. HARKER. WOOL on it. ‘On our ice,’ he says. ‘Under our Liberty. Under Clemmy’s glove.’ He turns round. The Hall has gone quiet. ‘The Company’ll not cut its own ice while there’s that to be done on it,’ says Gaffer Tredgold. ‘What do you want of us?’ Sam gains 150 experience: set the watermen to work.

**Wait › An hour**

Fog stands in the street to the height of the booth-roofs; the pennants at their peaks are lost in it, and their ropes go up into nothing. THE SECOND DAY OF THE FAIR. Fog, in the night, off the Reach: the watermen call it the drowned weather. It stands on the ice to the height of a house, white and wet and very still, so that from the bridge the Fair is a roof of sound with nothing under it, and in Freezeland Street the booths come at you out of it one at a time like ships. The cathedral bell sounds as if it were being rung in a cupboard. People speak low without knowing why.

**Wait › An hour**

Word comes down Freezeland Street at a run: the bear’s off his chain at the bear-stake, and gone to the ox roast, and is eating the Butchers’ Company’s ox off the spit with his muzzle torn off, and nobody will go near him.

**Wait › An hour**

All over the Fair this morning: another hat on the stake at the Black Reach. Jem Rudd’s, the carter’s lad from Garthwaite, hired yesterday at the Ring; he was in Kemble’s line at noon and not in the bondhouse at dark. The river has taken him, everyone says, and touches the ice with a knuckle.

**Wait › An hour**

The Hiring Ring: a rope on stakes round a trodden circle of ice, and inside it twenty or thirty young people from the north country, standing very still in the cold with a token in their hats for what they can do: a tuft of wool for a shepherd, a whipcord for a carter, a mop-head, a twist of straw. Their breath goes up. Master Harker walks round inside the rope in his fur collar with his clerk behind him, and stops in front of one, and talks a little, kindly, and puts out not his hand but his open palm, and waits; and the one in front of him shakes it, and Kemble writes. A girl with a milk-pail in her hat, who cannot be more than fifteen, has just shaken. An hour passes.

**Put a tuft of straw in your hat and stand in the Hiring Ring**

You stand in the cold with the others with a twist of straw in your hat, and after a while Master Harker comes round to you in his fur collar and looks at you as a man looks at a horse at a price he likes. ‘A strong back,’ he says kindly. ‘And an honest face. A year’s place on a farm in the hills: good food, a roof, wages at the year’s end.’ He holds out his palm, open, and waits. You shake it. His hand is soft and very warm. ‘Kemble,’ he says, without looking round, and Kemble writes you down.

An hour passes. Bess gets up, stretches, and comes over to push her nose into your hand.

The hiring ends. Kemble walks you off the ice in the line with the rest, two by two, across to Hay Stairs, up onto the wharf, and in at the bondhouse door, and you hear the bar go down outside. The bondhouse floor: a long dim barn of a place smelling of wool and tallow, fleeces stacked to the beams down one side, and down the other, on straw, Harker’s hired, sitting close together for the warmth with blankets round them. **Read Kemble's bond book (Sam)**

This Fair’s hirings: Tansy Hobb, fifteen, Swale Head, pail; Amos Pell, nineteen, Garthwaite, whip; and on, name by name, each with a little drawn hand. **Talk to Tansy Hobb**

Tansy Hobb looks at you the way she must look at a strange cow: carefully, and from a little way off. ‘Are you hired too?’

**Tell Tansy Hobb where the farm in the hills is**

She does not cry. She puts her milk-pail down on the straw, carefully, as if it might spill. ‘Lead,’ she says. ‘Mam said.’ Round her the others have gone quiet, listening; somebody says ‘Gallowmoor’ in a whisper, and it goes down the straw like a draught. A big lad with a whip in his hat stands up. ‘Then we’ll not go,’ he says. ‘We’ll not.’ Tansy looks at the barred river door, and then at you. ‘We shook,’ she says. ‘We all shook.’ Sam gains 300 experience: went into the bondhouse as one of the hired.

**Ask Tansy Hobb about home**

‘Swale Head.’ She brightens. ‘Under the fell. We’ve the low farm, by the wash-dub. Three cows, and Gran’s goat, which is a devil.’ She counts on her fingers. ‘Nell, and Moss, and Lady. Lady won’t stand for anyone but me. She’ll be kicking our Sam into the beck.’ She laughs, and then stops. ‘Gran said not to shake. She said there was a Swale Head lass once that never shook on anything in her life, and went to the town and did very well.’ She looks at her hand. ‘I don’t know who that was.’

**Wait › An hour**

In the dark at the far end of the straw somebody is singing, very low, the song a dairymaid sings to keep a cow standing at the milking: come up, Cushie, come up, my lady, it’s only me. It is Tansy Hobb, with her milk-pail in her lap. Nobody tells her to stop. An hour passes.

**Wait › An hour**

Far off down the river, past the end of the Fair, past the Reach, a sound you take at first for the wind in the bridge: rising, and falling, and rising, and then two of it, and then four. Wherever people are awake in Pellam they stop talking to listen. Somebody near you says, ‘Wolves. On the river,’ and somebody else says, ‘They’ll be after the ox,’ and laughs, and nobody laughs with him.

**Wait › An hour**

Late in the night, from down the Reach, a roar, and dogs, and men shouting, and lanterns going every way at once over the ice. In the morning they say Harker’s searchers met Hester Mabb’s bear on the Black Reach in the dark, and ran, all three, and the dog with them, back to Hay Wharf, and would not go out again; and that the bear went on down the grey ice toward the hole that never freezes, and through it.

**Wind a stave in a greasy fleece off the stack, and light it at the bondhouse brazier**

Harker’s fleeces are full of their own grease; one of them round the end of a stave out of the straw-rack burns like a church candle, low and yellow and smoking. Tansy Hobb watches you light it and says nothing, and then says, ‘For the wolves, is it,’ as if everyone knew there were wolves. The brand hisses and spits in your fist, and the dark goes back from it a little way on every side.

**Get the hired up, all together, to work the iced bar off the river door**

Twenty pairs of hands on the baulk, and the big lad with the whip counting, and Tansy Hobb pouring hot water out of the brazier-kettle into the iron brackets a cupful at a time: the ice cracks, and the bar shifts, and on the third heave it comes up out of its brackets with a shriek of iron, and the river door swings a hand’s breadth open on the dark and the ice. The bar is off the river door, and the door stands a hand’s breadth open on the ice.

**Bring the hired out through the river door onto the ice**

One at a time, through the low door in the river-wall, crouching, onto the ice under the wharf, in the dark: Tansy Hobb first with her pail clutched to her, and the big lad with the whip last. Nobody speaks. Above you on the wharf, old Fowke’s brazier glows. The ice under the piles is black and ridged and nobody has walked on it since it froze. The foot of Hay Stairs, on the Bankside shore of the ice. Here are Harker’s hired, a line of young people from the north country, hired at the Ring; and Tansy Hobb, a dairymaid from Swale Head, fifteen, with a milk-pail on a string. The lower end of Freezeland Street: the clanking press, the Frozen Lamb, the ribboned booth of the Ice Chapel. Here are Harker’s hired, a line of young people from the north country, hired at the Ring; and Tansy Hobb, a dairymaid from Swale Head, fifteen, with a milk-pail on a string.

The ox roast: a whole ox on an iron spit over a fire of sea-coal laid on a hearth of bricks, so that the ice will not melt under it, which it is doing all the same, slowly; the hearth has sunk a hand’s breadth into a pool of its own making and the Butchers’ Company’s men stand round it arguing. In the small hours of the last day, in the stocks at the foot of the Glove Post, Magister Edrin Callow stopped coughing. The sledge marks run on south over hard ice. Here are Harker’s hired, a line of young people from the north country, hired at the Ring; and Tansy Hobb, a dairymaid from Swale Head, fifteen, with a milk-pail on a string.

**By the sledge marks south, to the sledge road south, on the ice**

You go out of the Black Reach into the dark, by what light there is. On the sledge road ahead of you, where the ice goes on grey and empty into the dark, four shapes are sitting in a row, the way dogs sit outside a butcher’s. They get up, without hurry. One of them puts its head down, and you hear it: a sound like a saw in green wood, very low. The sledge marks go on south down the middle of the river, where the Lisk is wide and frozen hard, toward the downs and the south road beyond the snow. Nobody is on them. From here you can see the whole Fair behind you, very small, smoking. The sledge marks run on south over hard ice. Here are the wolves off the downs, four of them, grey, lean as rakes with the winter; Harker’s hired, a line of young people from the north country, hired at the Ring; and Tansy Hobb, a dairymaid from Swale Head, fifteen, with a milk-pail on a string.

**Hold the brand up high and walk straight at the wolves, swinging it**

You walk at them with the brand over your head, and it roars in the wind of your going, and drips burning on the ice. The nearest backs off a step, with its ears flat, and snaps at the sparks; then they are all going, sideways, unhurried, offended, down the sledge road into the dark, and the dark closes behind them. Your hand is shaking so that the brand shakes. Nobody behind you says anything at all. Sam gains 200 experience: got past the wolves on the sledge road without a fight.

**Send the hired on down the sledge road south, on foot, toward the downs**

They go away from you down the sledge road on foot in a long line, south toward the downs and the snow, holding each other’s coats, Tansy Hobb looking back once. Nobody behind you. The Reach is grey and empty and enormous. By the time it is light they will be off the river and into the snow of the downs, where the roads are shut, and where nobody from Pellam can follow them until the thaw, and by then they will not be landed, and not anyone’s. **Wait › An hour**

At Hay Stairs, as the watermen hurry the last of the Fair off the ice, the bailiffs are waiting with their writ, and Sir Lionel Gant walks up the stairs into their hands with his hat on, very straight, saying ‘It was three’ to the bailiff on his left. **Wait › An hour**

Under your feet, all over Pellam, the ice has begun to talk: a long creak like a ship’s timbers, a crack like a gun a long way off, and then a groan that goes on and on, up the whole length of the river. On the ice everybody stops and listens, and laughs, too loudly, and goes on. THE THIRD DAY OF THE FAIR, AND THE LAST. The wind has gone round to the west in the night, soft and wet, smelling of earth for the first time in a month: the thaw-wind. The icicles along Fleece Row are dripping. Under Pellam Bridge, from every arch, water is running in threads down the stone, and where it falls the ice has gone grey and pocked, like old pewter. Nobody on the Fair says the word. Everybody looks at the sky. A waterman who walked down the Reach at first light to look at the ice comes back up it fast, and stands in the Calf with his cap in his hands. The drowned went south in the night, he says: the tracks of seven or eight people on the sledge road, going south; and over them, the other tracks, four, going the same way, and then not the same way, going round. A cap. A great deal of trodden snow. He did not go on past it. Nobody in Bankside asks him to go back. Another hat on the stake at the Black Reach this morning: Polly Sneath’s, the shepherd’s girl from the second hiring. The watermen have stopped laughing about the Lisk Wife. Master Harker kisses his daughter, and shakes his son-in-law’s hand, and smiles all through the wedding-breakfast at the Frozen Lamb, where Bel Ashby serves it herself and does not look at anyone.

*(5 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

Of the Lisk Wife: Widow Saffery stood on the bridge all the time the ice was going out, with her lantern, and when it had gone, blew it out, and went in. Of the wedding: Alderman Gosling and his bride went home to Fleece Row after the Landing, and at midnight, they say, when the ice went, the alderman was seen alone on the bridge in his chain, looking down at it. Of the debtor: Sir Lionel Gant is in the Fleece Row compter for thirty coin, saying to anyone who visits him that it was three. Of the bear: Hester Mabb sat all day at the bear-stake with the broken chain in her lap, and went up the North Road this morning alone.

**Wait › An hour**

In the morning there is a river under Pellam Bridge, and on the far side of the downs, in the snow, twenty people from the north country walking north-about to the fells by roads nobody in Pellam can follow until the thaw comes up the valleys; not landed, and not anybody’s. Master Harker’s bondhouse on Hay Wharf stands open on the river with its straw trodden and its brazier cold. Kemble’s book has twenty little drawn hands in it, for shaken, and against every one, in the Court’s book, nothing at all. On the Black Reach the hole that never froze is only river now, like the rest.
