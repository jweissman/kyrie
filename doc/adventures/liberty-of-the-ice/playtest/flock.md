The watermen found him at first light with his blanket round him and his brazier out, and the snow not melting on his face. Someone has written on the board at the foot of the Glove, under KEEP WHAT YOU SHAKE ON, in charcoal: NOTHING STAYS.

**Wait › An hour**

THE THIRD DAY OF THE FAIR, AND THE LAST. The wind has gone round to the west in the night, soft and wet, smelling of earth for the first time in a month: the thaw-wind. The icicles along Fleece Row are dripping. Under Pellam Bridge, from every arch, water is running in threads down the stone, and where it falls the ice has gone grey and pocked, like old pewter. Nobody on the Fair says the word. Everybody looks at the sky.

**Wait › An hour**

They are saying on the ice that the Squire of Hawsfold’s chair at the Harker wedding stood empty right through it, with a card on it, and that Master Harker kept looking at it. At noon, at the Ice Chapel, Parson Doody marries Thessaly Harker to Alderman Hamnet Gosling for a shilling, sober, in front of half the Fair. The bride says her part in a clear dry voice as if she were reading a bill. The alderman says his as if someone had died. Master Harker kisses his daughter, and shakes his son-in-law’s hand, and smiles all through the wedding-breakfast at the Frozen Lamb, where Bel Ashby serves it herself and does not look at anyone.

**Wait › An hour**

Two of Harker’s wharf men find you. ‘The Squire of Hawsfold,’ says one, touching his cap. ‘Master Harker’s compliments, and he’ll see you at the Landing.’ They do not go away.

**By the Spotted Calf's door, to Bankside**

Bankside: inns and cookshops, the Watermen’s Hall, the long wall of the Clink.

**By the lane to Hay Stairs, to the head of Hay Stairs**

The head of Hay Stairs, on the Bankside shore, with the bondhouse on Hay Wharf beside it, and below, the ice. The bailiffs, a pair of bailiffs in brown with a writ, stamping in the snow at the head of Hay Stairs, are here.

**By Hay Stairs, to the foot of Hay Stairs, on the ice**

The foot of Hay Stairs, on the Bankside shore of the ice. Underfoot the ice has a skin of water, and the planks squelch, and the boards of the booths are dark with wet where they stand in it.

**By the ice, to Freezeland Street, the lower end**

The lower end of Freezeland Street: the clanking press, the Frozen Lamb, the ribboned booth of the Ice Chapel. Mr. Ambrose Quarle, a natural philosopher in a scorched coat, with a tub, is here.

**By Freezeland Street, to Freezeland Street, by the Glove Post**

Beside the stocks stands a pillory nobody is in, with snow on the board where the head goes, and a placard hung on it ready, blank, for whatever the Court writes there. At the foot of the Glove Post stand the Court’s stocks, empty. Here are Ursel Cobb, a liar by trade, in a man’s caped coat; and Sir Lionel Gant, a gentleman in a waistcoat that was magnificent ten years ago, living on the ice. The planks of Freezeland Street are standing in a skin of water. The great glove on the mast has thawed limp and hangs down, fingers first, dripping on the crier’s barrel. Today’s bill on the Glove Post, wet already: THIRD DAY. — The WEDDING at NOON. — The LANDING at TWO, before the REEVE: every hand shaken on the ice to be CRIED, and LANDED, or let go. — OFF THE ICE at NINE, every soul. — God keep the Liberty.

**Wait › An hour**

On a board at the Reeve’s elbow the Court’s clerk has chalked the Landing’s list, and crosses each case off as it is cried: the heretic Callow, and his hand; Sir L. Gant, debtor; the Hawsfold flock, to J. Harker; J. Harker’s hired, twenty; and then ALL OTHER HANDS. Then, in bigger letters: OFF THE ICE AT NINE. The Landing. The whole Fair has come to the Glove Post and stands packed on the ice in a great ring, quiet for once, breathing. Justice Broom sits at the foot of the mast on the Court’s chair, set on a herring barrel, with her trumpet; the waterman-clerk at her side with the Court’s book; and at her other elbow, on a stool, as the city’s witness, Alderman Gosling, in his chain. Master Harker stands at the front with Kemble and his book. Above them all the great frozen glove. The clerk calls Magister Edrin Callow, on the charge of unswearing. Justice Broom turns her trumpet toward the empty stocks, as if they might say something. ‘Discharged,’ she says. ‘By the Flow.’ Nobody laughs.

**Wait › An hour**

The clerk calls Sir Lionel Gant, of nowhere in particular. Master Harker asks the Court that when the ice goes Sir Lionel be cried off it at Hay Stairs, into the hands of the bailiffs who wait there with his writ, for thirty coin. Sir Lionel stands before the Reeve with his hat in his hand and says, very quietly, ‘It was three.’

**Wait › An hour**

Under your feet, all over Pellam, the ice has begun to talk: a long creak like a ship’s timbers, a crack like a gun a long way off, and then a groan that goes on and on, up the whole length of the river. On the ice everybody stops and listens, and laughs, too loudly, and goes on. The clerk calls a hand: Josias Harker, and the Squire of Hawsfold, two hundred fell ewes at Hawsfold under Swale Fell, delivery at the shearing. Master Harker turns to you, smiling, with his palm already open. You shake again, before the Reeve, and the clerk cries it: ‘Landed!’ and writes it in the Court’s book; and now, the Fair knows and you know, it is good from here to the sea. Master Harker smiles, and has Kemble count out what remains of the price.

**Wait › An hour**

The clerk calls Master Harker’s hired, twenty hands, a year’s service on a farm in the hills. They stand in a line before the Reeve in their blankets, with their tokens in their hats: crooks, pails, whips, mops. Tansy Hobb is at the end, with her milk-pail on its string. Harker stands beside them with his palm open, kindly, and one by one they come to shake again before the Reeve.

**Wait › An hour**

Freezeland Street at lamp-lighting: lanterns on every oar, the ice gone gold in them, and the Fair louder now it cannot be seen so well. The clerk cries it, hand by hand: ‘Landed! Landed! Landed!’ twenty times, and writes it in the Court’s book, and Master Harker thanks the Reeve, and Kemble walks his hired back off the ice to the bondhouse, to wait for Ossett’s agent and the thaw.

**Wait › An hour**

Beside the stocks stands a pillory nobody is in, with snow on the board where the head goes, and a placard hung on it ready, blank, for whatever the Court writes there. Justice Broom gets down off her barrel. ‘The ice is landed,’ she says, through her trumpet, to the whole Fair. ‘What’s landed is landed, and what’s not goes out with the river. God save the ice.’ The crowd breaks up. Up and down Freezeland Street, already, they are taking the sailcloth off the booths.

**Wait › An hour**

At Hay Stairs, as the watermen hurry the last of the Fair off the ice, the bailiffs are waiting with their writ, and Sir Lionel Gant walks up the stairs into their hands with his hat on, very straight, saying ‘It was three’ to the bailiff on his left. The booths of Freezeland Street are coming down: sailcloth folded, oars carried up Swan Stairs on men’s shoulders, the ox’s bones dragged off on a sledge. The ice is grey under them, and wet. The crier, for the last time, on his barrel at the Glove Post: ‘O yes! O yes! O yes! Off the ice! Off the ice, every soul, the Liberty is ended! God save the ice!’ And the watermen take the great glove down off the mast, and fold it like a sail. Two watermen take you by the arms. ‘Off,’ says one. ‘Off, off, off, it’s going,’ and walks you to the nearest stairs at a run, with the ice cracking behind you like a whip. The top of Swan Stairs, and the Fair below. A bailiff, a man in brown with a copy of the writ, at the head of Swan Stairs, is here.

**Wait › An hour**

Swan Stairs go down twelve steps into black water running fast, full of broken ice. The watermen are already putting their boats in. The stairs go down into running water now. From down the Reach, all at once, a sound like a city falling down: the ice going out. It comes up the river from the sea toward the bridge in the dark, crack after crack, louder, the whole frozen Lisk breaking into plates and rearing up against the starlings and going under, and the black water coming up through it steaming, and people on both banks with torches shouting at it, or crying, or cheering. By midnight there is a river under Pellam Bridge, running, full of ice, where Freezeland Street was.

**Wait › An hour**

Of the bear: Hester Mabb sat all day at the bear-stake with the broken chain in her lap, and went up the North Road this morning alone. Of the wedding: Alderman Gosling and his bride went home to Fleece Row after the Landing, and at midnight, they say, when the ice went, the alderman was seen alone on the bridge in his chain, looking down at it. Of the debtor: Sir Lionel Gant is in the Fleece Row compter for thirty coin, saying to anyone who visits him that it was three.

**Wait › An hour**

In the morning there is a river, and a bargain landed in the Court’s book, good from here to the sea: two hundred Swale ewes at Hawsfold under Swale Fell, to Josias Harker, at the shearing. They exist. You bought them off Abel Haws for forty coin and sold them to Harker for sixty, and every hand that was shaken in it will be kept, which is more than can be said for any other bargain of the Fair. Harker will get his ewes, and Abel his forty, and you twenty and a story. It is the most honest swindle anyone in Pellam has ever heard of, and nobody believes it.
