# Ursel at night: Swale Head, the Bench, and Tansy Hobb

Seed `lisk`, a thief (cutpurse); fixture `ursel-night`; 31 steps; ended at watch 73; 300 experience in all. Written by `bun scripts/liberty-of-the-ice-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The North Road comes down off the fells between walls of snow higher than your head, trodden to a trench by a month of feet. Below, in a white bowl of hills, Pellam: smoke going straight up from a thousand chimneys, a grey cathedral tower with a gilded pair of scales on its spire, a long bridge on many arches, and under the bridge, where the river ought to be, a street. A street of tents and smoke and pennants and people, on the river, a mile long. Faintly, all the way up here, a sound like a market and a wedding at once. The North Road goes up out of Pellam into the snow.

**By the North Road, to the North Bar**

You set out from the North Road, above Pellam early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. THE FIRST DAY OF THE FAIR. A hard blue frost, the sky like a glazed dish, not a cloud from the fells to the downs; the smoke of a thousand chimneys going straight up and flattening at a height, as if against a ceiling. Your breath freezes in your scarf. Sound carries: you can hear a hammer on the far side of the river, and a girl laughing on the bridge, and under everything, from the ice, the noise of the Fair, like a hive. The North Bar: a gate-tower with its gates open and snowed into the open position, and a toll-house nobody is in. On the gate somebody has nailed a broadside, half torn: THE GREAT FROST — A FAIR UPON THE ICE — ALL WELCOME — NO LAW BUT THE GLOVE. Beyond it the city goes down to the river in steep white streets. The North Road goes up out of Pellam into the snow.

**By Northgate Street, to the Cheaping**

The Cheaping, Pellam’s wool market: a great square with a market-cross and a covered hall on stone legs where the fleeces are sold in summer, empty now, snowed knee-deep, with paths trodden across it in every direction to the Exchange, to the Scale Church, to Fleece Row, and above all down Swan Lane to the river. Nobody is buying wool here. Everybody is going to the ice.

**By Swan Lane, to Swan Lane**

Swan Lane runs down steep between warehouses to the river and stops at the top of Swan Stairs, the watermen’s stairs, where in any other winter you would hail a boat. Below, at the foot of the stairs, there is no water: there is the Fair. Two watermen sit on the top of Swan Stairs with a plank across it between them, and a tin cup. ‘A penny a stair,’ says one of them. ‘Shake on it.’ A bailiff, a man in brown with a copy of the writ, at the head of Swan Stairs, is here.

**Shake on a penny a stair with the watermen**

You put out your hand, and the waterman spits on his and shakes it, very hard, and lifts the plank. ‘Twelve stairs,’ he says. ‘Twelve pennies. Pay at the bottom or pay at the Court, it’s all one to us.’ He grins. ‘Mind the ninth. It’s slippy.’ The watermen’s plank is lifted aside for you; they nod as you go by.

**By Swan Stairs, to the foot of Swan Stairs, on the ice**

The foot of Swan Stairs, on the ice. The river is white and hard from bank to bank, scarred grey with sledge-runners and skates, and up it, from the bridge to as far down as you can see, runs a street of booths made of oars and sailcloth, with smoke going straight up from every one of them into a sky the colour of pewter. There is a noise like a market and a wedding at once. A boy goes past on bones strapped to his boots, very fast, shrieking. The watermen’s plank is lifted aside for you; they nod as you go by.

**By the ice, to Freezeland Street, by the Glove Post**

Somebody knocks into you in the crush by the Glove Post, a woman in a caped coat, who says ‘Beg pardon, my love,’ and goes on, and when you put your hand to your purse it is not there. You turn round. She has stopped ten feet away and is holding it up by its strings for you to see, and laughing. ‘Never shook on it,’ she calls. ‘Did I? Did you?’ A few people nearby laugh too. Nobody calls the watch. There is no watch. A bill is pasted on the Glove Post, new this morning, the ink smudged with frost: THE GREAT FROST FAIR, FIRST DAY. — HIRING at the RING, TEN till NOON, by Master J. HARKER, for a farm in the hills. — The LIBERTY CRIED at NOON. — The COURT of the ICE sits at FOUR. — The LIARS’ BENCH at the FROZEN LAMB from SEVEN. — A BEAR. AN OX. FROST-CREAM. — NO LAW BUT THE GLOVE. Beside the stocks stands a pillory nobody is in, with snow on the board where the head goes, and a placard hung on it ready, blank, for whatever the Court writes there. At the top of Freezeland Street a ship’s mast has been stepped in the ice and guyed with ropes, and nailed to the head of it, stiff with frost, is a leather glove as big as a sail-bag: the sign of the Liberty. A board at the foot of the mast says in red: SAY WHAT YOU WILL. KEEP WHAT YOU SHAKE ON. C. BROOM, REEVE. Freezeland Street, at its top end, by the Glove Post: the thick of the Fair. Booths of sailcloth on oars, gin and gingerbread, hot codlings, a skittle alley where men bowl a frozen sheep’s head, a swing-boat on a frame, a man selling slices of the ox, a man selling knowledge of the future, a man selling nothing anyone can see with great conviction. Everyone is shouting the price of something. Nobody is saying what anything is worth. In the lee of the Glove Post, a lean-to of sailcloth on two oars, with a brazier, a folding chair, a bottle and a gentleman in it. At the foot of the Glove Post, on an upturned keg, a thin old man in a green-black gown is preaching to a crowd that laughs and shouts back. ‘Look under your feet!’ he cries. ‘This is a river! Yesterday it ran; tomorrow it will run; today you have built a street on it and called it a country and made a law for it. Nothing stays, my friends. Not this ice, not that glove, not the man who shook hands with you on Monday. He is gone! He went out with the river! Why should you pay his debts?’ A waterman throws a snowball at him. He catches it, and eats it. At the foot of the Glove Post stand the Court’s stocks, empty. Here are Ursel Cobb, a woman in a man’s caped coat, with a laugh like a door; Sir Lionel Gant, a gentleman in a waistcoat that was magnificent ten years ago, living on the ice; and Magister Edrin Callow, a thin old preacher in a black gown gone green, coughing, with a crowd.

**Lift your purse back off her while she is laughing · likely**

**Ask Ursel what her proposition is**

She sits you down on an upturned keg out of the wind and talks low and fast. ‘Josias Harker. Wool-factor, Fleece Row, churchwarden, very fond of God. With the roads shut he’s buying fleeces that aren’t shorn yet off sheep he can’t see, cheap, for when the thaw comes and the price goes up. Every factor in Pellam is at it.’ She taps your knee. ‘So we sell him a flock. Two hundred fell ewes, up at Hawsfold, snowed in. Nobody can go and look till the thaw, and when the thaw comes, we’re gone.’ She sits back. ‘You’re the Squire of Hawsfold. I’m your man of business. Sixty coin, forty to you. We take his money on the ice and we’re over the bridge before the Landing, and the bargain goes out with the river.’ She looks at you. ‘Well?’

**Ask Ursel Cobb to join the company**

‘Partners,’ says Ursel Cobb, and does not put out her hand. ‘I don’t shake. Nothing personal. I’ve never shaken on anything in my life, and I’m not starting on the ice.’

**By the way to the Hiring Ring, to the Hiring Ring**

The Hiring Ring is empty: a rope on stakes round a trodden circle of ice, and in the middle a mop-head somebody dropped, frozen to the ice.

**Wait › A while**

The Hiring Ring: a rope on stakes round a trodden circle of ice, and inside it twenty or thirty young people from the north country, standing very still in the cold with a token in their hats for what they can do: a tuft of wool for a shepherd, a whipcord for a carter, a mop-head, a twist of straw. Their breath goes up. Master Harker walks round inside the rope in his fur collar with his clerk behind him, and stops in front of one, and talks a little, kindly, and puts out not his hand but his open palm, and waits; and the one in front of him shakes it, and Kemble writes. A girl with a milk-pail in her hat, who cannot be more than fifteen, has just shaken. Time passes.

**Talk to Tansy Hobb**

Tansy Hobb looks at you the way she must look at a strange cow: carefully, and from a little way off. ‘Are you hired too?’

**Ask Tansy Hobb where she is going**

‘A farm in the hills.’ She says it like a charm. ‘A year’s place, and good food, and wages at the year’s end, and I can send them home.’ She swallows. ‘Mam said not to go to Harker. Mam said nobody comes back from Harker’s. But Mam said that about the town, too, and I came back from the town.’ She looks at her hands. ‘I shook. It’s done now. You can’t unshake.’

The Hiring Ring is empty: a rope on stakes round a trodden circle of ice, and in the middle a mop-head somebody dropped, frozen to the ice. A shepherd has come in over the North Bar out of the snow, with a crook and a black-and-white dog, asking the first person he meets in the Cheaping, very loudly, where a man might find a buyer for two hundred fell ewes, from Hawsfold, under Swale Fell. **By the way to the Hiring Ring, to Freezeland Street, by the Glove Post**

A bill is pasted on the Glove Post, new this morning, the ink smudged with frost: THE GREAT FROST FAIR, FIRST DAY. At the foot of the Glove Post Magister Callow sits in the stocks with his ankles through the holes, a blanket round him and a brazier at his feet, coughing, and preaching the Flow to whoever stops. Here are Sir Lionel Gant, a gentleman in a waistcoat that was magnificent ten years ago, living on the ice; and Magister Edrin Callow, a preacher of the Flow, charged with unswearing. Freezeland Street at lamp-lighting: lanterns on every oar, the ice gone gold in them, and the Fair louder now it cannot be seen so well.

**By Freezeland Street, to Freezeland Street, the lower end**

The lower end of Freezeland Street, where the booths are older and the jokes worse. A printing booth with a press in it, clanking. A gin-tent with a painted sign of a lamb on skates. A booth hung with ribbons, with a board that says MARRIAGES. Beyond the last of them the street gives out into open ice, and the ox roast’s smoke, and further down, the grey of the Reach.

**By the Frozen Lamb's flap, to the Frozen Lamb**

The Frozen Lamb: a gin-tent the size of a barn, sailcloth on a frame of oars, a brazier in a tub of sand at either end and a bar made of a ship’s hatch-cover. Benches. Hot gin with nutmeg on it. At the far end, under a lantern, one bench by itself has LIARS painted on its back. The tent’s keeper is a big handsome woman in her fifties with her sleeves rolled up, who looks at everyone who comes in as if she had been expecting someone else. On the Liars’ Bench under the lantern sits Justice Broom with her ear-trumpet and a glass of hot gin, and round her a crowd, waiting. Whoever has a lie stands on the bench opposite and tells it, and the crowd shouts, and Broom listens with her eyes shut. Alderman Gosling sits at the end of the Frozen Lamb’s bar over a hot gin he does not drink, and does not look at Bel Ashby, so carefully that everyone else in the tent does. Here are Justice Clemency Broom, the Reeve of the Ice, a waterman’s widow of seventy with an ear-trumpet; Bel Ashby, the keeper of the Frozen Lamb; Silas Kemble, a clerk, thin, with a red nose and a quill behind his ear; and Alderman Hamnet Gosling, an alderman of Pellam, stout and anxious, with ink on his cuffs.

**Ask Ursel, late, why she has never shaken on anything**

She is quiet so long you think she will not answer. ‘Swale Head,’ she says. ‘Up under the fell, where the becks start. We had a farm, forty acres of stones. The year I was eleven the clip failed, and a Pellam factor came up the dale with a note, against next year’s wool, and my father shook on it in our own kitchen, with the factor’s glove on the table, and I watched him do it.’ She turns her cup round. ‘Next year’s clip failed too. The note was good in any court of the country. They landed the farm, and the stock, and the bed. My mother walked us down to Pellam with Dickon on her hip.’ She drinks. ‘I don’t know the factor’s name. I never asked. If I knew it I’d have to do something.’ A pause. ‘So I don’t shake. A word’s free. A hand’s what they take your bed with.’ Sam and Ursel Cobb each gain 100 experience: heard why Ursel Cobb never shakes.

**Ask Ursel where she learned to lie**

‘On the Liars’ Bench.’ She laughs, properly, for the first time that evening. ‘Not this one. The Bench is older than the Frosts; when there’s no ice it’s a plank at the back of the Calf, and the Reeve’s whoever’s oldest. I won it at fifteen, with a story about a man who sold the bridge to itself, and old Broom’s mother gave me the prize, a penny with a hole in it.’ She shows you: it is round her neck on a string, worn thin. ‘The truth’s very dear, for poor folk. So we tell it on a bench, for fun, with everyone knowing it’s lies. That’s where you can say anything.’ She looks at the fire. ‘I’ve told the truth on that bench more times than anywhere else in my life.’

**Tell Ursel that one of Harker's hired, Tansy Hobb, is a dairymaid from Swale Head**

‘Hobb.’ She closes her eyes. ‘The Hobbs had the low farm, by the wash-dub, where the beck goes under the road. Mary Hobb gave my mother a cheese the morning we walked down. She’ll be this Tansy’s grandmother.’ She opens them. ‘Fifteen, you said.’ She stands up. ‘Right,’ says Ursel Cobb. ‘Right. Then it’s two of us I want out of that barn.’
