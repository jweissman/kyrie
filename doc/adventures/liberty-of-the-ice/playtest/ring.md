# The Ring: hired, and out down the Reach on the first night

Seed `lisk`, a warrior (laborer); fixture `ring`; 99 steps; ended at watch 420, at the ending "Not Landed"; 1200 experience in all. Written by `bun scripts/liberty-of-the-ice-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The North Road comes down off the fells between walls of snow higher than your head, trodden to a trench by a month of feet. Below, in a white bowl of hills, Pellam: smoke going straight up from a thousand chimneys, a grey cathedral tower with a gilded pair of scales on its spire, a long bridge on many arches, and under the bridge, where the river ought to be, a street. A street of tents and smoke and pennants and people, on the river, a mile long. Faintly, all the way up here, a sound like a market and a wedding at once. The North Road goes up out of Pellam into the snow.

**By the North Road, to the North Bar**

You set out from the North Road, above Pellam early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. THE FIRST DAY OF THE FAIR. A hard blue frost, the sky like a glazed dish, not a cloud from the fells to the downs, and the sun on the snow so bright it hurts to look at. Your breath freezes in your scarf. Sound carries: you can hear a hammer on the far side of the river, and a girl laughing on the bridge, and under everything, from the ice, the noise of the Fair, like a hive. The North Bar: a gate-tower with its gates open and snowed into the open position, and a toll-house nobody is in. On the gate somebody has nailed a broadside, half torn: THE GREAT FROST — A FAIR UPON THE ICE — ALL WELCOME — NO LAW BUT THE GLOVE. Beyond it the city goes down to the river in steep white streets. The North Road goes up out of Pellam into the snow.

**By Northgate Street, to the Cheaping**

The Cheaping, Pellam’s wool market: a great square with a market-cross and a covered hall on stone legs where the fleeces are sold in summer, empty now, snowed knee-deep, with paths trodden across it in every direction to the Exchange, to the Scale Church, to Fleece Row, and above all down Swan Lane to the river. Nobody is buying wool here. Everybody is going to the ice.

**By Swan Lane, to Swan Lane**

Swan Lane runs down steep between warehouses to the river and stops at the top of Swan Stairs, the watermen’s stairs, where in any other winter you would hail a boat. Below, at the foot of the stairs, there is no water: there is the Fair. Two watermen sit on the top of Swan Stairs with a plank across it between them, and a tin cup. ‘A penny a stair,’ says one of them. ‘Shake on it.’ A bailiff, a man in brown with a copy of the writ, at the head of Swan Stairs, is here.

**Step over the plank and go down without paying · an even chance**

You step over the plank and the bigger waterman stands up and puts you back on the top step with one hand, not roughly. ‘A penny a stair,’ he says. ‘Or the bridge, if you know the way, which you don’t.’ Sam fails the brawn check (d20 9 + strength 1 + brawn 2 = 12 against 13).

**Shake on a penny a stair with the watermen**

You put out your hand, and the waterman spits on his and shakes it, very hard, and lifts the plank. ‘Twelve stairs,’ he says. ‘Twelve pennies. Pay at the bottom or pay at the Court, it’s all one to us.’ He grins. ‘Mind the ninth. It’s slippy.’ The watermen’s plank is lifted aside for you; they nod as you go by.

**By Swan Stairs, to the foot of Swan Stairs, on the ice**

The foot of Swan Stairs, on the ice. The river is white and hard from bank to bank, scarred grey with sledge-runners and skates, and up it, from the bridge to as far down as you can see, runs a street of booths made of oars and sailcloth, with smoke going straight up from every one of them into the cold air. There is a noise like a market and a wedding at once. A boy goes past on bones strapped to his boots, very fast, shrieking. The watermen’s plank is lifted aside for you; they nod as you go by.

**By the ice, to Freezeland Street, by the Glove Post**

Somebody knocks into you in the crush by the Glove Post, a woman in a caped coat, who says ‘Beg pardon, my love,’ and goes on, and when you put your hand to your purse it is not there. You turn round. She has stopped ten feet away and is holding it up by its strings for you to see, and laughing. ‘Never shook on it,’ she calls. ‘Did I? Did you?’ A few people nearby laugh too. Nobody calls the watch. There is no watch. A bill is pasted on the Glove Post, new this morning, the ink smudged with frost: THE GREAT FROST FAIR, FIRST DAY. — HIRING at the RING, TEN till NOON, by Master J. HARKER, for a farm in the hills. — The LIBERTY CRIED at NOON. — The COURT of the ICE sits at FOUR. — The LIARS’ BENCH at the FROZEN LAMB from SEVEN. — A BEAR. AN OX. FROST-CREAM. — NO LAW BUT THE GLOVE. Beside the stocks stands a pillory nobody is in, with snow on the board where the head goes, and a placard hung on it ready, blank, for whatever the Court writes there. At the top of Freezeland Street a ship’s mast has been stepped in the ice and guyed with ropes, and nailed to the head of it, stiff with frost, is a leather glove as big as a sail-bag: the sign of the Liberty. A board at the foot of the mast says in red: SAY WHAT YOU WILL. KEEP WHAT YOU SHAKE ON. C. BROOM, REEVE. Freezeland Street, at its top end, by the Glove Post: the thick of the Fair. Booths of sailcloth on oars, gin and gingerbread, hot codlings, a skittle alley where men bowl a frozen sheep’s head, a swing-boat on a frame, a man selling slices of the ox, a man selling knowledge of the future, a man selling nothing anyone can see with great conviction. Everyone is shouting the price of something. Nobody is saying what anything is worth. In the lee of the Glove Post, a lean-to of sailcloth on two oars, with a brazier, a folding chair, a bottle and a gentleman in it. At the foot of the Glove Post, on an upturned keg, a thin old man in a green-black gown is preaching to a crowd that laughs and shouts back. ‘Look under your feet!’ he cries. ‘This is a river! Yesterday it ran; tomorrow it will run; today you have built a street on it and called it a country and made a law for it. Nothing stays, my friends. Not this ice, not that glove, not the man who shook hands with you on Monday. He is gone! He went out with the river! Why should you pay his debts?’ A waterman throws a snowball at him. He catches it, and eats it. At the foot of the Glove Post stand the Court’s stocks, empty. Here are Ursel Cobb, a woman in a man’s caped coat, with a laugh like a door; Sir Lionel Gant, a gentleman in a waistcoat that was magnificent ten years ago, living on the ice; and Magister Edrin Callow, a thin old preacher in a black gown gone green, coughing, with a crowd.

**Wait › A while**

The Hiring Ring: a rope on stakes round a trodden circle of ice, and inside it twenty or thirty young people from the north country, standing very still in the cold with a token in their hats for what they can do: a tuft of wool for a shepherd, a whipcord for a carter, a mop-head, a twist of straw. Their breath goes up. Master Harker walks round inside the rope in his fur collar with his clerk behind him, and stops in front of one, and talks a little, kindly, and puts out not his hand but his open palm, and waits; and the one in front of him shakes it, and Kemble writes. A girl with a milk-pail in her hat, who cannot be more than fifteen, has just shaken. Time passes.

**Put a tuft of straw in your hat and stand in the Hiring Ring**

You stand in the cold with the others with a twist of straw in your hat, and after a while Master Harker comes round to you in his fur collar and looks at you as a man looks at a horse at a price he likes. ‘A strong back,’ he says kindly. ‘And an honest face. A year’s place on a farm in the hills: good food, a roof, wages at the year’s end.’ He holds out his palm, open, and waits. You shake it. His hand is soft and very warm. ‘Kemble,’ he says, without looking round, and Kemble writes you down.

The bondhouse floor: a long dim barn of a place smelling of wool and tallow, fleeces stacked to the beams down one side, and down the other, on straw, Harker’s hired, sitting close together for the warmth with blankets round them. Here are Harker’s hired, a line of young people from the north country, hired at the Ring; and Tansy Hobb, a dairymaid from Swale Head, fifteen, with a milk-pail on a string.

**Read Kemble's bond book (Sam)**

**Talk to Tansy Hobb**

Tansy Hobb looks at you the way she must look at a strange cow: carefully, and from a little way off. ‘Are you hired too?’

**Tell Tansy Hobb where the farm in the hills is**

She does not cry. She puts her milk-pail down on the straw, carefully, as if it might spill. ‘Lead,’ she says. ‘Mam said.’ Round her the others have gone quiet, listening; somebody says ‘Gallowmoor’ in a whisper, and it goes down the straw like a draught. A big lad with a whip in his hat stands up. ‘Then we’ll not go,’ he says. ‘We’ll not.’ Tansy looks at the barred river door, and then at you. ‘We shook,’ she says. ‘We all shook.’ Sam gains 300 experience: went into the bondhouse as one of the hired.

**Into the bondhouse loft**

The loft: fleeces, cobwebs, a little grey daylight through the tiles, and names. The beams are covered in names, cut with knives and nails and pins, year after year: initials and hearts and the names of villages, and crooks and pails scratched beside them.

**Search here**

You search the bondhouse loft with care, a little at a time. On a beam of the bondhouse loft, cut deep with a nail, among years of names: D. COBB SWALE HEAD. And under it, newer, cut by the same hand, deeper: GALLOWMOOR LEAD. THEY DONT COME BACK. I DID.
