# Five Coin a Head

Seed `lisk`, the bard of the title page; fixture `sold`; 92 steps; ended at watch 420, at the ending "Five Coin a Head"; 0 experience in all. Written by `bun scripts/liberty-of-the-ice-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The North Road comes down off the fells between walls of snow higher than your head, trodden to a trench by a month of feet. Below, in a white bowl of hills, Pellam: smoke going straight up from a thousand chimneys, a grey cathedral tower with a gilded pair of scales on its spire, a long bridge on many arches, and under the bridge, where the river ought to be, a street. A street of tents and smoke and pennants and people, on the river, a mile long. Faintly, all the way up here, a sound like a market and a wedding at once. The North Road goes up out of Pellam into the snow.

**By the North Road, to the North Bar**

You set out from the North Road, above Pellam early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. The North Bar: a gate-tower with its gates open and snowed into the open position, and a toll-house nobody is in. On the gate somebody has nailed a broadside, half torn: THE GREAT FROST — A FAIR UPON THE ICE — ALL WELCOME — NO LAW BUT THE GLOVE. Beyond it the city goes down to the river in steep white streets. The North Road goes up out of Pellam into the snow.

**By Northgate Street, to the Cheaping**

The Cheaping, Pellam’s wool market: a great square with a market-cross and a covered hall on stone legs where the fleeces are sold in summer, empty now, snowed knee-deep, with paths trodden across it in every direction to the Exchange, to Saint Evenhand’s, to Fleece Row, and above all down Swan Lane to the river. Nobody is buying wool here. Everybody is going to the ice.

**By Swan Lane, to Swan Lane**

Swan Lane runs down steep between warehouses to the river and stops at the top of Swan Stairs, the watermen’s stairs, where in any other winter you would hail a boat. Below, at the foot of the stairs, there is no water: there is the Fair. Two watermen sit on the top of Swan Stairs with a plank across it between them, and a tin cup. ‘A penny a stair,’ says one of them. ‘Shake on it.’ A bailiff, a man in brown with a copy of the writ, at the head of Swan Stairs, is here.

**Shake on a penny a stair with the watermen**

You put out your hand, and the waterman spits on his and shakes it, very hard, and lifts the plank. ‘Twelve stairs,’ he says. ‘Twelve pennies. Pay at the bottom or pay at the Court, it’s all one to us.’ He grins. ‘Mind the ninth. It’s slippy.’ The watermen’s plank is lifted aside for you; they nod as you go by.

**By Swan Stairs, to the foot of Swan Stairs, on the ice**

The foot of Swan Stairs, on the ice. The river is white and hard from bank to bank, scarred grey with sledge-runners and skates, and up it, from the bridge to as far down as you can see, runs a street of booths made of oars and sailcloth, with smoke going straight up from every one of them into a sky the colour of pewter. There is a noise like a market and a wedding at once. A boy goes past on bones strapped to his boots, very fast, shrieking. The watermen’s plank is lifted aside for you; they nod as you go by.

**By the ice, to Freezeland Street, by the Glove Post**

Somebody knocks into you in the crush by the Glove Post, a woman in a caped coat, who says ‘Beg pardon, my love,’ and goes on, and when you put your hand to your purse it is not there. You turn round. She has stopped ten feet away and is holding it up by its strings for you to see, and laughing. ‘Never shook on it,’ she calls. ‘Did I? Did you?’ A few people nearby laugh too. Nobody calls the watch. There is no watch. At the top of Freezeland Street a ship’s mast has been stepped in the ice and guyed with ropes, and nailed to the head of it, stiff with frost, is a leather glove as big as a sail-bag: the sign of the Liberty. A board at the foot of the mast says in red: SAY WHAT YOU WILL. KEEP WHAT YOU SHAKE ON. C. BROOM, REEVE. Freezeland Street, at its top end, by the Glove Post: the thick of the Fair. Booths of sailcloth on oars, gin and gingerbread, hot codlings, a skittle alley where men bowl a frozen sheep’s head, a swing-boat on a frame, a man selling slices of the ox, a man selling knowledge of the future, a man selling nothing anyone can see with great conviction. Everyone is shouting the price of something. Nobody is saying what anything is worth. In the lee of the Glove Post, a lean-to of sailcloth on two oars, with a brazier, a folding chair, a bottle and a gentleman in it. At the foot of the Glove Post, on an upturned keg, a thin old man in a green-black gown is preaching to a crowd that laughs and shouts back. ‘Look under your feet!’ he cries. ‘This is a river! Yesterday it ran; tomorrow it will run; today you have built a street on it and called it a country and made a law for it. Nothing stays, my friends. Not this ice, not that glove, not the man who shook hands with you on Monday. He is gone! He went out with the river! Why should you pay his debts?’ A waterman throws a snowball at him. He catches it, and eats it. At the foot of the Glove Post stand the Court’s stocks, empty. Here are Ursel Cobb, a woman in a man’s caped coat, with a laugh like a door; Sir Lionel Gant, a gentleman in a waistcoat that was magnificent ten years ago, living on the ice; and Magister Edrin Callow, a thin old preacher in a black gown gone green, coughing, with a crowd.

**Ask her for your purse back**

‘Course you can have it.’ She tosses it to you. ‘Lesson’s free.’ She watches you count it. ‘All there. I’m not a thief, I’m a teacher. On the ice a word’s free and a hand’s bound, and I never gave you my hand, and you never asked for it.’ She grins. ‘Ursel Cobb. And you’ve a face nobody would ever suspect of anything, which is worth more than what was in there.’

**Wait › An hour**

The crier is on his barrel, crying the Liberty again: say what you will; keep what you shake on. A shepherd has come in over the North Bar out of the snow, with a crook and a black-and-white dog, asking the first person he meets in the Cheaping, very loudly, where a man might find a buyer for two hundred fell ewes, from Hawsfold, under Swale Fell. Noon. The crier climbs onto a barrel at the foot of the Glove Post and rings his bell until the whole top of Freezeland Street is looking at him, and cries it: ‘O yes, O yes, O yes! The river Lisk being frozen from bank to bank, and lying in no parish and under no lord, the ice is a Liberty, by the custom of the Watermen of Pellam, until it goes! Say what you will on it, and no man shall have the law of you for a word! Keep what you shake on, and the Reeve shall have the law of you for a hand! God save the ice!’ Everyone cheers, and goes back to lying to each other. At noon the hiring ends. Kemble walks Harker’s hired off the ice in a line, two by two, across to Hay Stairs and up onto the wharf and in at the door of the long blind warehouse with J. HARKER. WOOL painted on its gable, to be kept there, warm and fed, he tells anyone who asks, until the Landing.

Freezeland Street at lamp-lighting: lanterns on every oar, the ice gone gold in them, and the Fair louder now it cannot be seen so well. An hour passes.

Nobody would be Callow’s hand. Freezeland Street by night: half the booths dark and laced up, the other half louder than ever. Under the lantern at the Glove Post the frost on the great glove glitters. An hour passes.

**Wait › An hour**

Freezeland Street, at its top end, by the Glove Post: the thick of the Fair. Booths of sailcloth on oars, gin and gingerbread, hot codlings, a skittle alley where men bowl a frozen sheep’s head, a swing-boat on a frame, a man selling slices of the ox, a man selling knowledge of the future, a man selling nothing anyone can see with great conviction. All over the Fair this morning: another hat on the stake at the Black Reach. Jem Rudd’s, the carter’s lad from Garthwaite, hired yesterday at the Ring; he was in Kemble’s line at noon and not in the bondhouse at dark. The river has taken him, everyone says, and touches the ice with a knuckle.

**By Freezeland Street, to Freezeland Street, the lower end**

The lower end of Freezeland Street, where the booths are older and the jokes worse. A printing booth with a press in it, clanking. A gin-tent with a painted sign of a lamb on skates. A booth hung with ribbons, with a board that says MARRIAGES. Beyond the last of them the street gives out into open ice, and the ox roast’s smoke, and further down, the grey of the Reach.

**By the way to the ox roast, to the ox roast**

The ox roast: a whole ox on an iron spit over a fire of sea-coal laid on a hearth of bricks, so that the ice will not melt under it, which it is doing all the same, slowly; the hearth has sunk a hand’s breadth into a pool of its own making and the Butchers’ Company’s men stand round it arguing. The smell is extraordinary. Slices are a penny, on bread. Absolute is at the ox. He has pulled the spit down and is lying on the sunken hearth in the steam with the ox between his paws, eating it, and making a sound like a cart going over cobbles. The Butchers’ Company’s men stand in a ring well back with poles and cleavers, saying what they will do. Hester Mabb stands nearer than any of them, holding out her hand and talking to him, and he is not listening. Hester Mabb, a bear-ward, small and weathered, in a coat of patches, is here.

**By the ice past the last booth, to the head of the Reach, past the last booth**

Past the last booth the Fair stops as if at a wall, and there is only ice: grey, wide, and very quiet, with the river showing black through it in places like a vein. A line of sledge-marks goes away down the middle of the Reach, and people keep to it. Far down, a hulk sits locked in the ice at an angle, with her masts gone.

**By the grey ice of the Reach, to the Black Reach**

On a stake of the rope round the black hole a hat has been hung, a carter’s felt hat with a whipcord round the crown, very neatly, as if on a peg. Under it, in the snow, someone has put a penny. The Black Reach: the deep channel, where the Lisk still runs under the ice, and the ice over it is grey, and creaks when you stand still. Off to one side, near the bank, a ring of stakes with a rope round them marks a hole that has never frozen over, black water breathing a little steam. Someone has put a penny on one of the stakes. The sledge marks run on south over hard ice.

**By the ice out to the hulk, to the deck of the Patience**

The deck of the Patience: a collier-brig frozen in on her way up to the city a month ago and left, her masts struck, her deck white, listing to starboard in the ice. A hatch, closed. Her name is still on her stern in flaking gold.

**By the Patience's hatch, to the hold of the Patience**

The hold of the Patience, sloping with her list: coal-dust, ballast stone, a brazier in a bucket of sand, the hull sweating frost. It smells of people.

**Search here**

You search the hold of the Patience with care, a little at a time. Behind the ballast, where the hull curves in, there is a breath of warmth that is not the brazier’s, and blankets, and in the blankets people.

**By the ice out to the hulk, to the Black Reach**

The Black Reach: the deep channel, where the Lisk still runs under the ice, and the ice over it is grey, and creaks when you stand still.

**By the ice past the last booth, to the ox roast**

**By the way to the ox roast, to Freezeland Street, the lower end**

Mr. Ambrose Quarle, a natural philosopher in a scorched coat, with a tub, is here. The lower end of Freezeland Street: the clanking press, the Frozen Lamb, the ribboned booth of the Ice Chapel. By the printing booth a man in a scorched coat has set up a trestle and a wooden tub, and a board: THE PERPETUAL FROST COMPANY. SHARES FIVE COIN. THE RIVER KEPT FOR EVER. A small crowd watches the tub, in which, very slowly, as they watch, a skin of ice is forming on the water.

**By Freezeland Street, to Freezeland Street, by the Glove Post**

At the top of Freezeland Street a ship’s mast has been stepped in the ice and guyed with ropes, and nailed to the head of it, stiff with frost, is a leather glove as big as a sail-bag: the sign of the Liberty. At the foot of the Glove Post Magister Callow sits in the stocks with his ankles through the holes, a blanket round him and a brazier at his feet, coughing, and preaching the Flow to whoever stops. Here are Ursel Cobb, a liar by trade, in a man’s caped coat; Sir Lionel Gant, a gentleman in a waistcoat that was magnificent ten years ago, living on the ice; and Magister Edrin Callow, a preacher of the Flow, charged with unswearing. Freezeland Street by the Glove Post, loud and smoking: gingerbread, skittles, the swing-boat, the crier’s barrel.

**By the way to the Hiring Ring, to the Hiring Ring**

The Hiring Ring: a rope on stakes round a trodden circle of ice, and inside it twenty or thirty young people from the north country, standing very still in the cold with a token in their hats for what they can do: a tuft of wool for a shepherd, a whipcord for a carter, a mop-head, a twist of straw. Their breath goes up. Master Harker walks round inside the rope in his fur collar with his clerk behind him, and stops in front of one, and talks a little, kindly, and puts out not his hand but his open palm, and waits; and the one in front of him shakes it, and Kemble writes. A girl with a milk-pail in her hat, who cannot be more than fifteen, has just shaken. Here are Master Josias Harker, a wool-factor in a black coat with a fur collar, and a churchwarden’s ring; and Silas Kemble, a clerk, thin, with a red nose and a quill behind his ear.

**Ask Kemble about the drowned**

Kemble puts his pen down for the first time. ‘Drowned.’ He says it like a sum that will not come out. ‘The river has very particular tastes, I find. Only Master Harker’s hired. Only the strong ones. Only the ones who stood near the edge at the hiring and looked at the Reach.’ He picks up his pen again. ‘Master Harker pays five coin a head for word of anyone who has been drowned and is walking about. It is a Christian concern. The bereaved must be comforted.’

**Tell Kemble where the drowned are hiding, for five coin a head**

Kemble listens with his pen moving, and asks two questions, very exact, and counts fifteen coins onto the desk in a row. ‘Master Harker thanks you,’ he says. ‘The bereaved will be comforted.’ He does not look up again.

An hour passes. Bess thumps her tail on the ground without getting up, which is as much welcome as you are going to get.

**Wait › An hour**

The Hiring Ring is empty: a rope on stakes round a trodden circle of ice, and in the middle a mop-head somebody dropped, frozen to the ice. An hour passes.

**Wait › An hour**

At noon three of Harker’s wharf men come down Freezeland Street with boar-spears, at the Butchers’ Company’s asking. Before they reach the ox, Absolute gets up off it, all at once, and goes away from them down the ice past the last booth, onto the Reach, at a long rolling run, with his broken chain jumping behind him, and Hester Mabb running after him calling, until she cannot run any more.

In the night, from down the Reach, a long sound of saws, a dozen of them, and men singing to keep the time. By morning there is a channel of black water forty feet wide across the foot of the Reach from bank to bank, steaming, and the sledge road south ends at it.

**Wait › An hour**

Late in the night, from down the Reach, a roar, and dogs, and men shouting, and lanterns going every way at once over the ice. In the morning they say Harker’s searchers met Hester Mabb’s bear on the Black Reach in the dark, and ran, all three, and the dog with them, back to Hay Wharf, and would not go out again; and that the bear went on down the grey ice toward the hole that never freezes, and through it.

**Wait › An hour**

In the night there is a noise of boots on the bridge, and a door broken in, and in the morning the rope-walk house stands open with its hemp all over the street: Harker’s searchers took a drowned girl out of a hole under the floor, and an old woman with her, who walked between them very straight and said nothing at all. In the small hours there are lanterns and shouting out on the Reach, and a dog going mad, round the hulk Patience. In the morning everybody knows: Harker’s searchers took three drowned men out of her hold, alive, and Dickon Cobb with them, and walked them up Hay Stairs to the bondhouse with their hands tied, to be landed at the Landing.

Under your feet, all over Pellam, the ice has begun to talk: a long creak like a ship’s timbers, a crack like a gun a long way off, and then a groan that goes on and on, up the whole length of the river. On the ice everybody stops and listens, and laughs, too loudly, and goes on.

At Hay Stairs, as the watermen hurry the last of the Fair off the ice, the bailiffs are waiting with their writ, and Sir Lionel Gant walks up the stairs into their hands with his hat on, very straight, saying ‘It was three’ to the bailiff on his left. **Wait › An hour**

Swan Stairs go down twelve steps into black water running fast, full of broken ice. The watermen are already putting their boats in. The stairs go down into running water now. From down the Reach, all at once, a sound like a city falling down: the ice going out. It comes up the river from the sea toward the bridge in the dark, crack after crack, louder, the whole frozen Lisk breaking into plates and rearing up against the starlings and going under, and the black water coming up through it steaming, and people on both banks with torches shouting at it, or crying, or cheering. By midnight there is a river under Pellam Bridge, running, full of ice, where Freezeland Street was.

**Wait › An hour**

Of the bear: Hester Mabb sat all day at the bear-stake with the broken chain in her lap, and went up the North Road this morning alone. Of the wedding: Alderman Gosling and his bride went home to Fleece Row after the Landing, and at midnight, they say, when the ice went, the alderman was seen alone on the bridge in his chain, looking down at it. Of the debtor: Sir Lionel Gant is in the Fleece Row compter for thirty coin, saying to anyone who visits him that it was three.

**Wait › An hour**

In the morning the river is running and Harker’s hired are on Ossett’s agent’s sledges on the south road over the downs, roped, the first party out of Pellam since the snow, and among them, coughing, Dickon Cobb, landed. You have fifteen coin. Kemble wrote you a receipt. Widow Saffery’s rope-walk house on the bridge is shut and nobody knows where she is. On the stake by the Black Reach, where the hole was, somebody has hung a hat, and under it, in the snow, put a penny.
