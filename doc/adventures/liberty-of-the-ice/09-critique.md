# An honest literary and experiential critique

*Written after playing the finished work through its nine transcripts and its wanders, as a reader who did not write
it would try to read it. Against the repertoire survey's list of what Kyrie had not yet learned to express, and the
atlas's §30.*

## What it is

A comedy of deception in a city on a frozen river, with a romance in it, a trafficking plot under it, and a law that
the whole of it turns on. It is the collection's first work in which the protagonist lies for a living, wants money,
can be robbed by their partner, can betray hidden people for fifteen coin, can be pilloried, can marry, and can change
the law by an argument they found with a heretic. Nothing in it is older than thirty-one winters. Nobody descends to
a mechanism. There is no other plane.

## What works

- **The law is a game.** "Say what you will; keep what you shake on" is learned by being bitten three times in the
  first hour, and then everything is played through it. Every handshake is a `Grave` act and the page asks before it,
  which is the single best fit between a fiction and a construct in the module: the player feels a handshake as a
  thing that cannot be taken back because it is one.
- **The weather is the plot.** The snow makes the lie uncheckable, keeps the hired in the bondhouse, keeps the drowned
  hidden, and the thaw lands or melts everything at once. Remove the frost and nothing works; that is the right
  dependency.
- **The farce has an engine.** Belief built of props (a printed tally, wool, a coat, a letter, a tedious partner), a
  collision clock (the real shepherd shouting *Hawsfold* in the Cheaping), and success that costs (supper; the wedding;
  a present; a man at the North Bar). The Flock That Was, in which the lie becomes true because the protagonist pays
  the real shepherd with the mark's money, is the funniest outcome in the collection and the most honest.
- **Two approaches that are different games.** The Front Door is talk, props and timing over three days; the Ring is
  being locked in a barn with twenty frightened people, and is over in a night. A labourer with no charm and a bard
  play different stories, and the labourer's is the shorter and the darker.
- **The philosophy has consequences.** Callow's question has three answers, each with a cost the player lives with:
  the Reeve's (he is convicted), his own (he takes you at your word and leaves you to pay for him), and a third that
  only someone who has seen a hand shaken on a lie can give, which becomes law for one afternoon. The argument is the
  lever; the lever is the argument.
- **The city is alive without the mystery.** A debtor who lives on the ice; a natural philosopher who is not a fraud; an
  alderman who cannot rhyme his bride's name because every poem turns into the girl he married for four hours thirty-one
  years ago; a printer; a bear who hates the cold. Most of them can be ignored, and are worse off for it.
- **The prose** keeps the house voice (plain, concrete, dry) and finds a comic register inside it: "Nobody would
  invent anything so tedious"; "It was not a lie. It was an improvement" is Wrayford's, but "A man who has his flock
  printed is a man who means to sell it" is Pellam's.

## What does not, or not yet

- **The horror is thin.** The night ice, the searchers' lanterns, the bondhouse loft with the names cut in the beams,
  one unexplained second light: these are moments, and a player on the Front Door never has to go out at night at
  all. The design said "the comedy at night", and that is what it is; it is not *A Winter in Carth*. A work that wanted
  dread would make the second night unavoidable.
- **Harker is a type.** The repertoire wanted a human culprit who chose harm; Harker is one, and the farce form excuses
  his flatness, but he never surprises. He has no scene in which the player sees why. A better draft would give him
  one private moment (with Thessaly; with his wife's portrait) that the player can choose to witness.
- **The romance is a set of facts read once.** It is honest and not a meter, and Ursel's question is good, but the
  courtship is three or four scenes, all of them good, none of them long. The skating at dusk is the best scene in
  the work and it is one action.
- **Dickon is under-written.** The man who walked out of Gallowmoor has three topics. His plan exists mostly so the
  player can execute it.
- **The Landing is a sequence of rules.** It reads as a trial and plays as five half-hours with one or two actions
  each. A player who misses a case's window misses it, and the page does not warn them that the next case is coming.
  Kyr's occasions are all like this (friction 9).
- **News arrives in the wrong tense.** A company that comes back to the ice a day late is told "word comes down
  Freezeland Street at a run" about a bear who has been loose since dawn (friction 2).
- **Followers are noisy.** Callow in custody is announced in every room.
- **The day-one Front Door is fast.** A good liar can be believed, sup, tour the bondhouse and know everything on the
  first day; the second day then has little pressure until the night. This is partly the farce's nature (it runs
  downhill) and partly that Harker should test the Squire again on the second day. He does not.
- **What I could not test**: no browser journey was recorded; the *Away* ending is unplayed; the bear at the wedding,
  the bear lent past the searchers, the wedding presents, the Gazette column and the lie that sends the searchers to the
  downs are written, linted and reached by the wanders' random play only by chance, not asserted by a test.

## Against the survey's list

| What Kyrie had not learned to express | Here |
|---|---|
| Wanting | Yes: money, at first, and the partner's want under it |
| Fault | Yes: the leak; the sale; a hand broken |
| A person as an end | Partly: Tansy Hobb, Dickon, Ursel; the hired are a crowd more than people |
| Making | Partly: the lie is made of props, and a better tally is a better lie; nothing is made for its own sake |
| Comedy as structure | Yes, the work's main claim |
| Fear that lasts | No |
| The small that stays small | Yes: several of the stories (the alderman, the debtor, the bear, the cream) end in the parish |
| Minds changed by people | Yes: Ursel by being matched and kept faith with; Gosling by a word told to Bel; Callow by an answer; Tredgold by the truth about his ice; Abel by anything |

## Verdict

A good comedy with a serious centre, which a cold player could finish in an evening and replay as someone else; the
first work in the collection that is *fun* in the old sense. Its satire is a real law, its philosophy a real lever,
and its romance is real but brief. Its horror is a gesture. The next pass should give Harker one human scene, the
second day one more test of the Squire, and the second night a reason nobody can avoid it.

## After the critique: what the last pass changed

Two of the faults above were fixed after this critique was first written, and tested:

- **Harker has one human scene.** Late, after supper, asked about his wife's portrait, he tells the Squire that he was
  himself hired at the Pellam hiring at nine, for a farm in the hills that was a dye-works, and served eleven years:
  "a hand is all a poor man has to sell. So I buy it, at a fair price, and I never lie about the price." It explains
  him without excusing him, and nothing mechanical turns on it.
- **Harker tests the Squire again on the second day.** Talked to, he asks which way the beck runs at Hawsfold. The
  right answer is the one the real shepherd gave in the Cheaping ("east, down to the Swale"), which rewards having
  listened to Abel; the wrong one costs two points of belief; a liar can bluff it.

The others stand: the horror is still a gesture, the romance still brief, Dickon still thin, and the Landing still a
sequence of half-hours.

## The second reading

*After a second request to read it again, harder, as literature and as dramaturgy; and what the second pass did about
what that reading found. The first critique's sections above stand as written, as the record of what it thought then.*

### What I was not happy with

- **The work had no shape in time.** It had acts in the synopsis, but on the page the three days were the same day
  three times: the same light, the same street. The farce had a clock and the reader could not feel it. This was the
  worst fault, because it is the one a player feels without being able to name it.
- **Its theme was stated, not distributed.** "A hand" was the law, the con, the romance and the trial; but only Harker
  and Callow had a *position* on it. Ursel's refusal to shake was a tic, Tansy's shaking was a fact, and the city's law
  had no history: the Liberty was a premise rather than an inheritance.
- **The horror was a gesture** (the first critique said so) and the second night, the hinge of the flight, had no
  danger in it that was not a guard to be avoided. The searchers are paid men; nothing on the Reach could kill you.
- **The world stopped at the snow.** The roads are shut, which is the premise of the con, and so the work never looked
  past them: Gallowmoor was a word, Swale Head a token in a hat, the river's past a single "last Frost".
- **Pulp exuberance.** Some of the prose is too pleased with itself. Ursel's "laugh like a door" is used as a tag four
  times and becomes a Homeric epithet in a work that does not otherwise use them; there were six "a long moment"s and
  eighteen "a long time"s, the reflex of a writer marking a silence instead of filling it; the ice going out "like a
  city falling down" and the Exchange "in a panic" are opera, where the rest of the work is chamber music. The second
  pass cut the long moments and most of the long times, replacing each with something that happens in the silence (a
  clock on the stair; a skater going by; a man at the next table accused of cheating at cards); it kept the door and
  the opera, because a farce is allowed one tutti, and the thaw is it.

### What the second pass did

- **The days are acts, and each has its own air.** A hard blue frost, in which every sound carries and nothing can be
  checked (the farce); the *drowned weather*, a fog off the Reach to the height of a house, in which the city cannot
  see its own feet (the mystery, and the drowned); the thaw-wind, the bridge dripping, the glove limp on its mast, the
  ice gone grey (the reckoning). Each morning opens on a heading and its sky; a bill on the Glove Post says what is on.
- **The hand is everybody's question.** Harker and Ursel are now of one parish, Swale Head, and two answers to the
  same kitchen: a father with a note against the wool, a factor's glove on the table. He was sold at nine and learned
  to buy; she watched and never shook again. Tansy Hobb is from the farm below Ursel's, and has heard of a Swale Head
  girl who never shook on anything and did well in the town. Dickon's hand was counted twice a day at Gallowmoor by an
  overman who never looked at his face. Harker pays every spring for a pound of wax before the Saint's scales, "for
  the weight". Nothing explains anyone; it rhymes them.
- **The law has a history**, kept upside down at the back of the Watermen's accounts: seven frosts in two hundred
  years, of which the Glove made the Liberty, the Black Lambing made the hiring and the city's Statute of Servants,
  and one hanged a Reeve, Broom's ancestor, for landing a bargain against an alderman's son. Most of the seven pay off
  in nothing; they are there to be more than the story needs, which is how a world feels larger than its plot.
- **The world past the snow.** Gallowmoor's ladder and its eleven hundred hands; Swale Head's wash-dub and the Hobbs'
  cow Lady who will stand for nobody else; Ossett's dye-works, where Harker served eleven years; the Lady Day sessions,
  on dry ground, where a lie is a crime and the Statute runs. The Statute is a lever with no ending: laid before
  Gosling, it is nailed to Harker's door in the thaw. The land goes on after the ice.
- **Danger, and many ways round it.** Wolves, four, off the downs after the frozen river, sitting across the sledge
  road on the second night like dogs outside a butcher's. They are dangerous to fight; and they can be driven off with
  fire (a penny brand at the ox fire, a greasy fleece at the bondhouse brazier), fed the ox's offal, gone round
  downwind by a woodsman, faced down by a bully, outrun on the sledges. Five people foreshadow them on the second day;
  one book has them in four of seven frosts. If Dickon goes south alone that night, a warning sends him with fire;
  without it, a waterman finds a cap. Ursel goes and looks, and says it is not his.
- **Paid men, so that a plain player has more doors.** Kemble drunk at the Lamb says *eight a head, Gallowmoor* and
  sleeps on his key; the searchers walk the downs road for three coin a man; old Fowke sleeps in company over a bottle,
  and talks first about the last Frost, when he walked to Bankside for a bet and came back married.
- **Character, in the dark.** Ursel's three confidences at night; Tansy singing *come up, Cushie* to cows that are not
  there; Dickon's slate of lies (THE BRIDGE IS MADE OF CHEESE) and his letters the overman burned; Callow, on the first
  morning, looking at the ice under his boots: "I shall not see it go out, I think. That is all right. Nobody sees
  everything go."
- **The Landing signposts itself**: the clerk's list chalked at the Reeve's elbow, crossed off as each case is cried.
- **A hand shaken in the Ring and walked away from is fetched** the same afternoon, except from the Clink, within
  whose walls no man owes.

### Is there genuine depth?

More than there was, and of the right kind: not explanation, but correspondence. The deepest thing in the work is
now that its villain and its heroine are the same child, a decade apart, who drew opposite conclusions from the same
handshake; and that the law that frees the tongue is the very gap the hands fall through. The comedy's question (can
you trust what is said?) and the tragedy's (what can be done with a hand?) have become one question asked by two
registers. Whether that is depth or only design depends on whether a player feels it without the synopsis, and I
cannot know that from tests. A player who never hears Ursel at night, nor Harker at the portrait, plays the shallower
work, which is still a good farce. That is acceptable: depth that must be found is the OSR's kind.

### Tonal variation

The work now has four registers and places them: farce by day (the Cheaping, the Squire, the Bench), sentiment by dusk
(the Flashes, the Ice Chapel, the alderman's four hours), dread by night (the fog, the wolves, the cap), and law in
the afternoon (the Court, the Landing, the Statute). They are mixed in single scenes on purpose: the alderman's
comedy is a man unable to say a girl's name for thirty-one years; Kemble's drunken joke, "very deep hills", is the
most frightening line in the work.

### Is it playable and compelling as written?

Playable: forty-eight tests (journeys by the labels a player sees, and twelve long random wanders) say so, for six builds. Compelling: for a player who talks to people,
yes; the work rewards curiosity more than any other thing, and that is its risk, because a player who only walks and
waits sees fog, a bill, a bear loose and a Landing, and Landed. The signposting (the bill, the chalked list, the
foreshadowing table in the synopsis) is there for that player.

### What still is not right

- Harker's tragedy is mostly told, not played. The second pass gave him one scene in which he might change and does
  not: Ursel, at the Landing, asking him whether it was he who came up the dale with her father's note ("I do not
  remember the farms. I remember the hands."). It is a topic, reached only by a player who has heard both their
  stories; most will not see it. That is the right rarity for it, and the wrong place for the work's best scene.
- The romance is still a set of good scenes, not a progress. The confidences deepen her; nothing in them asks
  anything of the player.
- The Landing is still five half-hours of rules, now with a list on a board.
- The fog and the thaw are words; the engine's light rules (friction 15) and its weather do not change with them.
  Nothing is harder to see in the fog.
- The wolves can kill a first-level company that chooses to fight, which is right for the form and will surprise a
  player who has spent two days talking.

### If the ambition were ten times this

I would start not with more Pellam but with the other side of the snow, because the work's best idea is that the ice
is a liberty from the land, and the land has never been drawn:

1. **Gallowmoor as a country of its own**: the ladder, the overman, the chapel, the eleven hundred hands, the song
   about the hills; an adventure about getting out, which Dickon did, as the dark twin of getting in.
2. **The Last Frost, thirty-one winters ago, as a playable past**: Gosling's four-hour marriage, Saffery's son under
   the ice, Fowke's bet, young Harker home from Ossett with money and no hand to give; the same city, the same law,
   the same Landing, a generation earlier, so that what is found there changes what is known here.
3. **The river's other cities**: the sea a day east, the coal staithes the *Patience* came from, the cities that have
   their own frosts and their own Liberties, each with a different law on the ice (one where a lie binds and a hand
   does not), so that the law is a variable a campaign can play with.
4. **The seven frosts as an anthology**, each a small adventure in its century, with the Book of Frosts as its index.
5. **The Lady Day sessions**: the land's court, where the Statute runs and a lie is a crime, and everyone who was clever
   on the ice must answer for it on dry ground.
