# The Cradle Across the Cleave

*A story, before it is code. Wonder, a crossing, a birth.*

## The ground

The Cleave is a gorge a quarter-mile across and too deep to hear the bottom of. On its north lip stands Wick Edge, a
town of nine chimneys. On the south lip stands Overleap, which is a farm and a half. For sixty years the two were one
parish, because between them hung the Spinners' bridge: a road of grey silk a cart wide, spun in a single night each
thaw by nine great spiders the size of ponies, and kept by their old Mother, Cerise, from a hub of silk and rock
halfway across.

Cerise died at midwinter. Her daughters wrapped her in silk and hung her from the hub, where she turns in the wind
and glows a little after dark. They have not spun since. The bridge went slack, then sagged, then in the first
thaw gale it went altogether, leaving a Stub of road sixty yards out from the north lip and a loose grey beard on the
south. Wick Edge has been cut off from its own farms for eleven days.

On the south lip, in the Pennards' kitchen, Linnet Pennard has been in labour since the small hours. Her husband,
Dov, came north the night before to fetch the midwife. He has been stuck on the wrong side of the hole for a night
and a morning, and has been trying to cross it ever since.

## The company's morning

The company is on the road into Wick Edge when Dov comes at them down it, hatless, with a midwife's bag in each
hand. He has been carrying both for eleven hours, because Hesper Lunn would not carry hers across a thing she had not
decided to cross.

Hesper Lunn is seventy-four. She has delivered fourteen hundred and six children and kept a tally on the kitchen
door-post with a nail. She crossed the Spinners' bridge every week for forty years on a donkey named Prudence.
Now she sits at her table with her boots on and will not go, and everybody thinks they know why.

> 'It is the basket,' says Dov. 'Ned Quarrel's basket. It swings.'
>
> 'It does swing,' says Hesper. 'I have no objection to swinging. I have been swung. Sit down, boy, you are making
> the bag nervous.'

She does not say why. The company is expected to find out, and the finding-out is the adventure.

## What each person wants

**Hesper Lunn** wants to be at Linnet's bedside and cannot say what stops her. Forty years ago, a girl of fourteen,
she sat out a hard spring night on the hub with Cerise's own last egg-case under her apron, because a spider who is
dying of cold cannot hatch what she has laid, and nobody else in Wick Edge would touch it. The egg hatched. The Mother
remembered her. For forty years Cerise's silk carried her over the Cleave and she never once looked down. Now
Cerise is dead, the bridge is gone, and the thing under the hub is another egg-case, cold since midwinter, that the
daughters will not leave. She knows it as she knows her own hands. She has not been out to look because she is
afraid of what it means: that if she goes and it is dead, the bridge is dead too, and if she goes and it is alive,
she is asked to sit another night in the wind at seventy-four.

**Dov Pennard** wants someone to tell him what to do, and keeps providing the wrong things to do. He has tried the
basket (Ned would not send it in a gust), the Stub (he got ten yards), and the ledge ladder (he stood on the first
rung for a long time). He is afraid for Linnet in a way that comes out as chatter, and as the bags.

**Ned Quarrel** keeps the haulers' line: one cable, one wicker basket, one hand-brake, five coin a crossing and
double in a gust. He is sour about the bridge. Forty years of free silk cost him forty years of trade, and he does
not see why the thing should come back. He is also the most honest person in Wick Edge, and the only one who has
actually crossed since the bridge fell. Ask him straight and he will send you, in calm air, for five coin, and sit
on the brake and not lie to you about the weather.

**Cassia Wend** has been the web-warden at the Stub for twelve years and has not slept in nine nights. She sits at
the cut end of the Stub with a reed-pipe, humming the note the spiders spin to, and the spiders, who can see her,
do not spin. She will tell you the Mother is dead and the daughters are in mourning and the cold case at the hub is
not to be touched. She is right about all three. She is also wrong about the third.

**Ottoline Toop** lives on a ledge thirty feet under the Stub with nineteen hens and a ring of coloured lamps,
which she uses to talk to Overleap. She is sixty-eight. She flies a kite she calls a flitter, a thing of oiled
silk and ash-wood that she has been jumping off her ledge on since she was thirty-one. She knows the mother's
chimes. She knows the Pennards' lamp-code: three red and one white means *come*, white white white means *a daughter*.

**Linnet Pennard**, across the gap, is in the middle of the oldest work there is, and is being looked after
by her mother, **Gammer Roke**, who has delivered two hundred calves and feels that this qualifies her.

## Scenes that should be good

1. **Hesper's kitchen, morning.** She will not discuss it. The company can learn her three rules of midwifery
   from her (the cord, the cloth, the waiting), which she teaches as if irritated, and which matter if the company
   goes across alone. Pressed about the hub she says only: 'I do not go out to the hub.' *Out to the hub.* Nobody
   else said hub.

2. **Cassia at the Stub, humming.** She does not look up. 'Don't step on the thread.' The Stub is a road of grey
   silk, tacky, springing, going out into nothing. At the end of it the world falls away, and across the gap hangs
   a lit lantern the size of a coffin, turning slowly: Cerise. Around her, nine long dark shapes, not moving.
   Cassia tells you what she knows and does not tell you she is afraid that the egg is dead.

3. **The ledge.** Ottoline, hens, a rope-ladder, wind. She lets the company send a lamp-message to Linnet; the
   answer comes in minutes: three red, one white. *Come.* She also offers the flitter, once: it takes one person, and
   it is a long, silent glide across the Cleave to the south lip. In the lull before sunset the whole gorge is
   gold and the bottom is a river of cloud.

4. **The hub at dusk.** Hesper has come after all, if she has been told the egg is cold. Walking the Stub with
   her is slow, because she holds Dov's arm and will not look anywhere but at the thread. At the hub the
   daughters part around her. They know her. The oldest daughter, whom Cassia calls Dun, taps the silk twice and
   drops a thread across her lap, and Hesper, seventy-four years old, sits down on the hub of the world with a
   cold egg-case in her apron and says, 'Well. Here we are again.' It takes the night. The company can sit with
   her, or go down on the ledge, or stand watch. In the small hours the egg-case cracks, and two hundred
   spiders the size of a thumbnail come out onto her lap and, one at a time, spin their first thread, and the
   thread goes out into the dark toward the south lip and stays.

5. **Linnet's kitchen.** However the company arrives, the baby is coming. Gammer Roke, who has no use for
   midwives, hands Hesper a towel with the air of a woman handing over a sword. If the company comes alone,
   Gammer says, 'Who are you?', and then, 'Can you boil water?', and the lessons are what they have.

## Consequences

- If the midwife is carried over by the line in calm air, the baby is born healthily and the bridge does not come
  back. Wick Edge pays Ned five coin a crossing for the rest of the year. Cassia sits on the Stub another
  winter.
- If the egg hatches at the hub, the bridge is spun by morning and every person in both parishes walks across it
  at dawn. Cerise is let down into the mist by her daughters as the sun comes up.
- If the company crosses alone on the flitter, with Hesper's three rules and her bag, they will deliver the child
  themselves with Gammer Roke, and not well if they did not listen.
- If the company is late, it is late. The baby comes. Linnet is alive, and tired, and does not want to be told
  how the company was delayed.
- The wind rises in the afternoons and falls at dusk, but not always: sometimes it will not let up, and the
  company must choose between a rough night walk and the line.

## What is unusual

It is a crossing and an errand with a fixed human stake, not a mystery with a verdict. The wonder is functional:
the spiders are not guarded treasure but an unresolved grief that needs one old woman's hands. There is no villain.
There is no ledger. The central act is *sitting up with something that is trying to be born*, on both sides of a gap.
