# The Still Hours: the story first

*Pass one. Fiction before implementation; some of it survives into the Kyr nearly verbatim, some is cut.*

## The premise

Every thirty-one years, on an October night, the Sellow Wood gets up. It pulls its roots out of the ground with a
sound like a tablecloth torn the whole length of a table, and it walks east, a mile deep and nine across, to the
Maugh Mere, where it lies down in the shallows and seeds. The road it takes goes through Haywad.

It goes round whatever stands still. It goes through whatever moves. Haywad is sixty-three people and four doors on
the Way, and a door with someone standing in it is a rock in a stream; a door with no one in it is a stump. Every
thirty-one years the village counts its standers, and every thirty-one years there are fewer.

The company arrives the morning of the Rising, as the village is hiring anyone with steady hands.

## Who is in the Way

**Edda Notler**, Marshal of the Way, sixty-six. Has stood the west door since the last Rising, alone, through six
bad winters of other people's rumours. Her husband Orrin was a Sellow, a walk-born: the wood left him in a doorway
sixty-two years ago, in a nest of leaves, and took him back on the night of his thirty-first birthday. Everyone in
Haywad says he ran, and was trodden, and Edda lets them. She stood in her door that night and watched her husband
walk into the trees at an easy pace, with the trees going round him like water round a wading man. She did not
follow. In the morning there was a baby in the doorway, in leaves.

**Tibyl Sellow**, thirty-one tonight. The baby. Edda raised her, and she moved out at nineteen, to the Hollow at
the east end of the Way, a house built in the stump of a tree that did not get up in time. She can feel the Rising
in her back teeth. She hums at the pitch the wood hums when it is thinking. She has never said she means to go,
and Edda has never asked.

**Rogot Selve**, the lampwright, eighty, deaf, fond of money, and the only person alive who saw what Edda saw. He
sells doorway lamps at eleven crowns each and swears by them. Pressed, he will tell you the lamp does nothing for
the wood. 'It is for your hands,' he says. 'A person standing still wants a thing to hold. Otherwise they scratch.'

**Sorel Fender**, baker, widowed in the last Rising by a man who ran, and her son **Pipet**, twelve, who has
decided to see the trees from the inside. He is not wicked. He is curious, which, tonight, is worse.

**Grik Toller**, a birdfolk surveyor in the rafters of the Stile, three weeks into the only measurement of the
Sellow anyone has ever made. She has proved that the wood goes round a still bell and through a swung one. She
has not noticed what her instruments say about the third thing: that it goes round a thing that moves *with* it.

## The night, in scenes

**The marking.** Edda, at her door, handing you a chalk. 'Hold still,' she says. 'That is the whole of the work. It
sounds like very little. The last strangers I hired, one sneezed and one prayed, and the prayer was worse.'

**Rogot's shop.** Forty lamps in the window, and he cannot hear a word you say. You write on his slate. He reads it
and answers the question beneath the question.

**Statues.** Pipet, on the bakehouse step, throwing a stone at nothing. He wants to know what it is like.
The company can say: play statues, and teach him a word. *Stand.* A boy who has been taught one word to freeze at
will freeze at it, in the dark, in the middle of a forest that is walking, when his mother cannot say anything he
will listen to.

**The Rising.** At nine the pewter on every shelf in Haywad starts to hum. The ground ticks. The tops of the trees at
the end of the Way lean east and do not come back. The wood is not a wall coming but a hillside standing up: roots
lifting, trailing their dirt like hems, each trunk walking in a slow rolling stride with the crown held level. It
smells of leaf-mould and rain and cold iron. Lamps do not make it brighter. The trunks glow a little at the knee,
where the fungus is.

**Holding.** A doorway. A lamp in each hand. A trunk the width of a cart passes close enough to touch. You do not
touch it. Later: a sapling, running with the rest, carrying in its branches a child's wooden horse it has had
since before you were born. A heron riding a bough with its eyes shut. A rain of old leaves, red, dry, entirely
silent.

**Pipet.** At the first hour past ten, the boy is not on the step. He is thirty paces into the Way with his arms
spread, laughing. If you taught him *Stand*, you say it, and he stops like a clock, and the wood divides around him,
and the white of his teeth is the last thing you see before the trunks close. If you did not, someone has to go
out among the legs of the trees and hold him still.

**The Heart.** At two, the oldest of the trees, the Leaner, which has leaned east since before there was a Haywad,
comes down the Way at a stroll and stops at the Hollow. It stops at the Hollow every time. It lowers a branch
the size of a bridge to the doorstep and holds it there. Tibyl, in her doorway, is standing very straight.

**The choice.** She can stand. The Leaner will wait a while, and go on. She can step on to the branch. The one thing
nobody in Haywad has told her is that it is not a sentence: that Orrin Sellow was not trodden. He was carried. He
keeps a hut in the crown of the Leaner, and tends the seedlings, and has written to nobody for thirty-one years
because there is no post.

And Edda, at the other end of the Way, can stand again. Or walk east with the wood, at its pace, which is the one
way through the trees nobody in Haywad has ever tried. You may be the person who tells her.

**The Mere.** After, on the shore, in the pale morning, the elders lie down in the shallows one by one, slowly, like
cattle. The mud is full of green. By noon the seedlings are knee-high. A man of ninety-three with a beard like
moss sits on a branch the thickness of a road, and is greatly surprised to see anybody.

## What is not obvious

- There is no villain. The harm in this book is something people do to themselves for love, by standing very still.
- The truth about Orrin is a discovery that changes what can be done, not a verdict. Learn it early and Edda has to
  be told something she has hidden for thirty-one years. Learn it late and there is no time to tell her.
- The wood is not benign. It has trodden a man this night, in some versions. The warning is real.

## Endings, as small scenes, not a table

- **Held.** The wood passes. Haywad is whole. Tibyl stays, the first walk-born to refuse. The Leaner waits an hour
  at the Hollow, and lowers its branch, and lifts it.
- **Carried.** Tibyl goes. Edda stands. At dawn Edda goes to the Hollow and sets a bowl of porridge on the step,
  as for a lodger.
- **Both.** Edda walks east with the wood and Tibyl. The Marshal's chalk is left on the step.
- **Trodden.** A door lost, a child hurt, a lamp rolling.
