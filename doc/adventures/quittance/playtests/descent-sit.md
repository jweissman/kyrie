# The Descent: three fighters, no charm, and a Fire that wants company

A company of warriors with no licence, no key and no friends in the city goes down by the Exchange cellar stair, pays the Queue a penny a head, pays the glass men their fee, and sits with the Fire.

Seed `descent-sit`. Played by the labels a player sees; each block is the page's telling of the step.

**1. To the Declarations Hall** _(watch 0, arch_hall)_

> The city is already awake, and you are standing at its edge with a little money and nothing insured. In nine days it will be Reckoning. Under the Arch the road widens into a hall: counters, brass rails, and a clerk to every counter under a hundred-year-old painting of the Burning. The Honest Bell hangs in the vault above on a plain iron hook, a large, plain, dull bell, with a rope that nobody holds. It rings by itself, once, for a lie told under it. A sign: DECLARE YOUR GOODS. DECLARE YOUR PURPOSE. DECLARE YOUR INTENTIONS, IF YOU HAVE ANY. On a board by the Collector’s desk: RULES OF THE BELL. 1. Goods shall be declared. 2. Purpose shall be declared. 3. A declaration shall be true. 4. The Bell is not to be reasoned with. 5. A declaration is not required to be complete, but must be sufficient. 6. The Collector is paid by the declaration and not by the fine. Rule five has been underlined by someone, in pencil, and a small hand has written beside it: ‘sufficient to what?’. The Arch’s inner mouth is barred by a brass rail, and the Collector has not passed you through it. The Honest Bell hangs in the vault of the Arch, plain and dull and larger than a cart-wheel. Mr Prest, the Collector of Declarations, is here.

**2. Declare yourselves truthfully, and take out a Policy of Passage (3 coin)** _(watch 1, arch_hall)_

> You declare your goods, which are few, your purpose, which you find is harder to put in a sentence than you expected, and your intentions, such as they are. The Honest Bell hangs in the vault above and says nothing at all. Mr Prest writes in the Roll of Entrances, blots it, and hands you a Policy of Passage. ‘Welcome to Quittance,’ he says. ‘For nine days you are insured.’ Orm takes a Policy of Passage.

**3. Out to Gate Plaza** _(watch 1, gate_plaza)_

> Gate Plaza is a square of brokers, porters and people who have just been told what they owe. Every doorway has a plate over it, and every plate has a small rival plate beside it that is smaller. A man is selling mark-polish from a tray. ‘Rates! Rates! Fire at ninepence, water at a groat!’ The brokers cry them in a chant as old as the Arch, and no one is listening and everyone is. A tarnished placard on the Arch wall, framed against pigeons: THE ASSIZE OF MARKS, YEAR THREE. A MARK IS THE POLICY. NO MARK, NO RESCUE. ORDERED AFTER BREWERS’ ROW, WHERE BOTH BRIGADES ARRIVED AT ONE FIRE AND EACH WAITED FOR THE OTHER TO PASS ITS HOSE. THE LOSS WAS TWENTY-SIX HOUSES AND A BREWERY. THE BREWERY WAS INSURED.

**4. >quittance__brokers_stair** _(watch 2, exchange_court)_

> Forty steps of worn limestone climb out of the Gate Quarter toward the Exchange. Brokers sit on them in the sun with their hats beside them like cats, shouting rates at nobody.

**5. >quittance__row_street** _(watch 2, exchange_court)_

> Policy Row is the widest street in Quittance and the only one that is entirely stone. The Offices face each other across it like families at a wedding: the Phoenix with its gilt bird, the Lamprey’s black fish over a door that always seems wet, the Sovereign’s sun, the Providence’s open hand, and, squeezed between two of them like a cough, a door with an eye over it that says only RESIDUARY. Clerks go by in sleeve-guards, not looking at one another.

**6. >quittance__exchange_court** _(watch 2, exchange_court)_

> The court of the Old Exchange is paved in black glass that is not quite level. The Exchange itself is a stump: four arches and a plinth, preserved exactly as the Burning left them, with a rail round it and a notice, DO NOT PRESUME UPON THE FOUNDATIONS. To one side the Hall of the Five stands with its bronze doors open, and on its front, in letters of white fire gone the colour of old teeth, the Receipt: RECEIVED OF THE CITY OF QUITTANCE, THE SUM OF ITS PREMIUM, IN FULL. Not much of it can be read. The Receipt on the Hall’s front is still legible if you stand where the clerks stand. RECEIVED OF THE CITY OF QUITTANCE, THE SUM OF ITS PREMIUM, IN FULL. The last three words have gone the colour of old teeth; the rest is white. At the head of the cellar stair a black iron door is shut, with a bird for a keyhole. It is warm to the hand, and a very little air leans on it from below. A bronze notary stands on a granite plinth in the middle of the court, a quill in her left hand and a sheaf under her arm. Her right hand is held out, palm up, a little cupped, as if to receive something small. No bird has ever settled in it. On the south side of the court a wall of fused black glass, ten feet high, is all that is left of the Exchange’s great window. In the day it shows your own face, a little darker than it is.

**7. To the foot of the Exchange stair — put your shoulders to the cellar door until the bar goes** _(watch 3, exchange_court)_

> The bar goes with a crack that runs down the whole stair. Far below, in the dark, something in glass turns its head. Pike resists (dexterity). The cellar door hangs off one hinge, its bar in pieces on the steps.

**8. >quittance__cellar_foot** _(watch 3, queue_hall)_

> At the foot of the Exchange stair the cellars begin, vaulted in black glass, dry, warm, silent. Somewhere there is a draught that moves your lamp-flame toward the dark, steadily, like a hand drawing you by the sleeve. The cellar door hangs off one hinge, its bar in pieces on the steps. The draught in the cellars makes a sound, down the arcade, like someone breathing in to speak and then thinking better of it.

**9. >quittance__cellar_arcade** _(watch 3, queue_hall)_

> An arcade of fused pillars goes away in both directions, and under the arches there are the shapes of desks, counters and ledgers, melted into the floor in the attitude of men and women at work. Past the pillars the arch to the Queue’s hall is open, and from it comes a sound you take for the draught, and which turns out, when you stop, to be several hundred people not coughing. Dobbin Tench, a junior clerk of the Old Exchange, is here.

**10. >quittance__queue_hall** _(watch 3, queue_hall)_

> The Queue: a long vaulted hall with a line of the dead standing in it, patient, in nightclothes and Sunday best and sleeve-guards, a good many with children by the hand. They are the colour of candle smoke. Each holds a scrap of something. They are, to a man, looking toward a desk at the far end where a clerk’s chair is empty, and every one of them is waiting to be called. The dead stand on the head of the slag stair three abreast, politely, to the full width of it, and each holds out a scrap of paper. There is no pushing past them that does not go through someone. Here are Mrs Gorringe, a widow of the Burning, with a claim for furniture; Ned Pounceby, a boy of seven, with a claim for boots; Mr Fluellen, a claimant for a house; and Mr Crumpe, a claimant for his own death, in good health.

**11. Lay three coin on the nearest claim, a penny a head, and ask to be let by** _(watch 4, queue_hall)_

> A woman in a Sunday bonnet looks at the three coins on her claim, and at you, and at the coins. ‘Well,’ she says, ‘it is a penny.’ There is a long, civil pause. ‘Let them by, Mr Tench would say,’ she says to the line, and the line, with great reluctance and perfect manners, draws in its skirts. The dead have drawn aside to the walls of the stair, as people do for a funeral, and a gap runs down the middle of them to the glass.

**12. >quittance__glass_street** _(watch 5, furnace_row)_

> The Glass City: a street of fused houses, a street of glass, slag and brick, all melted at once and cooled at once, so that the façades run into each other like wax. In a window a glass table is laid for four. A glass cat sits in the road. The dead have drawn aside to the walls of the stair, as people do for a funeral, and a gap runs down the middle of them to the glass. A table of glass is laid in a window for four: four plates, four glasses, a tureen with its lid on, all of glass, all fused to the cloth, which is also glass. One chair is pushed back. A cat of clear glass sits in the road with her tail round her feet, looking at the stair you came down.

**13. >quittance__furnace_row** _(watch 5, furnace_row)_

> The three glass men turn together, with a sound like a drawer of cutlery. The first of them holds out a glass hand, palm up. ‘Papers,’ it says, in the voice of someone who has been saying it for ninety-four years. ‘Or the fee. Six. Or the audit.’ The glass men will wait a few minutes more, no longer. Furnace Row: forty furnaces, cold, in a row, each with a brass plate bolted to the brick, each plate the same, THIS PREMISES INSURED. Glass men stand between them in rows, like statues of clerks, their cuffs smoking. At the head of the Last Stair three glass men stand between the furnaces, with their ledgers open. One desk has stayed standing in the Row, its legs run down into the floor like candle-wax. A sheet of paper is sealed under its glass top, perfectly legible. On the dead clerk’s desk stands a lamp of thick glass, with a grey lump in the bowl where the oil was. It is cold.

**14. Show the glass men a Policy of Passage** _(watch 5, furnace_row)_

> ‘Nine days,’ reads the second glass man. ‘Buried, fished out or put out.’ It looks, for a moment, almost wistful. ‘Proceed. The Office regrets.’

**15. >quittance__hearth_below** _(watch 6, hearth_below)_

> A chamber of glass, round, wide as a cathedral crossing, with a great hearth in the floor: not a fire, but the memory of a fire, a bed of white ash as large as a threshing-floor, and in the ash, very small and very bright, a red coal the size of a human heart. The air is a held breath. The coal in the ash is looking at you. It has no eyes, and it is the most attentive thing you have ever stood in front of. At the head of the Last Stair three glass men stand between the furnaces, with their ledgers open. The Fire holds still, as a creditor does who has opened his book on the table. The heat in the hall is the heat of a sum. The Fire, the Name Beneath, is here.

**16. Talk to the Fire** _(watch 6, hearth_below)_

> The ash stirs. Something in it is as courteous as a bailiff at a door. ‘You are late,’ says the Fire.

**17. Tell the Fire that you have come to speak with it** _(watch 6, hearth_below)_

> ‘Who keeps you?’ says the Fire. It is not a pleasantry. It is the first line of a form. Orm, Pike and Bas each gain 300 experience: spoke with the Fire.

**18. Ask the Fire what it is owed** _(watch 6, hearth_below)_

> ‘Company,’ says the Fire. ‘It was agreed. A coal in every house, and one night in the year when the houses come to me. I was given a plate on the door, and then the doors were walled.’ The coal brightens, and settles. ‘I do not complain. I count.’

**19. Sit down in the ash with the Fire for an hour, and say nothing** _(watch 12, hearth_below)_

> You sit. The Fire does not speak. After a while the heat settles on you not as an attack, but as a hand on the back, and you understand that you are being counted, one by one, as present. Somewhere to the west the Phoenix bell begins, and then, a little late and a little out of time, a deeper note that is the Lamprey’s. Over the roofs of the Brickfields a brown thumb of smoke goes up. The two bells go on, not together, for some time.

**20. Sit down in the ash with the Fire for an hour, and say nothing** _(watch 18, hearth_below)_

> You sit. The Fire does not speak. After a while the heat settles on you not as an attack, but as a hand on the back, and you understand that you are being counted, one by one, as present. The coal has drawn in, and the heat with it. The Fire is not weighing you now; it is listening, and it has not done that in a long time.

**21. Sit down in the ash with the Fire for an hour, and say nothing** _(watch 24, hearth_below)_

> You sit. The Fire does not speak. After a while the heat settles on you not as an attack, but as a hand on the back, and you understand that you are being counted, one by one, as present. ‘Three hours,’ says the Fire, to itself. ‘Present for three hours.’ It writes it down somewhere, and you cannot see where. The roof of Number Nine goes in with a long soft sound, like a sigh let out by something that has been holding it. Both captains take their hats off. Neither takes the other’s.

**22. Ask the Fire what it would take, instead of the Burning** _(watch 24, hearth_below)_

> ‘Instead,’ says the Fire. It tastes the word. ‘Instead is a thing that is offered. I will hear it.’

**69. Wait › An hour** _(watch 1248, hearth_below)_

> At eleven on the second day Mr Gudgeon of the Phoenix goes into Tolliver’s with a ten-foot rule, a ledger and a lit candle in his hat, and the bow window is, for a moment, entirely full of apprentice.

**70. Wait › An hour** _(watch 1248, hearth_below)_

> Mr Gudgeon holds a candle up the flue and watches the flame lean, steadily, toward the oven as though drawn by the sleeve. ‘It draws,’ he says, with satisfaction, ‘in the face of the Ordinance.’ He writes a word in the margin of the form, in capitals, and Gwen Tolliver closes her eyes. ‘The cover lapses,’ says Mr Gudgeon, ‘the day after tomorrow, at midnight. Good day.’ At the door, he looks over his shoulder at the loaves, and appears to find something there to forgive. He does not, however, forgive it. At noon on the second day Mr Orpwood signs a Total Loss on the Prudent Margaret, and the lightermen along the Reach read it from a distance and agree that it is a pity. Somewhere in Mortmain a clerk’s pen goes down on a form for the last time, and the Sovereign’s sun is struck through.
