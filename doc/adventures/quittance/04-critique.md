# Quittance — the second review: a literary and dramaturgical reading of the work as built

Written after the thirteen cases, the spine, the Hearing and the Descent were merged and playing. The first review (02) judged a
*design*; this one judges a *text*. Findings are numbered C1… and each says what was done, and what was not.

## What the book is about, and whether the form says it

The book's subject is **what is owed to a guest**. Insurance is its comedy; hospitality is its argument. A premium is a price put on
a risk; hospitality is an obligation put on a person; the Offices have spent a hundred years confusing the two, and the Fire is the
creditor who still knows the difference. Every thread was meant to be an instance:

| Thread | The guest | The host who forgot |
|---|---|---|
| The Receipt | the Fire | the city |
| The Lent coal | the coal in the tin | the households that dropped it |
| Pip, Tolliver | a flame kept by the poor who cannot afford to forget | the Ordinance |
| The Queue | the dead, still waiting to be called | the clerk who never was |
| The Annuitant | a name, kept alive by a family | a contract that says "while the life shall last" |
| The Wake | a parrot, who has outlived the man he was loyal to | two women who cannot agree whose husband it was |
| Candlewick, Gasket | people counted as fewer on paper | Prewitt, who wrote the paper |
| The Arch | the stranger | the Bell, which rings for a lie and not for silence |

**C1. The motif was in the plot but not in the prose.** A reader could finish the book and not notice that "guest" is its
key word. *Done:* the counting-rhyme ("two for the guest that has nothing to eat"), Honora's marginalia ("sufficient, my dear, to the
guest"), the pageant's paper coal, the statue's cupped hand, the Loss-Bell's polished nest, the cut line over the oven — all
`lore.kyr`. *Not done:* the Hearing never says the word; it is argued as arithmetic. That is the right register for the Five, but
a late line from the Chairman of the Residuary would complete it.

**C2. The Fire was a rumour for too long.** Until a company went below, nothing in the surface city moved except a number. *Done:* the
Receipt now loses its words by the day and the clerks chalk the date of each loss; the Exchange paving warms (day 5); the plaster behind
the Hall's dais goes warm, then shows a line of red (day 9); the gulls on the Furnace Wharf face the wrong way; the Founders' Glass
shows, at dusk, a woman walking into a street of fire, writing. *Still weak:* nothing in the *Brickfields* is warm. The Fire's
proximity should be felt in the marked wards as a slight overheating of ordinary stoves (a stove that cracks, a kettle that boils too
quickly) — a good place to put the "houses burning again in marked wards" evidence into the street.

## Tone, contrast and register

**C3. The comedy was uniformly dry.** Dry wit sustained for nine days becomes a mannerism. *Done:* a register of **awe** (the Founders'
Glass; the Hearth Below), **elegy** (the afterwords, which tell the small threads over at the last minute; the Queue's Tench saying good
night to the dead), **tenderness** (Pip, Hob, Auberon), **menace** (the Tithe men's foreman who held the shovel when his mother's coal was taken
off her; the wardens' sergeant whose daughter Ottoline taught to read). The comedy now has people to be *wrong about*. *Still weak:*
the Bare Wards are the warmest place in the book and should also be the funniest; their market has more colour than jokes.

**C4. Pulp exuberance.** Passages that were stock: "A thin man behind a grating…" (Skelter), the villain lines at the Hall, some
Gas Company boardroom. Read hard, the strongest passages are the ones where a **specific object** carries a feeling (the shawl on the empty
chair; the nest polished into a bird; the heel of bread passed round a burning yard) and the weakest are those where a character
**states what they feel** ("He looked old, suddenly, and hard"). *Done:* the new characters of the second pass (the wardens' sergeant, the Tithe men's foreman) are written around an object or a habit. *Not done:* Skelter's greeting and the Hall's villain lines are still the stock
versions this paragraph complains of. *Not done:* a full line edit of the authors' thirteen files. The authors were briefed on this and mostly honoured
it; a human editor should still read `q_barge.kyr` and `q_coldlamp.kyr` aloud.

## Characters

**C5. Prewitt.** The best-conceived person in the book and the least seen. The player meets him for ten minutes on day 1 and again at Gasket
Yard or the Hearing. *Done:* his wardens, his shawl, his four levers, the lamp, the Bell ringing for his denial. *Missing:* one scene in which he
is **kind** to the company — a gift, an unexpected piece of help — so that the turn at the end is a return and not an arrival.

**C6. Ottoline** is a function (lever, companion, kin). Her one great moment is the lane; her pencil is the only thing in her hair. She needs a case of her
own to run: the best candidate is a small Phoenix claim she is failing at, which the company can help or hinder.

**C7. Honora.** The most important character in the book is dead and never on stage. This is right. Her presence is the pencil hand, the statue, the
glass. No one should ever meet her.

## Dramaturgy

**C8. The acts had no hinge.** The shape was episodic: twelve claims then a finale. The hinge is **Candlewick** (night of day 3): the first time the city
does something to the company's *conscience*. *Done:* the three Appointed Nights are real clocks with real, different resolutions, and the Great Tithe is
now a set piece with five ways round it, one of them a fight. *Missing:* the company should *feel* the second act turn — the Slate's tone changes
(it does: the news columns acquire a single word, "regrets") — but also the people it met in act one should *come to it*: Tolliver with a loaf, Hob at the door. That is
in the afterwords but could come earlier, as a 'visit' on day 5 from whichever small cases went well.

**C9. Consequences were silent.** A player who finished the Bakery heard nothing of it again. *Done:* `afterwords.kyr` — twelve chapters, opening in the last minutes before
midnight, one thread each, conditioned on what the company did. The ending closes on the city, not the Hall.

**C10. The ending texts are static.** Nine endings, each a paragraph. The paragraph cannot say *which* of the small things went right. The afterwords carry that. The
epilogues should still name the casualties (`{casualties}`) — not done.

## Playability

**C11. Is it playable as written?** Mostly. Tested: each case's doors; the Descent end to end by a plain party (it **does not suffice**: three hours' sitting is accord 3 of 5, so a
company that merely sits ends in *The Controlled Loss* — an honest and slightly cruel result that the page should foreshadow better: the Fire's
"You are late" should mean *you have an hour*); the Hearing with and without the company; the Arch five ways. Not tested end to end by a human: the **civic** finale
(six coals across a city in a day and a half). The fuzz found no invariant violations in about 900 random offered actions (8 walks); it did find that the company can start a fight
with a Phoenix warden or Skelter and die, which is correct.

**C12. Seed-lottery tests.** Many checks are pinned to a seed; any change to earlier steps changes the die. This is a property of Kyrie's identity-keyed randomness,
not an error, but a mortal test suite for a 9-day adventure is brittle. Recorded as friction.

## If the ambition were ten times

1. **A ninth act: the Reckoning procession as a playable street.** The five floats of the pageant are all in place; make each a small encounter: the Phoenix's gilt bird is
   carried by cadets who have been told to put it out of its misery; the Lamprey's fish has a boy in its mouth who is Kitty's brother. The company can ride a float to the Hall.
2. **The Burning as a level.** Not a vision: a playable, one-hour, three-room scene, "The Exchange, ninety-four years ago", entered through the Founders' Glass at dusk,
   in which the company is *Honora's clerks* and gets to draft one clause. What they draft changes the Treaty's text in the Strongroom (the Descent author made clause
   reading a game; this makes writing one).
3. **The Offices as places.** Each Office has a *character*: the Lamprey's rooms smell of wet paper, the Sovereign's are hung with sunbursts, the Providence's with falling men. Give each a floor to explore, a clerk to follow, and a secret.
4. **A fourth principal approach: the Slate.** Take Prewitt's place. A company with the Phoenix's confidence, a writ and a seat could *run* the Appointed Losses — and choose, each year, whom to spare. A terrible and fascinating game.
5. **Weather.** The city has none. Smog days that hide the Receipt; a rain on the sixth that puts out every unlit hearth in the Wards and makes the Fire's thirst audible.
6. **A real second city.** Overbridge, across the river, uninsured by custom, where the Fire's cousins sit.

## What I am still not 100% happy with

- The **Hearing** is the weakest set piece: it is a list of actions in a room, not a scene. The Chairmen have no faces. It deserves the care Mark Street got.
- **Companions** are a means: Tobias, Pettifer, Ottoline mainly fill skills. They should disagree with the company about something.
- Several quests rest on a **single check** with one result paragraph (Ouseley's persuasion, the Gasworks keep-place). Honest, but thin.
- **Combat is rarer than the brief implies** and mostly avoidable. That suits the book, but a player who wants a battle gets Skell's sons, a pair of ambushes and the Tithe men.
- The **Sunk City** has beautiful pieces and a short middle: it wants a fourth gallery.
