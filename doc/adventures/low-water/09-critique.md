# Low Water: a critical reading

*2026-10-26. A literary-analytic pass over everything built through [08](08-second-pass-built.md), read as a player
would read it (from the transcripts) and as an editor reads a manuscript. Each point names its passage. What was done
about each is in [10](10-final-revision.md).*

## What the work is actually about

Under the plot, Low Water is about **accounting as a moral act**. Its one maxim is carved over the Office door:
WHAT IS MEASURED HONESTLY CAN BE SETTLED PEACEFULLY. Isaura's gloss, *Honestly is the hard word. Most people think
it's measured*, is the best line in it. Every faction measures:

* the Guild in red ink;
* the Cantor in verses;
* the Ward in painted spans on its own landing;
* the Reckoner in ninths.

The tragedy of Act I is that all of them measured honestly, and the city still drains. The second pass added a
creditor with a just claim. In the right hands this is the culmination of the theme: the last measurer is the one to
whom the account was owed, and honesty toward it would destroy the city. The climax then asks Isaura to lie *with a
number*.

The theme has genuine depth. It does not yet have a **form**. It is stated and argued, mostly in Isaura's
conversations, but nothing in the structure enacts it. The second pass's best idea, the mirror, sits on top of it
like a costume: left-for-right figures and a copied city, spooky but unconnected to accounting. The two have to be
made one thing.

## The form that was missing: double entry

A ledger entered by double entry writes every figure twice: once on the side it left, once on the side it arrived.
If the sides disagree, someone is lying or someone is wrong. That *is* a mirror, and it is the Office's own craft.

Isaura's forty missing ells are a figure entered on one side with no entry on the other. The Interval is the other
side of the Bowl's book. The jars of counted water are the credit column. The copied city is the Bowl entered again,
reversed, in the creditor's hand. Once the work knows this, every doubling it has already made becomes part of one
design:

* the Reflected;
* the doubles of the company;
* Aun's image singing backward;
* the ledger of water;
* the Assayers' level beams;
* the Equals.

The design also offers more doubles than the work has used. Isaura and Dov, Office and Ward, one sibling on each
side of the counter. Halm and Pell, the Reader and the Second Reader. Orr and Aun: the Cantor who cut the Fourth and
the Lector who died keeping it. The Auditor and the Office itself.

**This is the main revision.** Without it the second pass is a better adventure and a worse book.

## What I am not happy with

### 1. The Auditor shouts

`citadel.kyr` gives the Auditor's speech in capitals (YOU ARE ON THE ACCOUNT. SIT, IF YOU LIKE.). It is a borrowed
horror convention, the voice of Death in a comic novel, and it is the most pulp thing in the work. It also breaks a
rule the work had kept until then: **capitals are for cut letters**. The motto over the door, the arch, the dial
(OWED) and the citadel's dome inscription are all in capitals. A voice in capitals reads as one more inscription,
which is half right, but the half that is wrong is loud.

There is a better idea in the material. The Interval hears whatever is said at the water's edge, and Isaura's
grandmother lived through the Thirst Riots, after which *the Office measured every right in the Bowl and read them
out in the square, every one, for a month*. **The Auditor learned to speak from the Office.** Its register should be
the counter's: *Received. Noted. That is reasonable.* It believes the motto, because the motto is the first sentence
it ever understood. So it is Isaura's double in vocation. Her lie in front of it then costs both of them something,
and its last line, *That is not what I counted*, becomes grief, not menace.

### 2. The Auditor explains too much

The cosmology is told three times: Halm at the ninth wheel, the model of the nine circles, and four of the Auditor's
six topics. Telling it three times makes it smaller each time. An auditor should **ask**, not lecture. It should make
the player say what the Ward is worth, which is the question the Hearing asked in Act IV, now asked by the creditor.
Its menace (*IT WILL BE THE LAST THING ANYBODY TELLS HIM*) is a villain's line. The Auditor is not a villain, and the
line should go.

### 3. A simile used three times

*The way you would practise a word in a language you meant to speak well* (Aun's image). *The way you would look at a
word you meant to learn* (the doubles). *The way you would practise a word…* again (the Mere standing up). Used once
it is a good image of the Auditor's patience. Used three times it is a tic.

### 4. The Mere stands up as a giant woman

A woman of water as tall as the Sounding, singing the Long Hymn backward: spectacle, of the kind the OSR welcomes. But
it latches onto nothing and reads as a monster-movie set piece. There is a quieter, more frightening version already
in the text. Isaura says *I learned to swim off the fourth step*. The mirror city's Mere stands *to the fourth step
of the Ward landing*. So when the Mere stands up, it should come back, in one night, to the fourth step, exactly where
it was when Isaura was a girl, copied. Every face in it looks up. In the morning it is gone. The adversary is not
showing strength. It is showing that it *remembers the Bowl better than the Bowl does*.

### 5. No foreshadowing, so the revelations are announced, not discovered

Acts I to IV contain no hint of the cosmology of Acts V and VI. The Bowl is a dent made by a falling heart, but the
Rim has no impact glass, Halm never wonders what made the crater, and nobody in the Ward has a custom about mirrors.
The cold of the Reflected appears from nowhere. One reference is broken outright: Aun's image has *the face carved
over the Lector's bench*, and there is no Lector's bench in the real Sounding.

### 6. The second pass has no danger worth the word

The Reflected are a 1d6 chill. The Assayers are peaceable. The doubles walk away and do nothing. The Equals remain
the only fight in the work, and they are an Act III fight. The brief for an OSR module is dangerous combat with ways
around it, and the second pass offers neither.

### 7. After the count closes, the player isn't told what is at stake or how to act

The heart is taken in the night, invisibly. A player who has not been to the ninth wheel has no idea a clock has
started, or that the bell could stop it. Only one tool works, the bell. A company that sang the Seventh at the
Reflected all through Act II cannot use that skill here.

### 8. Arcs that stop

* **Isaura.** Her arc is the work's spine and it is strong.
* **Halm.** Turned by Pell's cipher, then nothing: he never learns what his patron was writing to.
* **Orr.** She never learns that the verse she cut is the one Aun died keeping. The marked hymnal makes her a dupe,
  not a tragic figure.
* **Dov.** Has his doubles. Annet Kettle, if the diggers drowned, is a face in the Reflected and a jar in the
  receipts, and the player can do nothing for her.
* **Pell.** Exists only in footnotes. That is right; he should not appear. But his correspondence should exist
  somewhere physically.

### 9. The endings all end on the tally

Seventeen endings, and most close on Isaura entering something in the tally. Read together this is a refrain, and I
think it is the right one: every ending is literally an entry in the book. It needs to be *meant*, so the entries
should differ (Returned to Owner, crossed out to Claimant; Referred; Removed, Pending). They mostly do. I keep it.

## What I am happy with

* Act I's prose: plain, exact, funny without winking (*the cake is better when she's frightened*). It is the register
  the whole work should return to after the citadel.
* The hymn as an operating program. The Dyers' Basin as a puzzle that teaches the water. The Lock-Rhyme.
* The hall of receipts: every cupful kept, a face in each, every face looking down. It is the best image of the second
  pass, and it is about accounting.
* The model of the nine circles: the Bowl seen from above as a dent. Once foreshadowed, it can carry the reveal.
* The False Measure: the right end for Isaura. The question is whether the player will understand what it costs, and
  that depends on point 1.

## The revisions this calls for

1. **Double entry as the governing form.**
   * Isaura explains the Office's double entry early.
   * The forty ells become *the figure with no other side*.
   * In the Interval she finds the other side.
   * The real Office has a black glass on its far wall that the clerks read the motto in, so the mirror Office's
     reversed letters are an echo, not an invention.
2. **The Auditor's voice rewritten** in the Office's register. It learned to speak from the Thirst Riots readings, it
   asks the player to value the Ward, and the explanation is cut.
3. **Foreshadowing.**
   * Mere-tears (green impact glass) in the Rim's dust.
   * Halm on what made the Bowl, *not fire from below*.
   * The Ward's custom of turning mirrors to the wall at night, *so the water can't learn your face*.
   * Your reflection in the Mere lagging at dusk.
   * Frost on the fountain in summer.
   * Wet prints on the lock steps in Piet's book.
   * The Lector's bench and its carved face, in the real Sounding.
4. **Danger with ways around it.**
   * **The doubles** come up into the Ward at night once loosed: four of them, the company's own shape, hostile. You
     can fight them, sing the Seventh, ring the bell, have Isaura measure them (a reflection measured honestly reads
     left for right, and lies down), or stay off the landing after dark, as Dov tells you.
   * **The Assayers** rise if you break a jar in the hall of receipts. You can fight them, or make the account equal:
     a cup of the still water, a weight-stone in the pan, or Isaura entering the debit.
5. **Annet's jar.** If the diggers drowned, her jar can be broken and she let go. That is the jar that wakes the
   Assayers.
6. **After the count.** Frost in wet prints going down the landing by day. Isaura and Dov both read them. The
   Seventh, the Reflected's old enemy, can also win the heart back.
7. **Arcs closed.**
   * Orr learns about Aun.
   * Halm learns what the Second Reader was writing to, and offers to write Pell one false footnote, his own small
     double of Isaura's lie.
   * Pell's twenty years of letters lie wet beside the glass chair.
8. **Depth.**
   * Jars labelled by year: the Thirst Riots, the Long Silence, *the year of the white Mere*.
   * The eight dark pins in the model, each under somewhere named and never visited.
   * The Collegium's other instruments.
9. **The Mere stands up to the fourth step**, and the simile is used once.
