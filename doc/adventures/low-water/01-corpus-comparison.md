## Where Low Water's first conception collided with them

I had read none of these when I wrote the conception. Reading them, several of my
surface images turn out to be already in the corpus:

1. **The Long Man** is a lake held by ancient water-works, with a drowned town of the
   builders revealed when the water is drawn down, gauge-notches cut at every depth, a
   priest called *Mother* Ysolt, an old woman called *Gammer* Coller who keeps a
   practical rod, and a dyke-reeve's tool that is a key. My conception has a lake, a
   drowned Aenean ward, gauge-posts, *Mother* Cantor Orr, *Gammer* Lusk with her rhyme.
   That is too close in its furniture, though the situation is different.
2. **The Patient Star** has a Sluice with three consequences, Outfall eels, "the
   Tally", "the Stair That Counts", and a six-seat Concord voted on. My conception has
   lake-mothers (eels), Tallymen, and a Council.
3. **Let One Remember** has a Registry that "works the sum nobody has asked it for".
   My Reckoner computes.
4. **The House That Grows** is a crater-ring town. Ammerlin is a crater city.
5. **Thrume** has a killing-gauntlet tomb in the Tomb of Horrors tradition, with a
   doctrine cut over every trap. My Undercroft was described "in the Tomb tradition".

## What Low Water does that none of them does

These are what the comparison says to keep, and to build the adventure around:

* **A profession and a finding.** The player is not a hired sword or a returning
  child; they are the *Independent Assessor*, appointed because no one in the city can
  be neutral, and the climax is the delivery of a formal Finding at a hearing — an
  investigation that ends in testimony, where what you can *show* and who will *stand
  with you* decide what the city does. No other adventure here ends in an argued
  verdict. (The Patient Star's Concord is a vote among seats moved by evidence; the
  Finding is a single document the player writes, item by item, with the city's
  response following from what it contains.)
* **Water as a conserved quantity, as the puzzle language of a whole city.** The Long
  Man has one master wheel that draws one lake down. Low Water has a network of
  basins and gates where water is *moved, never made* — a level-and-flow physics the
  player learns on the Dyers' Terrace and applies at the Ninth Lock, in the intake
  culvert, and under the Mere. The rule's single exception (water that goes *nowhere*)
  is itself the central clue, and the Office's ledger is a puzzle instrument.
* **Music as the operating program of a city.** The hymn is the city's clock; the
  lock-keepers open and close by its verses. The Short Hymn reform is a liturgical
  change with hydraulic consequences, made by a good person for good reasons. Learning
  the hymn's notation is learning to speak to the engine — a compositional code, not
  a password: the player can say things to the Reckoner that no one has ever sung.
* **No villain and no monster gate.** Every party has an intelligible commitment and
  is partly right. Danger is social (bailiffs, a riot, a breach), hydraulic (pressure,
  flooding, a culvert that fills when a gate opens) and mechanical (wardens with one
  rule). Nothing must be killed.
* **A companion whose faith is an institution's.** Isaura Vant believes that what is
  measured honestly can be settled peacefully. Her brother is on the other side of the
  water. Her arc is whether measurement can bear what it is asked to carry.

## Revisions to the causal model, from thinking it through again

The conception had the Deep Sluice closed by a keeper at the Seventh Remainder. Its
weakness: a keeper who saw the sluice never close for three years would simply close
it. The revision makes the hymn *literally* reach the engine:

* The **Sounding** stands over the head of the Bowl's great cistern, which is the top of
  the engine's listening shaft. The choir's tones go down the water. The **Deep Gate**
  at the Ninth Lock is a bronze shutter with no wheel and no chain: the engine moves it
  itself, by what it hears.
* Piet Harl's charge, in the Lock-Rhyme, is to *watch*: "at the Sixth the Deep goes up,
  at the Seventh the Deep comes down; if it does not, send to the Sounding." He has
  sent. Twice. They thought he was asking a theological question.
* So the Seventh Remainder is not a cue for a man. It is a command to a machine, and
  the Aeneans who sang it, and the Ammerliners who inherited the singing, never knew.

This removes the keeper's implausible inaction, puts the city's music in direct
contact with the engine, and gives the player four physically different levers on the
same gate (sing the Seventh in the Sounding; sound it at the listening column; jam the
gate with the lock's stop-logs; or feed the engine from elsewhere so the gate's being
open no longer drains the Mere).
