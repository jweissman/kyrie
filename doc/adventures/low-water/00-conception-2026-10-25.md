# Low Water — the first conception

*Written 2026-10-25, before reading any Kyr syntax, engine source, test fixture, or
existing adventure. The only things known at the time of writing were this
commission's brief, its Imbar lore, and the names of four existing adventures
(The Long Man, Thrume, The House That Grows, Let One Remember). Everything here is
what the adventure wanted to be before it learned what the engine finds easy.*

*Later documents record where and why this conception changed. This one is not
edited after the fact except to fix typos.*

---

## 1. Five premises, considered

### A. The Toll of Ninefold Bridge
A bridge-city spanning a chasm, built in the Mathematical Age. Its toll-houses
count crossings and the count is a computation nobody remembers commissioning.
The bridge is sagging. A bounded structure of great internal depth: piers going
down into the dark, a guild of toll-keepers, a smugglers' route through the
trusses.

*Strengths:* vertical topology, a single structure that could rival a continent,
a clear civic institution. *Weaknesses:* the bridge's problem is structural and
the drama tends toward "fix the bridge or it falls" — a countdown rather than a
situation. The chasm below wants to be an abyss, and "descend into the abyss" is
an obvious shape.

### B. The Assay Office
An institution that certifies the prima materium content of goods — and, quietly,
of people. A scandal of counterfeit souls: someone is passing people whose bodies
are bound to less than they should be. An urban investigation with real
philosophical bite (what is a person worth; who measures). *Weaknesses:* the
premise is mostly talk; the exploration and puzzle half would be thin, and its
central horror risks being one idea stretched across an adventure.

### C. The Lighthouse Without a Sea
A lighthouse in a desert that once guided ships across a planar sea now dry. Its
keeper's family has kept the lamp lit for nine hundred years for no ship. Lovely,
elegiac, small. *Weaknesses:* elegy is not momentum. It is a wonderful *episode*
— and may survive as an optional discovery somewhere.

### D. The Choir of Hollow Vell
A mining town whose collective life is organized by a choir: the hymns are timed
to the shifts, and the resonance of the hymn is believed to hold up the galleries.
Music as psychic and collective organization. *Strengths:* a genuinely unusual
institution; real intellectual material (what does a song *do*?).
*Weaknesses:* "the song holds up the mine" is a single binary — sing or collapse.

### E. Low Water at Ammerlin
A terraced city built down the inside of an ancient crater, around a lake at its
floor. Its life is carried by the **Stair**: Mathematical Age cisterns, aqueducts,
locks and sluices that move water up and down the terraces. Water is money; an
**Office of Measures** keeps the ledgers of water rights. **The lake is falling.**
It has fallen for three years. Nobody knows why, everybody has a theory, and every
theory is also a claim on somebody else's water.

*Strengths:* it contains B's institution (an office that measures), D's choir
(music as the city's clock), A's single deep structure (the Stair and what lies
under the lake), and C's elegy (the drowned Aenean ward coming up into the air as
the water goes). It has a **physical system the player can reason about** —
water seeks its level, water is conserved, water turns mills and floats boats and
drowns doors — which makes puzzles fair and transferable. And it has a *situation*
rather than a countdown: many parties acting on one shared, finite thing, each
for intelligible reasons, each partly right.

**Selected: E**, absorbing D (the choir), B (the ethic of measurement) and a
trace of C.

## 2. The central situation

### What the city believes

Ammerlin stands in the **Bowl**, a crater four terraces deep. The **Ninefold** —
a river from the northern hills — enters at the rim and is carried down the
terraces through the Stair: the **Nine Locks**, three great cisterns, a hundred
lesser sluices. At the bottom is the **Mere**, the lake. The Mere has always been
there; the Choristers say it is where the dead go, each soul a drop.

Water rights are measured in **ells-per-day** and recorded by the Office of
Measures, founded two hundred years ago after the Thirst Riots, on a principle cut
over its door: **WHAT IS MEASURED HONESTLY CAN BE SETTLED PEACEFULLY.**

The city's day is kept by the **Choristers of the Sounding**, whose bells and
hymns tell the lock-keepers when to open and close. Nobody in Ammerlin thinks of
this as engineering. It is simply how the day goes: at the Third Verse the Dyers'
sluice opens; at the Long Amen the mill-races close.

### What is actually happening

Three changes, all made by sensible people for sensible reasons, compound.

1. **The Short Hymn.** Three years ago Mother Cantor **Sabeth Orr** reformed the
   liturgy. The Long Hymn — two hours at dusk — was emptying the Sounding; the
   young would not stand for it. She cut it to forty minutes, removing
   repetitions she judged to be later accretions. One of the verses she cut,
   the *Seventh Remainder*, is the verse at which, for three thousand years, the
   keeper of the lowest lock closed the **Deep Sluice**. Nobody knew that verse
   closed anything. The keeper simply closed the sluice when the verse was sung;
   now the verse is not sung, so the keeper (a dutiful young man named Piet
   Harl, three years in the post) does not close it. *He was taught: open at the
   Sixth, close at the Seventh.* He has written to the Sounding twice asking
   when the Seventh is sung now. Nobody answered; they thought he was asking a
   theological question.

2. **The Fulling Channel.** Two years ago the **Millwrights' Guild**, under its
   Master **Harrow Quill**, diverted a fifth of the Ninefold's upper flow into a
   new channel to drive fulling mills on the Rim Terrace. This was legal: the
   Guild bought the rights from failing farms. It reduces inflow to the Mere. It
   is real, measurable, and *not the main cause* — but it is the cause everyone
   can see, so it is the cause everyone argues about.

3. **The Reckoner wakes.** Beneath the Mere, under the drowned Aenean ward, under
   the bedrock, lies a hydraulic calculating engine of the Mathematical Age: the
   **Reckoner**. It runs on water — every operation passes a measured quantity
   through a valve and *out of the world*: the spent water goes through a
   crossing into somewhere else (where, is one of the adventure's open
   questions). For three thousand years the Deep Sluice fed it a cupful a day.
   It computed slowly. Since the Short Hymn, the Deep Sluice stands open. The
   Reckoner is drinking the lake, and computing faster than it has since it was
   built. It is close to finishing.

The *Aeneans* — who built the drowned ward and its temples over the Reckoner's
intake — did not build the engine. They found it and worshipped its outputs.
**The Long Hymn is a transcription of the Reckoner's output.** The engine's
results were expressed as tones — a resonating column of water sounding notes —
and the Aeneans sang them back as praise. The Seventh Remainder was the
engine's own instruction: *throttle the intake*. The Mathematical Age engineers
had built the instruction into the output so that whoever was listening would
keep it fed but not flooded. The Aeneans obeyed it as liturgy. Ammerlin
inherited the liturgy. Mother Orr cut it as an accretion.

### What the Reckoner is computing

The engine was asked a question. The adventure lets the player approach the
question through inscriptions, the hymn, the engine's own architecture, and the
arguments of three people who want different answers.

What the inscriptions say (in Aenean, which mistranslated an older notation):
*"It counts what is bound."* The prima materium binds souls to bodies. The
engineers asked the engine to count it: **a census of everything that is, was,
or will be bound** — whether the bound is finite, and if so, how much.

The adventure does not decide whether the count is literally a census of souls.
Halm, the scholar, believes it is a proof — a mathematical object with no
theological content. Mother Orr believes the lake was made with exactly as many
drops as souls, and the engine counts them as they pass. Isaura, the clerk,
believes it is a *ledger* — and that a ledger is an instrument of settlement,
which means somebody intended to settle something with it. Each reading has
evidence. The game's ending is not the revelation of which is correct.

### The crossing

When a computation completes, the engine sends its remainder — the final spent
water and the result-tone — through the crossing. The crossing is a door in the
engine's lowest chamber. The Mathematical Age meant the answer to be *received*.
By whom: the Virina? the planar powers whose settlement the prima materium
underwrites? nobody? The adventure offers, at its deepest optional point, a short
passage through the crossing into the **Interval**, a place of perfectly still
water under no sky, where the water of three thousand years of computation lies,
and something there has been reading it. The player does not meet a god. The
player finds that the water *has been used* — that out there, the spent water
has been made into something (terraces? a slow-growing glass? a reflection of
Ammerlin, built by someone who has only ever seen it in water?). Left open.

## 3. The principal people

For each: what they believe, want, can do, know, misunderstand, fear; what they
are doing before the player arrives; what they do if left alone.

### Isaura Vant — Clerk-Measurer, Third Grade, Office of Measures
The companion. Thirty-four. Assigned by the Office to accompany the player — an
outsider hired as *Independent Assessor* because no Ammerliner can be neutral
about water. She carries a brass ell-rod, a ledger, a stub of graphite, and a
sense of humour so dry the player may not notice it for an hour.

* **Believes:** the falling Mere has a cause, the cause can be measured, and
  once measured it can be settled. The principle over the Office door is not a
  slogan to her; it is the reason her grandmother did not die in the Thirst
  Riots.
* **Wants:** an honest finding, delivered in time to matter.
* **Can do:** read any ledger in the city, recite rights by terrace, measure a
  flow by eye to a tenth of an ell, open Office-sealed sluices with her warrant,
  stand perfectly still in an argument until the other person runs out.
* **Misunderstands:** that measurement is neutral. She will learn that choosing
  *what* to measure is already a judgement, and that some things the city must
  decide — whose need counts — no ledger settles.
* **Fears:** that the Office is a costume over force. That her brother is right
  about her.
* **Before the player:** she has been keeping a private tally — the Office's
  official inflow-outflow balance does not close. She has a discrepancy of
  forty ells a day she cannot account for and has told no one, because the
  Registrar would make her find a culprit.
* **If left alone:** she would deliver her discrepancy to the Registrar, be told
  to attribute it to the Guild, and do so. She would be wrong and she would know
  it.
* **Voice:** precise, short sentences, units. *"That's an opinion with a number
  in it. I'd like the number without the opinion."* Says "noted" when she
  disagrees. Jokes by understatement. When moved, becomes more precise, not
  less.

### Dov Vant — fisher of the Mere Ward, her brother
* **Believes:** the city above is letting the Ward die because the Ward is poor.
  The lake falling is a theft and the thieves have names.
* **Wants:** his people kept together; his boat afloat; the dignity of not
  moving up to work in Quill's mills.
* **Can do:** knows every reed-channel, every drowned roof now surfacing, every
  rumour. Commands the loyalty of forty diggers. Has a boat (the *Tench*) and a
  crew. Has started digging into the exposed Aenean ruins and selling what he
  finds — the only income left.
* **Misunderstands:** that the ruins are just loot. They are the Reckoner's
  intake works, and his diggers are close to breaching something.
* **Fears:** becoming a labourer for Quill. Losing his sister to the Office.
* **If left alone:** sells the Aenean finds to Halm and Quill; his diggers breach
  the Undercroft and three are drowned when the water goes the wrong way; he
  blames the Office.
* **Voice:** loud, warm, profane in the Ward way (fish-oaths), quick to laugh and
  quick to quarrel. Calls Isaura "Measure."

### Harrow Quill — Master of the Millwrights' Guild
* **Believes:** water should go where it does the most good, and the most good is
  measurable in cloth, wages, and bread. The Mere is a beautiful waste. The Ward
  is a beautiful poverty. The city has to choose growth or nostalgia.
* **Wants:** the Fulling Channel kept; the Guild's charter renewed at the coming
  Council; the Mere Ward's exposed land, which he is quietly buying through
  agents, because the ruins are worth more than the fish.
* **Can do:** money; lawyers; a gang of "bailiffs"; an excellent rhetorical
  education; the ear of half the Council.
* **Knows:** the Channel is not enough to explain the fall — his own engineers
  told him. He has not said so, because the confusion is useful.
* **Misunderstands:** he thinks the fall will stop on its own once the drought in
  the hills breaks. There is no drought in the hills.
* **Fears:** that the Guild's debts become known — he overbuilt the fulling mills
  and the charter's renewal is all that keeps the creditors back.
* **If left alone:** his agents buy the Ward; his bailiffs clear the diggers; his
  argument wins the Council; the Mere falls anyway.
* **Voice:** gracious, quotable, generous with concessions he does not mean. A
  sophist in the precise sense — he can argue either side and enjoys doing so,
  and once in a while the argument carries him somewhere true.

### Mother Cantor Sabeth Orr — the Sounding
* **Believes:** music is how a city becomes one thing out of many people. The
  Long Hymn had become a monument nobody entered. She shortened it so the young
  would sing again — and they do; attendance has tripled.
* **Wants:** her reform defended; her singers kept; the hymn alive.
* **Can do:** command the bells (and therefore, unknowingly, the timing of every
  lock in the city); read the old notation better than anyone living; sing a
  tone that makes a water-glass ring across a room.
* **Misunderstands:** that the Long Hymn's repetitions were accretions. They were
  numbers.
* **Fears:** that she was wrong in a way that killed something. That faith is
  only a habit of obedience to a forgotten machine.
* **If left alone:** defends her reform at the Council against the
  traditionalists; wins; the Mere falls.
* **Voice:** seventy-one, funny, profane in a churchly way, impatient with
  piety, fiercely serious about music. *"A hymn isn't a prayer, child. A prayer
  is a letter. A hymn is a building everybody stands in at once."*

### Teodor Halm — Fellow of the Collegium of Lesser Instruments
The visiting scholar from outside — and the adventure's public door into the
wider world (the Collegium is a society with interests in every ancient
machine on Imbar).
* **Believes:** the Mathematical Age solved problems we have not learned to
  state. Any working machine of theirs is worth more than any city.
* **Wants:** the Reckoner to finish. He will help anyone who helps that.
* **Can do:** read Mathematical notation (badly, but better than anyone in
  Ammerlin); buy Dov's finds; write letters to the Collegium that bring more
  scholars, or soldiers.
* **Misunderstands:** people. He is not cruel; he simply does not weigh them.
* **Voice:** delighted, tangential, quick, endlessly footnoted. The adventure's
  best jokes and its coldest moment both belong to him.

### Registrar Odile Fenn — Head of the Office of Measures
Wants a finding the Council will accept before the charter vote. Not corrupt;
tired, cornered, practical. The player's employer. Her pressure is the clock.

### Piet Harl — Keeper of the Ninth Lock
Twenty. The most important man in Ammerlin and has no idea. The Deep Sluice is in
his charge. He has been waiting three years for the Seventh Remainder to be
sung. Finding Piet and his unanswered letters is the adventure's first great
hinge.

### Gammer Lusk — retired lock-keeper
Keeps the *Lock-Rhyme*, the children's mnemonic every keeper learns: which
verse opens which sluice. She is the practical half of the hymn. With her rhyme
and Mother Orr's notation, the player can understand the hymn as a program.

### The Reckoner
Not a character with a voice — a character with *behaviour*. It responds to
water, tone, and balance. It has wardens (the **Tallymen**: bronze automata
whose single rule is *restore balance* — remove any weight that would tip a
reckoning). It can be throttled, starved, fed, flooded, or broken. It
*answers*: a tone sounded into its listening-column in the old notation
produces a reply in tones. Learning to ask it questions is the adventure's
deepest puzzle.

## 4. The causal model (what moves when nothing is done)

The world has a small number of stocks and flows the adventure tracks:

* **Mere level** — falls one *mark* every few days while the Deep Sluice is open
  and the Channel is diverted; falls more slowly if either is fixed; rises only
  if both are.
* **Reckoner progress** — rises each day the Deep Sluice is open; completes at a
  threshold. At completion: the final hymn sounds through the Bowl (every
  bell in the city rings by itself), the crossing opens, the Mere drops sharply
  one last time.
* **Ward tenure** — Quill's agents' purchases; falls if Dov's people are cleared
  or sell.
* **Council date** — fixed; the Finding must be delivered by then, or the
  Registrar delivers one herself (blaming the Guild, which wins on rhetoric).
* **Relationships** — Isaura's trust; Dov's trust in Isaura; Quill's regard;
  Mother Orr's faith/doubt.

Thresholds produce events: at Mere *Mark 3* the Ward's last landing is dry and
Dov's boat is stranded; at *Mark 4* the Undercroft roof is exposed and Dov's
diggers breach (disaster unless prevented); at *Mark 5* the Aenean Temple of
the Measure stands in the open air.

A player who delays: the Ward is lost; the Council hears the Registrar; the
Reckoner finishes alone; the crossing opens beneath an empty lakebed. A player
who returns *later* finds Ammerlin altered: a drained bowl, Quill's ruin-mines,
the Sounding half-empty, and Isaura either gone from the Office or its new
Registrar. The adventure remains playable from there — the Interval is still
reachable, the engine still answers.

## 5. Dramatic composition

### Movement I — *The Measure*
Arrival at the rim. The city seen whole: terraces, the Stair, the Mere far below
like a coin. The Office hires you. Isaura. The first walk down the Stair: learn
the city, learn the locks (first water puzzle — float a lock-barge to cross a
cistern). The four theories: Quill's drought, the Ward's theft, the
traditionalists' heresy, the Office's culprit. The ledgers do not close.

*Turn:* Piet Harl's letters. The Seventh Remainder. The Short Hymn closed nothing
— *it stopped closing something*. Mother Orr is suddenly implicated, and the
player's sympathy (she is wonderful) is suddenly costly.

### Movement II — *The Drowned Ward*
The lakebed rises into the air. Descent into the Aenean ruins: drain-and-fill
puzzles that teach that the Stair's logic continues under the Mere; Aenean
inscriptions of the hymn in an older notation; Dov's diggers. The politics heat:
Quill's agents, the Council's summons, the Sounding split between reformers
and the old Cantors. Halm arrives at the dig with money.

*Turn:* Closing the Deep Sluice (the obvious fix, available now) slows the fall
— and the Reckoner responds: the water-clocks of the city run backwards for a
night, bells ring without ringers. The engine is *asking* to be fed. It is near
completion. The question is no longer "why is the lake falling" but "what do we
owe the thing under it, and what do we owe each other."

### Movement III — *The Reckoner*
The Undercroft and the engine: a bounded, intricate, dangerous structure in the
Tomb tradition. The Tallymen. The listening-column. The notation learned from
Gammer's rhyme and Orr's hymn and the Aenean walls — now used to *ask the
engine* what it is doing. Choices made here govern the outcome: throttle it,
feed it from the Fulling Channel (requires Quill), starve it, break it, or let it
finish.

### Movement IV — *The Finding*
The Council of Ammerlin. The player, as Independent Assessor, delivers the
Finding. Evidence matters (what you can show); relationships matter (who will
stand with you); what you did below matters most. Quill argues. Orr confesses or
doesn't. Dov's people are in the gallery. Isaura has to decide whether to sign.

### Coda — *The Interval* (optional)
If the Reckoner finished, the crossing is open. A short passage into the
Interval: still water, the spent water of three thousand years, and the
evidence that someone has been using it.

### Outcomes (not exhaustive, not ranked)
* **The Throttled Census** — the Seventh Remainder restored; the Mere recovers
  over years; the engine continues its count at its ancient pace; the Sounding
  takes custody with the Office; nobody now living will hear the answer.
* **The Fed Census** — the Fulling Channel redirected into the Deep Intake by
  agreement with Quill (who gets the Ward's ruins, or the charter); the engine
  finishes in days *without* draining the Mere; the mills idle; the answer
  sounds over the Bowl; Halm writes to the Collegium.
* **The Broken Engine** — the Tallymen fought or fooled, the engine's
  governor smashed; the Mere recovers fully; the hymn is only a hymn now; Orr's
  faith survives or doesn't; Halm never forgives you.
* **The Low Water** — the engine finishes on the lake; the Ward is lost; the
  answer is heard by a city that can no longer afford to listen.
* **The Measured Accord** — a new institution: the Office and the Sounding
  together keep the engine's ration as a *right* in the ledger, like any other
  water-user's; the engine becomes a citizen of the Bowl. Requires Isaura's
  conviction, Orr's consent, and evidence the Council believes.

## 6. Representative scenes (to test the premise)

### Scene: The Rim, first hour

> The road comes over the lip of the Bowl and stops, because the world does. Below you the city goes down in four great steps, each a ring of roofs and gardens and wet stone, and between the rings the water: falling, turning, held. You can hear it before you understand it — a sound like a thousand people breathing out. At the bottom, very far, the Mere lies like a coin somebody dropped. There is a pale ring around it. It takes you a moment to see that the ring is mud.
>
> A woman in a grey coat is standing at the head of the Stair with a brass rod under her arm, as if she had been there all morning and would be there all afternoon.
>
> "You're the Assessor." It is not a question. "Isaura Vant. Office of Measures, Third Grade. I'm to accompany you, record your findings, open what you need opened, and stop you being robbed. The Registrar would like your finding before the Council sits, which is in nine days." She glances down at the Mere. "It was at the fourth step of the Ward landing when I was a girl. It's below the eleventh now. I'll show you the eleventh. That's the kind of thing I'm for."

### Scene: Isaura and the discrepancy (once trust is earned)

> **ISAURA:** May I show you something that isn't in the official ledger?
> **PLAYER:** You keep a second ledger?
> **ISAURA:** I keep a *tally*. A ledger is what the Office believes. A tally is what I've counted. *(She opens a thin book. Columns, tiny, perfect.)* Inflow at the Rim Gauge, less the Fulling Channel. Less every right recorded on every terrace. Less evaporation, which I've measured with a pan on my roof for two summers and which my landlady thinks is witchcraft. What's left should arrive in the Mere. It doesn't. Forty ells a day don't arrive anywhere.
> **PLAYER:** Stolen?
> **ISAURA:** Forty ells is a river. You can't steal a river in a bucket and nobody's dug a second one. It isn't stolen. It's *missing*. *(Pause.)* Water doesn't go missing. That's the first thing they teach you. You can move it, spill it, drink it, sweat it out — every drop's somewhere. This is the only figure I've ever written that I can't put somewhere.
> **PLAYER:** Why haven't you told the Registrar?
> **ISAURA:** Because she'd tell me to put it on the Guild. And the Guild's channel is twenty-two ells, not forty, and I've measured it twice. *(She closes the book.)* I'd be writing a lie with a number in it. That's the worst kind. People believe the number.

### Scene: Mother Orr, before she knows

> **ORR:** You've come about the hymn. Everyone comes about the hymn. The old Cantors think I've cut the heart out of it. I've cut the *fat* out of it. Do you know what the Long Hymn was? Two hours. Seven Remainders, every one the same four bars, a hundred and twelve repetitions of a phrase nobody could tell apart. The young came once and never again.
> **PLAYER:** And now?
> **ORR:** Now they come. Three times as many. They *sing*, child. You should hear them — it's like standing inside a bell. *(She is pleased; it shows.)* A hymn isn't a prayer. A prayer's a letter. A hymn is a building everybody stands in at once. If nobody stands in it, it's a ruin, however holy the stones.
> **PLAYER:** What were the Remainders for?
> **ORR:** For? They weren't *for*. Some copyist in the Aenean days liked the phrase and wrote it seven times. Music accretes, like river silt. You dredge it now and again or the channel chokes.
>
> *(Later, after Piet's letters, the same question has another answer, and she knows it before you finish asking.)*
>
> **ORR:** Say it plainly. I'm seventy-one. I haven't time for you to be kind.
> **PLAYER:** The Seventh Remainder closed the Deep Sluice.
> **ORR:** *(A long time.)* Then it wasn't silt. *(She looks at her hands.)* Then somebody wrote it seven times because it was *meant* seven times. And I thought it was ugly. I thought it was the ugliest thing in the hymn, those four bars. Like somebody counting.

### Scene: Quill, in his counting-house

> **QUILL:** Sit, Assessor, sit. You'll want to hear my side and I'll want you to have heard it, so let's be efficient. Twenty-two ells. That's what my Channel takes. Your clerk will have told you, she measures everything, I admire her enormously. Twenty-two ells, bought fairly from farms that were dying, and with it four hundred people have work who had none. The Mere has fallen sixty ells' depth in three years. Do the sum. I am a sliver of your problem. I am the sliver you can *see*, and so I'm the sliver everyone would like to cut out.
> **PLAYER:** And the land you're buying in the Ward?
> **QUILL:** *(Delighted.)* Oh, good. You've done more than walk around. Yes. I'm buying it. Somebody has to. When the Mere's gone — and it's going, Assessor, it's been going since before either of us drew breath, it's just faster now — that land will be the richest in Ammerlin. There are Aenean temples under it. Do you want them looted by fishermen with shovels, or excavated properly, with wages?
> **PLAYER:** Those are the only choices?
> **QUILL:** They're the only ones anybody's paying for.

### Scene: Halm at the dig

> **HALM:** — and *this*, you see, isn't Aenean at all, look at the joins, the Aeneans couldn't cut a joint like that if you held a knife to their great-grandchildren, this is *older*, this is Mathematical, which means the temple was built *on* something, *over* something, the whole Ward is a lid — sorry. Sorry. I do this. Hello. Teodor Halm, Collegium of Lesser Instruments, lesser because the greater instruments are all broken, ha, that's the Collegium joke, there's only the one.
> **ISAURA:** He's been paying my brother for stones.
> **HALM:** I've been paying your brother *very well* for stones. Have you any idea what he's been digging through? Have you heard it? Put your ear to that wall. Go on.
> *(You do. Deep in the rock, slow, a sound like a single note held under water.)*
> **HALM:** Something down there is running. In three thousand years nobody's built a machine that runs a year without a man to oil it. And that has been running since before there were men here at all. *(Quietly, for once.)* I'd give the lake for it. I'm sorry. I would. I'd give the lake.

## 7. A fully specified puzzle: the Dyers' Basin

**Location.** The Dyers' Terrace has three water bodies connected by sluices:
the **Upper Cistern** (U), the **Dyers' Basin** (D), and the **Sump** (S) below.
Levels are counted in marks. U is at 3, D at 3, S at 1. D is 3 marks deep; on
its floor, under water, is an Aenean arch with a carved inscription (the second
fragment of the old notation) and the mouth of a passage downward.

**Underlying mechanism.** Two sluices: U→D (the *Upper Gate*) and D→S (the
*Sump Gate*). Opening a gate between two bodies lets water pass from the higher
level to the lower until they are equal, or until the lower is full. Water is
conserved (marks are not identical units of volume across bodies, but the
adventure simplifies: each body has a capacity and the marks transfer one-for-one).
S has capacity 4; D capacity 3; U capacity 5. The Sump also drains to the Ward
by a slow overflow: when S is at 4, any more water floods the dye-vats of the
Ward's widow **Mag Torrin**, ruining her season — a real cost, a person.

**What the player first perceives.** A full basin, a dark shape under the water,
two iron wheel-gates, a gauge-post in each body painted with marks, a dyer
complaining that she needs the basin full by the Long Amen for the blue.

**Evidence.** The gauge-posts (truthful level reports). The Lock-Rhyme's dyers'
couplet ("*Upper to Dyer, Dyer to Sump; / never the Sump when the Sump's at the
stump*"). Isaura can state capacities if asked. Mag Torrin says what happens if
the Sump overfills.

**Actions.** Open/close either gate. Bail by hand (one mark per long effort,
tiring). Ask Isaura to seal the Sump overflow with her warrant (closes the
Ward's overflow: no flood, but the Sump can then take no more and the Ward's
vats get *no* water that day — another cost to Mag). Return later after the
Long Amen when the dyers' sluice draws D down by one mark on schedule (the
hymn as clock: a player who understands the timing can wait for the city to do
part of the work).

**Solution space.** Naïvely opening the Sump Gate: D(3)→S(1) equalizes at 2/2 —
D still 2 deep. Opening again after S drains by its slow overflow drowns Mag's
vats. The elegant route: wait for the Long Amen (D drops to 2), *then* open the
Sump Gate (D2, S1 → can't equalize below... ) — the designer's job in
implementation is to tune numbers so that there are at least two fair solutions
(timing with the hymn; Isaura's seal plus bailing), one costly shortcut (flood
Mag), and one clever one (raise U first so the Upper Gate is a one-way spill
that... ). Partial understanding: drain partly and dive (dangerous; the arch is
read in glimpses; a half-reading).

**Relationships.** Teaches the conservation and level rules used throughout. The
inscription is one of three fragments needed to read the notation. Mag Torrin
remembers what you did to her vats and says so at the Council.

**State over time.** D refills from U at each dawn hymn. Drained basins stay
drained until the hymn refills them. If the Short Hymn is reformed back to the
Long Hymn, the dyers' timings change — a later return finds the puzzle shifted.

**Truthful interface.** Every body's mark is visible on its gauge-post at all
times; opening a gate reports the flow and the resulting levels; Isaura will
state the rule in plain words once you've seen it happen.

## 8. Tactical and dangerous play (intentions)

* **Quill's bailiffs** in the Ward: can be fought, bribed, outtalked (with the
  right document), or avoided by boat.
* **The Undercroft breach:** a flooding race — the water goes the wrong way
  when Dov's diggers break through; warning signs (the note in the wall, damp
  spreading) give a fair chance to prevent it.
* **Lake-mothers:** great eels in the shrinking pools of the drowned ward, more
  desperate as water falls. Better avoided than fought; can be fed.
* **The Tallymen:** bronze wardens whose single rule is *restore balance*. They
  are not hostile; they remove whatever tips a reckoning — including a person
  standing on a weigh-plate. Understood, they are a puzzle (balance the plate
  with stone; trick them into removing their own counterweights). Misunderstood,
  they are deadly.
* **Pressure and drowning:** opening the wrong valve in the engine floods a
  chamber; the player always sees the gauge first.
* **Expedition logistics:** lamps (oil), rope, a diving bell or bladders of air
  for the drowned galleries; supplies bought on the terraces.

## 9. Points of contact with the wider world

* **The Collegium of Lesser Instruments** — Halm's society; a public institution
  that can exist in any city, interested in any ancient machine. A natural
  export.
* **The Ninefold River** — flows from the north; a route.
* **The rumour of the Falling Mere** — spreads elsewhere.
* **A water-merchant or lock-barge** that could carry travellers to and from
  the Bowl.

## 10. What this adventure must not become

* Not a puzzle box with a city painted on the outside: the people must be acting.
* Not "sing the right song to win": the hymn is a key to understanding, not a
  password.
* Not a villain hunt: there is no villain; there is a shared thing and many
  hands on it.
* Not an abyss-descent with escalating monsters: the deepest place is the
  quietest.
