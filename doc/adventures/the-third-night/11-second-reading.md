# The Third Night: a second reading

Asked to read the finished module again, more severely and as literature, I re-read all 27,000 words of its prose
in order (every observation, topic, result and ending, extracted from the Kyr) and the seventeen transcripts. This
is that reading, written before the second revision; what the revision did is at the end.

## What is actually good

The register holds. The prose is plain, concrete and mostly restrained, closer to the ballad and to Hardy than to
pulp: a scrubbed step, a stopped clock nobody winds, a dog lying in the swept place where a cart stood, a boy's
boots with new laces. The best scenes are quiet ones: sitting by Kit on the ford-stone ("He used to throw me in off
this stone to teach me to swim… I can't swim"); Jennet's "Let the crows have him till the crows are sick", said
without raising her voice; Win counting the chisels with one finger. The mystery is honestly made: a reader who
looks at the cart sees that the blood is where a boy was lifted in.

So the risk is not, mostly, pulp exuberance. It is the opposite: a module that is tasteful, wry and *small*.

## What I am not happy with

**1. The central image is lying in pieces.** The story's real subject is carrying: who carries the dead over the
water, and who will not. It is everywhere and nowhere. The Company's motto is WE BEAR THE CROSSING, and the Company
bears nothing. Over the bridge chapel's altar a saint carries a child across a river, "looking worried"; in Saint
Wade's church the same saint looks cheerful. Wat lifted Ned into the cart "like a sack of something precious", and
once carried a calf over the ford round his neck like a collar. Kit carries what he did. And the whole night route
is carrying, one man at a time. None of these knows about the others. The saint is a painting; the motto is a joke;
the ford is a mechanism. Nobody in Wrayford tells you who Wade was or why the dead "go looking", so the custom on
which the whole deadline rests is a superstition with no content. This is the single largest failure, because it is
the difference between a situation and a story.

**2. Everyone is witty in the same way.** The working people of Wrayford (Pell, Dorcas, Corbin, Kemp, Quoin, Jack
Kemp, Goodwife Tull, Clout) all speak in the same shape: a dry line, a beat of business, a dry tag. "Bed, beer or
bread. Or trouble, but we're full up of that." "Prime chops. Or are you here about the hide?" "I'm a great believer in
both sides." Each is fine; together they are a sitcom's supporting cast. Nobody is inarticulate, frightening, coarse,
pious, mad or strange. The minor comic touches lean twee: a bear "smaller, more depressed" than the Overbridge bear;
Gidley "stole the communion wafers to sell to the tanners' children"; Lumley wiping out chins "as if he were drowning
kittens". These are the passages that read as exuberance without form: whimsy for its own sake.

**3. The dead are abstract.** Three shapes in sacking that "turn a little when the wind comes up the river". It is a
good image once. But it is high summer, they have hung four days, and on the third day nobody smells anything, no
crow is seen, nothing about the rail changes. The tragedy needs its body. Restraint that never lets the horror in is
not restraint, it is avoidance.

**4. The world stops at the edge of the map.** The lord has no name. Harbury is a word. Nothing happened in Wrayford
before four nights ago. The reeve's fear of a burning, which is his whole motive, is asserted, not grounded: he says
"three men on a rail, or thirty", but nobody remembers a time it was thirty. The captain's "Nobody likes a burning"
is a good line about nothing in particular. The illusion of depth that an OSR place needs (things referred to that
are bigger than the adventure) is almost absent.

**5. There is one tone, and no weather.** Every day of the four is the same day: hot, still, wry. The acts exist
in the city's clock, but the player does not feel a chapter turn. There is no rising pressure in the air, no
sound of the third night coming, no change of light. The third night itself, the title of the work, has no
atmosphere: it is a time window in which Maudie's rule fires.

**6. No danger the player can see coming.** The only violence is two fights that resolve inside a wait. The ford
is called dangerous, "a deep step that has drowned men", and the danger is a saving throw. The river, which is the
real antagonist of a city on a bridge, has no presence.

**7. Maudie has too few verbs** (as the first critique said), and the bread she cannot stop making is a symptom
that does nothing. It should be the counter-current to the city's ledgers: Pinch's tally, Inchbold's minutes, Pratt's
book, the captain's "ten for every rider", the farthing tally-stick. Bread is the one thing in Wrayford nobody counts.

**8. Foreshadowing is thin.** Hal's hand is on the decree from the start (good). But Pinch's pencil column, Kit's
black hands, the hymn Wat sang on the road, the mills that make the night ford, the captain's history: either absent
or mentioned once after the fact.

**9. The night is laborious.** Carrying three men by the ford is the same eight choices three times.

## Is there depth?

There is a real moral problem and real people, which is more than most adventures have. What it lacks is a *form*
for them: a myth the city lives inside, against which each character's choice means something. Wrayford has a saint
who carried the dead over the water, a Company that has taken his work for its motto and made a toll of it, a river
that drowns, and a custom that the unburied go looking. Assembled, that is the story: the dead are looking for
someone to carry them, and the city's whole economy is a refusal to. Every ending then becomes an answer to the
legend, and every small adventure a version of the same question (who carries what, for whom, and does anyone count
it).

## The plan

1. **Give Wade his legend**, and tell it in pieces across the city: the chapel, the church, the Abbess, Corbin, the
   badge-seller, Maudie at the vigil. Before the bridge, Wade kept the ford. On the third night after the great flood
   a drowned child came to his door asking to be carried over to be buried; at the deep place the child grew heavy as
   all the dead of the vale, and Wade stumbled and did not set him down. The deep place is **Wade's Step**. The dead
   who are not carried over by the third night go looking for someone who will. The hymn Wat sang on the road to the
   rope is the carters' hymn to Wade: *carry me over*.
2. **Make the river dangerous**, with a creature in the legend's place: the **Bargeman**, an old pike in the deep
   water at Wade's Step, that the drovers' cow goes round and Wat fed a loaf to the night he carried the calf. A fight
   that can kill a lone character, and five ways not to have it.
3. **Give Maudie's bread work to do**: she can be asked for bread to take into the city, and it opens what money
   cannot: the riders on the leads, Jennet's door, the river.
4. **Give the city a past**: the **Michaelmas Burning** thirty-one years ago, when an earlier captain hanged ten from
   the drawbridge chains and burned Water Lane in Overbridge; the reeve saw it as a boy, Corbin dug the graves, and
   the captain who comes now is that captain's daughter. Give the lord a name; give the night ford a cause
   (Alderman Dunch's mills, penning the river for the day's grinding).
5. **Let the dead and the weather change**: crows from the second day, the heat and the smell, thunder in the fells,
   the storm with the captain at dusk on the third day, rain on the third night, and a washed fourth morning. Mark
   each dawn as a chapter.
6. **Let the third night be strange**, without ever confirming anything: the dog, the candles, the river, people on
   the Wharf who say they heard wading.
7. **Trim the whimsy, vary the voices**: fewer quips, some people who are frightening or inarticulate or devout.
8. **Foreshadow**: Pinch's pencil, Kit's hands, the hymn, the mills, the captain's father.
9. **Shorten the carrying** once the way is known.
