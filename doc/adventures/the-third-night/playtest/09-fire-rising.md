## day1

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:30 a.m.*

You set out from the drove road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. Northgate is where the drove roads come into the city: a wide street of yards and stables and inns, horse-dung and straw and the smell of hot iron from the farrier’s, carts drawn up in rows with their shafts on the ground. Nobody is loading anything. The carters are standing about in twos and threes in the yard gates with their hands in their pockets, not talking, and the drovers bringing cattle down to the Shambles go past them very politely.

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 8:40 a.m.*

The Market is a sloping square with a stone cross in the middle where the hiring is done, the Guildhall along the top side with its clock and its arcade, and the Bridge Company’s counting-house next to it, newly painted blue, with a bridge in gold over the door. The Mercer Steps go up behind to the Hill. Down Shambles Lane you can hear cattle, and smell them. On the Cross itself, on every post of the arcade, and on the counting-house door, the same printed sheet is pasted.

**› To the Bridge End, under the gatehouse** · *Day 1, 8:45 a.m.*

The brindled dog under the gatehouse lifts his eyes to you, and not his head. Under the gatehouse wall, directly below the rail, a brindled dog lies with his chin on his paws, as close to the wall as he can get, and does not move when people step over him. The road comes out of the Market into the shadow of the gatehouse, a square tower of red stone over the bridge’s first arch, with the lord’s arms over the arch and the lord’s chain across it, new, the links still bright. Beside the arch, where a house stood, a heap of black timbers. In front of the chain, a booth of planks with a board nailed up beside it, very freshly painted. And up on the tower’s river side, along the parapet of the leads, three shapes in sacking hang against the sky on short ropes, and turn a little when the wind comes up the river, and turn back. A few people stand about at the Bridge End looking up, the way people stop to look at a fire, and then go on. A paper is nailed to the gate-ward’s door in the tower, in a clerk’s round hand with the reeve’s seal at the foot. Annis Pye, a girl with a tray of pies on a strap round her neck, is doing good business with the crowd. She has her back to the gatehouse. The gate-ward’s door into the tower is shut and locked. Here are Bramble, a carter’s dog, brindled, grey in the muzzle; Amos Pinch, the Bridge Company’s collector, in a new black coat; and Annis Pye, a pie-seller at the Bridge End, with a tray on a strap.

**› Read the Company's new toll-board** · *Day 1, 8:50 a.m.*

The Bridge Company’s new toll-board, by the booth: A CART, ONE PENNY. A BEAST, ONE PENNY THE HEAD. A CARCASS, THREE PENCE THE HUNDREDWEIGHT. GOODS OF A FELON: FORFEIT TO THE LORD, NOT TO PASS. A PILGRIM TO SAINT WADE’S ON HIS OWN FEET: FREE. THE ABBESS’S TITHE AND THE LIBERTY’S OFFAL TO THE TAN-YARDS: FREE, BY ANCIENT PRIVILEGE. A FOOT-PASSENGER: ONE FARTHING (SINCE THE RIOT). BY ORDER OF THE WORSHIPFUL COMPANY OF THE BRIDGE, FARMERS OF THE LORD’S TOLL.

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 8:55 a.m.*

The Market Cross: the Guildhall clock, the blue counting-house, the sheets on every post.

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 9:05 a.m.*

Northgate: the yards, the stables, the carters standing in the gateways.

**› To the carters' yards** · *Day 1, 9:10 a.m.*

The carters’ yards behind Northgate: wagons up on blocks, wheels stacked against the walls, a forge with the farrier at it. The young carters sit on a dray in the middle of the yard with nothing to do, talking low, and stop when anyone comes in. Three wagon-places along the wall stand empty, swept: where Pardoe’s, Fenner’s and Wilder’s carts stood. The carts are in the Guildhall yard, impounded. Here are Kit Pardoe, your brother; Jos Farrant, the Northgate farrier, leather-aproned; and the young carters, half a dozen carters’ sons, sitting on a dray.

**› Talk to the young carters** · *Day 1, 9:10 a.m.*

The young carters look at you, and at each other.

**› Ask the young carters about the night of the rising** · *Day 1, 9:10 a.m.*

‘We broke the chain,’ says the biggest of them, not quietly enough. ‘And we threw their board in the river. And we’d do it again.’ ‘We didn’t burn the toll-house,’ says another. ‘We never burned it.’ ‘Who cares who burned it,’ says the first. ‘They hanged Wat for the cart and Dunstan and Joss for nothing. Nothing!’ They look at you. ‘You’ll see,’ says the third. ‘Tomorrow night. Ask Kit.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 9:10 a.m.*



**› Talk to Kit Pardoe** · *Day 1, 9:10 a.m.*

Kit looks up, and looks away again.

**› Ask Kit what he and the others mean to do** · *Day 1, 9:10 a.m.*

He looks round to see who is near. ‘The counting-house.’ Low, and fierce. ‘Tomorrow at midnight. The Company’s. Where they count the toll and print the lies about Da. Lamp-oil from the yard, and every window.’ He is almost smiling. ‘Let them hang us for something.’

**› Tell Kit that if they must break something, they should sink the Company's new toll-board** · *Day 1, 9:10 a.m.*

He stares at you. Then, for the first time since you came, he laughs: a real one, that surprises him. ‘The board. Again.’ He is grinning. ‘In the river. Twice in one week. The whole of Wrayford’d laugh till Michaelmas. Pinch’d have to rule from memory.’ He sobers. ‘And nobody hangs for a board.’ He gets up. ‘I’ll tell them. Midnight, the board, not the counting-house.’ Joan gains 300 experience: kept Kit from the counting-house.

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 9:10 a.m.*

‘Be careful,’ says Kit Pardoe. ‘I know you won’t, but be careful.’

## wait

**… (waiting, to Day 4, 9:00 a.m.)**

> At two the Bench rises. The Remonstrance goes to the lord with one clause added at the end, which the clerk read in and nobody opposed: that the dead remain upon the rail until the Company's loss in the riot shall have been reckoned by the lord's captain, the Company's claims being thereby secured. The reeve, it is said, read it in his court with his face like a shut door.
> All Wrayford is laughing by breakfast. In the night somebody unbolted the Company's new toll-board from beside the booth, all eight feet of it, freshly painted, and carried it down Bridge End and threw it in the Wray, for the second time in a week, and the sergeant and two riders sat in the dark of the counting-house all night waiting for a fire that never came. Amos Pinch stood at the booth this morning and ruled from memory, and got the price of a beast wrong, and the drovers cheered him.
> At noon a boy comes running over the drawbridge from Overbridge shouting that there are riders on the south road, a long way off, a dozen of them and a woman in grey, and the whole of the Bridge End turns to look south.
> At dusk the captain rides in over the drawbridge with her riders behind her, and does not look up at the gatehouse as she passes under it, and goes to the Guildhall, where the Bench has laid supper.
> Word runs through Northgate in the small hours, door to door: Maudie Pardoe went up to the gatehouse with her bread knife for the cords, and the captain's riders took her at the gate-ward's door, and she is in the Guildhall cellar, and the captain has been woken.
> Before dawn the carters' yards empty. Kit Pardoe goes down the Cartway at the head of thirty men with cart-staves and torches, to the gatehouse where his mother was taken, and the captain's riders meet them at the Bridge End. By first light it is over, and Kit is in the Guildhall cellar with his arm broken, and two of the riders are being carried into the Bridge End inn.
> The captain gives her verdict at nine in the Guildhall, and it takes one sentence. By noon the carters' row is burning, Wain Row and the yards behind it, and the captain's riders stand at the end of every lane to see that nobody puts it out. Ten men are hanged from the gatehouse rail beside the three, which is the lord's law for a rising, and the rail is not long enough, and they use the drawbridge chains. In the evening the riders go home down the south road with the toll money and the lord's thanks, and Wrayford stands in its own smoke and does not speak to itself for a generation. The Company rebuilds its counting-house in stone.
> Kit Pardoe goes south with the captain, tied to a rider's saddle, for the lord's justice at Harbury. He looks back once, at the bridge.
> Maudie Pardoe is the first of the ten. She goes up the gate-ward's stair on her own feet, and does not look for anyone in the crowd.
