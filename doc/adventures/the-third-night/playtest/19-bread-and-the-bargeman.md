## bread

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:30 a.m.*

You set out from the drove road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. Northgate is where the drove roads come into the city: a wide street of yards and stables and inns, horse-dung and straw and the smell of hot iron from the farrier’s, carts drawn up in rows with their shafts on the ground. Nobody is loading anything. The carters are standing about in twos and threes in the yard gates with their hands in their pockets, not talking, and the drovers bringing cattle down to the Shambles go past them very politely.

**› To Wain Row** · *Day 1, 8:35 a.m.*

Wain Row is a lane of carters’ houses, each with its yard and its cart-shed behind, the doors painted the colours carters paint them, red and green and a blue that was once expensive. Four doors along, the Pardoes’ is red. Its step has been scrubbed this morning, hard. Two women talking on the next step stop talking when they see you, and go in.

**› Into the Pardoe house** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

The kitchen is as it was: the long table, the settle, the bread oven, the hook by the door where your father’s coat hangs, which is the first thing you look at and the last thing you will be able to stop looking at. It smells of bread. It always smelled of bread. Your mother is at the table with her sleeves up and flour to the elbow, and she looks at you in the doorway for a long moment, and then goes on kneading. There is more bread than one woman could eat in a month: loaves on the dresser, on the settle, under cloths on the windowsill. She makes bread when she cannot think. Maudie Pardoe, your mother, is here.

**› Talk to Maudie Pardoe** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

Maudie Pardoe does not stop kneading.

**› Ask your mother for bread to take into the city** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

She looks at the loaves on every surface as if she had not seen them before. ‘Take them,’ she says. ‘Take all of them. Give them to anybody. I can’t stop making them and I can’t eat them and I can’t throw them out.’ She is wrapping four in a cloth while she talks, quickly, not looking at you. ‘Your father gave bread to anybody. The drovers. That fish of his in the river.’ She ties the cloth. ‘Give it to the riders, for all I care. Give it to Simon Hulme. Let them choke.’ And then, differently: ‘No. Let them eat it.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

‘Be careful,’ says Maudie Pardoe. ‘I know you won’t, but be careful.’

**› Out to Wain Row** · *Day 1, 8:37 a.m.*

Wain Row: cart-sheds, painted doors, the Pardoes’ red one with its scrubbed step.

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:42 a.m.*

Northgate: the yards, the stables, the carters standing in the gateways.

## rope

**› Into the Nine Wheels** · *Day 1, 8:43 a.m.*

The carters’ inn: a long low room, smoke, a fire with a kettle, benches worn into the shape of carters. Nailed to the beams overhead for a ceiling are nine cart-wheels, one for every carter the fell road has killed since the inn was built, the oldest black with a century of smoke and the newest with its paint still showing. Over the settle by the fire someone has hung a black ribbon on a nail, and under it nobody sits. Pell Saxby, who keeps the Nine Wheels, is here.

**› Talk to Pell Saxby** · *Day 1, 8:43 a.m.*

Pell Saxby wipes her hands on her apron and looks you over. ‘Bed, beer or bread,’ she says, and then, because she is honest: ‘The bread’s Maudie Pardoe’s. She’s sent us forty loaves since Tuesday. I can’t sell it and I can’t stop her.’

## riders

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 8:57 a.m.*

The Market is a sloping square with a stone cross in the middle where the hiring is done, the Guildhall along the top side with its clock and its arcade, and the Bridge Company’s counting-house next to it, newly painted blue, with a bridge in gold over the door. The Mercer Steps go up behind to the Hill. Down Shambles Lane you can hear cattle, and smell them. On the Cross itself, on every post of the arcade, and on the counting-house door, the same printed sheet is pasted.

**› To the Bridge End, under the gatehouse** · *Day 1, 9:02 a.m.*

Alderman Gidley arrives at the Bridge End in his blue gown, as he has every morning, and stands below the rail with his hat in his hand and weeps, beautifully, for some minutes, while the crowd watches. Then he wipes his eyes, and asks Pinch for the morning’s takings, and goes up the Cartway to the Guildhall. The brindled dog under the gatehouse lifts his eyes to you, and not his head. Under the gatehouse wall, directly below the rail, a brindled dog lies with his chin on his paws, as close to the wall as he can get, and does not move when people step over him. The road comes out of the Market into the shadow of the gatehouse, a square tower of red stone over the bridge’s first arch, with the lord’s arms over the arch and the lord’s chain across it, new, the links still bright. Beside the arch, where a house stood, a heap of black timbers. In front of the chain, a booth of planks with a board nailed up beside it, very freshly painted. And up on the tower’s river side, along the parapet of the leads, three shapes in sacking hang against the sky on short ropes, and turn a little when the wind comes up the river, and turn back. A few people stand about at the Bridge End looking up, the way people stop to look at a fire, and then go on. A paper is nailed to the gate-ward’s door in the tower, in a clerk’s round hand with the reeve’s seal at the foot. Annis Pye, a girl with a tray of pies on a strap round her neck, is doing good business with the crowd. She has her back to the gatehouse. Pinch’s tally lies open on the ledge of the booth: a column of entries in ink, very neat, and beside every few lines, so faint you would not see it unless the sun were on the page, a small mark in pencil, like a tick against a sum he is keeping somewhere else. The gate-ward’s door into the tower is shut and locked. Here are Bramble, a carter’s dog, brindled, grey in the muzzle; Amos Pinch, the Bridge Company’s collector, in a new black coat; Annis Pye, a pie-seller at the Bridge End, with a tray on a strap; and Alderman Prosper Gidley, the Master of the Worshipful Company of the Bridge, in a blue gown.

**› Send Maudie Pardoe's bread up to the riders on the leads, in the gate-ward's basket** · *Day 1, 6:05 p.m.*

You put two loaves in the basket that hangs on its rope by the gate-ward’s door, and pull the bell, and the basket goes up into the dusk. For a while nothing. Then a rider’s face over the parapet, and another. ‘Whose?’ says Gault’s voice. You tell him: Pardoe’s widow’s. There is a long silence on the leads. Then the basket comes down again, empty, and Gault leans over and looks at you, and at the rail, and says, quite loud, to nobody: ‘My lads are tired. They’ll sleep sound tonight, all of them. So will I.’

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 6:10 p.m.*

The Market Cross: the Guildhall clock, the blue counting-house, the sheets on every post.

**› To the Shambles** · *Day 1, 6:15 p.m.*

The Shambles: a steep lane of butchers’ shops with their shutters down for counters, blood in the gutters, flies, drovers’ beasts in hurdle pens at the bottom, and at the top a cart waiting to take the day’s leavings over the bridge to the tan-yards. The Liberty’s offal cart: a tumbril with high sides, loaded to the brim with the Shambles’ leavings under a dripping tarpaulin. Here are Saul Kemp, a butcher, master of the Shambles; Jack Kemp, the butcher’s boy, who drives the offal cart; and Ham Ostler, a drover from the fells, in a smock and a hat with a curlew feather.

**› To the Wharf** · *Day 1, 6:17 p.m.*

The Wharf runs along the river west of the bridge: warehouses with hoists, barges tied two deep, eel-traps, nets drying, a smell of tar and river. Downstream the bridge goes over on its arches, and the water piles up white against the cutwaters and goes through roaring.

**› To the bank under the first arches** · *Day 1, 6:22 p.m.*

The bank under the bridge’s first arch: a strip of mud and stones where the city’s boys fish and its drunks sleep, and the great pier going up out of the water beside you into the shadow, green to a man’s height and red stone above. High overhead, the gatehouse parapet. Out from the bank, under the brown water, you can just make out a line of big squared stones going across: the old ford. The Wray runs deep and brown over where the old ford was. Nobody wades it while the mills are grinding.

**› To the gatehouse leads** · *Day 1, 10:52 p.m.*

The leads at night: the brazier’s red glow, the parapet a black line, the river loud below. The riders sit round the brazier with their backs to the river parapet, all of them, very deliberately, eating bread and dicing, and none of them looks round. The gate-ward’s door into the tower is shut and locked. Along the river parapet three men hang on short ropes from iron hooks driven into the coping, under sacking the reeve allowed. Their boots show below the sacking: a carter’s big boots, a pair mended with wire, a boy’s. Here are Sergeant Gault, the riders’ sergeant, a square man with a broken nose; and the lord’s riders on the gate, four of the lord’s riders, in buff coats, with the lord’s badge.

**› Cut the three down from the rail** · *Day 1, 11:02 p.m.*

You cut them down one by one, and take each one’s weight as the rope goes, and lay them on the leads. They are lighter and heavier than you thought. Nobody in Wrayford will ever look at you the same way, if they know. The iron hooks in the coping are empty. Three cut ends of rope hang from them. Wat Pardoe lies wrapped in his sacking, a big man, his boots showing. Dunstan Fenner lies wrapped in his sacking, his mended boots showing. Joss Wilder lies wrapped in his sacking. He was not very old.

**› To Wade's Step, in the middle of the old ford** · *Day 2, 1:32 a.m.*

Something moves in the black hole of the Step: not the current. A long shape, longer than you are, turning slowly under the surface with the patience of a thing that has never once been in a hurry. The far stones are on the other side of the Step, and of what lies in it across the way. Here two of the ford-stones are gone, and the water goes suddenly deep and black and quiet, a hole in the river the width of a cart: Wade’s Step.

**› Throw bread into the deep water at Wade's Step, the way the old drovers did** · *Day 2, 1:34 a.m.*

The loaf floats a moment, turning, pale on the black water. Then the water under it bulges, and opens, and there is a mouth there wider than a cart-wheel, and the loaf is gone, and the river closes over the place as if it had never been. You go on along the stones with your heart going, and nothing touches you. The long shape lies on the bottom of the Step like a sunk log, its gills working, and lets you by.

**› To the tan-yards** · *Day 2, 1:54 a.m.*

The tan-yards at night: the pits black, the hides on their frames like people standing.
