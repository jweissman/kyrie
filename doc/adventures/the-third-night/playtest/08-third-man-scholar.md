## day1

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:30 a.m.*

You set out from the drove road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. Northgate is where the drove roads come into the city: a wide street of yards and stables and inns, horse-dung and straw and the smell of hot iron from the farrier’s, carts drawn up in rows with their shafts on the ground. Nobody is loading anything. The carters are standing about in twos and threes in the yard gates with their hands in their pockets, not talking, and the drovers bringing cattle down to the Shambles go past them very politely.

**› Into the Nine Wheels** · *Day 1, 8:31 a.m.*

The carters’ inn: a long low room with nine cart-wheels nailed to the beams for a ceiling, smoke, a fire with a kettle, benches worn into the shape of carters. Over the settle by the fire someone has hung a black ribbon on a nail, and under it nobody sits. Pell Saxby, who keeps the Nine Wheels, is here.

**› Talk to Pell Saxby** · *Day 1, 8:31 a.m.*

Pell Saxby wipes her hands on her apron and looks you over. ‘Bed, beer or bread,’ she says. ‘Or trouble, but we’re full up of that.’

**› Buy › A coil of rope** · *Day 1, 8:34 a.m.*

Hesk pays Pell Saxby, and takes a coil of rope.

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 8:34 a.m.*

‘Off you go, then,’ says Pell Saxby, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**› Out to Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:35 a.m.*

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 8:45 a.m.*

The Market is a sloping square with a stone cross in the middle where the hiring is done, the Guildhall along the top side with its clock and its arcade, and the Bridge Company’s counting-house next to it, newly painted blue, with a bridge in gold over the door. The Mercer Steps go up behind to the Hill. Down Shambles Lane you can hear cattle, and smell them. On the Cross itself, on every post of the arcade, and on the counting-house door, the same printed sheet is pasted.

**› To the Shambles** · *Day 1, 8:50 a.m.*

The Shambles: a steep lane of butchers’ shops with their shutters down for counters, blood in the gutters, flies, drovers’ beasts in hurdle pens at the bottom, and at the top a cart waiting to take the day’s leavings over the bridge to the tan-yards. The Liberty’s offal cart: a tumbril with high sides, loaded to the brim with the Shambles’ leavings under a dripping tarpaulin. Here are Saul Kemp, a butcher, master of the Shambles; Jack Kemp, the butcher’s boy, who drives the offal cart; and Ham Ostler, a drover from the fells, in a smock and a hat with a curlew feather.

**› Ask Ham Ostler about the toll on his beasts** · *Day 1, 8:50 a.m.*

‘A penny a head to put my beasts over a bridge they’ve been going over since before the Company’s grandfathers were breeched.’ He spits. ‘Sixty head is five shillings. I sell them at the Liberty fair, Michaelmas, over the water, and the Company takes five shillings before I’ve sold a hoof.’ He grins. ‘So I’ll not pay it. My leader knows the ford. Small hours of the second night, when the mills have the river penned, I’ll take them down under the arches and over, and Pinch can rule on that.’ He looks at you. ‘You can come, if you like. It’s a sight. Most folk have forgot the ford’s there.’

## verdict

**… (waiting, to Day 4, 9:09 a.m.)**

> In the morning the reeve's men take Kit Pardoe from the yards, because he is Wat's son and the city knows what it would have done in his place, and he does not deny it, which he could have, and goes with them to the cellar with his head up.
> Word goes round: the reeve's men found Joss Wilder in the morning where he had been left, and carried him up to the Guildhall yard under a sheet, to wait for the captain.
> At noon a boy comes running over the drawbridge from Overbridge shouting that there are riders on the south road, a long way off, a dozen of them and a woman in grey, and the whole of the Bridge End turns to look south.
> Word comes over the water: at the end of the day old Corbin found Wat Pardoe lying by the church door in his sacking, and stood over him a while with his pipe, and then buried him, properly, without being asked.
> At dusk the captain rides in over the drawbridge with her riders behind her, and does not look up at the gatehouse as she passes under it, and goes to the Guildhall, where the Bench has laid supper.
> At the Bridge End, as the captain's riders water their horses, Ham Ostler leans on his crook and tells the nearest of them, loud enough for the captain, that he has driven beasts through every rising in the north these thirty years, and this is not one, it is a city with a toothache. The captain looks at him from the saddle. Drovers, it seems, are people she listens to.
> When the captain comes up the south road there is nothing on the rail but three cut ropes, and in Wade's Acre there are new graves that nobody will admit to digging, and not enough of them. The one who could not be got over the water before the river rose was found by the reeve's men at first light, where he was left, and the captain has him put in the lord's lime at the crossroads on the south road, alone, which is the lord's law for a rebel's body. Then she gives her verdict that Wrayford is in order, and goes. Ever after, in the alehouses of Northgate, Wrayford argues about which of the three it was, and whether it matters; and it does.
> Kit Pardoe goes south with the captain, tied to a rider's saddle, for the lord's justice at Harbury. He looks back once, at the bridge.
