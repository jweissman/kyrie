## day1

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:30 a.m.*

You set out from the drove road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. Northgate is where the drove roads come into the city: a wide street of yards and stables and inns, horse-dung and straw and the smell of hot iron from the farrier’s, carts drawn up in rows with their shafts on the ground. Nobody is loading anything. The carters are standing about in twos and threes in the yard gates with their hands in their pockets, not talking, and the drovers bringing cattle down to the Shambles go past them very politely.

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 8:40 a.m.*

The Market is a sloping square with a stone cross in the middle where the hiring is done, the Guildhall along the top side with its clock and its arcade, and the Bridge Company’s counting-house next to it, newly painted blue, with a bridge in gold over the door. The Mercer Steps go up behind to the Hill. Down Shambles Lane you can hear cattle, and smell them. On the Cross itself, on every post of the arcade, and on the counting-house door, the same printed sheet is pasted.

**… (waiting, to Day 1, 9:00 a.m.)**

**› Into the Guildhall** · *Day 1, 9:01 a.m.*

The Guildhall’s long hall: a hammer-beam roof, the city’s arms in plaster, a long table on a dais where the Bench sits, and at the far end the reeve’s chair and his clerk’s desk, where he keeps court. Along one wall hang the portraits of fifty years of the Bridge Company, Masters and Wardens, in blue gowns, each more prosperous than the last. An easel stands at the end of the row. On the easel, the present Bench of the Bridge Company, five aldermen in blue gowns round a table with a model of the bridge on it, blocked in and half painted. It is extremely good. Alderman Gidley is in the middle, with one hand on the model bridge as if blessing it and the other, which the painter has not finished, very near the money-box. Here are Tom Lumley, a painter, in a coat with paint on the elbows; Simon Hulme, the Reeve of Wrayford, grey and careful; and Hal Hulme, the reeve’s son, who writes his father’s papers.

**› Talk to Simon Hulme** · *Day 1, 9:01 a.m.*

Simon Hulme looks up, and you watch him decide, in the space of a breath, to be the reeve and not anything else.

**› Ask the reeve why the dead must stay on the rail** · *Day 1, 9:01 a.m.*

‘Because the captain comes at dusk on the third day,’ says Simon Hulme, ‘and asks one question: is Wrayford in rebellion. If she finds the city quiet and its ringleaders on the rail, she takes the toll money and goes home. If she finds it in rebellion, the lord’s law is ten hanged for every rider hurt and the carters’ row burned to the stones.’ He says it as if he had said it many times, to himself. ‘Three men on a rail, or thirty. I have kept this city twenty years.’ His hand is flat on the desk. ‘I did not hang them. I would have brought them to the lord’s court. The riders carried a warrant I was not shown.’ He looks at you for a long time. ‘You were in my house every day of your childhood,’ he says. ‘You and Hal. Tell me, then. You’ve come home and found your father on the rail, and you’re the only one in this city who can say anything to me I’d listen to.’ His voice does not rise. ‘What would you have me do?’

**› Tell him: cut them down, and let the captain find a city that buries its dead** · *Day 1, 9:01 a.m.*

He shuts his eyes. ‘A city that buries its dead,’ he says. ‘That’s a thing to say. That’s a fine thing.’ He opens them. ‘And when she asks who cut them down against the lord’s warrant?’ He waits. You do not have an answer, and he sees it. ‘No,’ he says, gently. ‘Neither have I.’ But he does not look away from you.

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 9:01 a.m.*

Simon Hulme lifts a hand, once.

**› Out to the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 9:02 a.m.*

The Market Cross: the Guildhall clock, the blue counting-house, the sheets on every post.

**› To the Bridge End, under the gatehouse** · *Day 1, 9:07 a.m.*

The brindled dog under the gatehouse lifts his eyes to you, and not his head. Under the gatehouse wall, directly below the rail, a brindled dog lies with his chin on his paws, as close to the wall as he can get, and does not move when people step over him. The road comes out of the Market into the shadow of the gatehouse, a square tower of red stone over the bridge’s first arch, with the lord’s arms over the arch and the lord’s chain across it, new, the links still bright. Beside the arch, where a house stood, a heap of black timbers. In front of the chain, a booth of planks with a board nailed up beside it, very freshly painted. And up on the tower’s river side, along the parapet of the leads, three shapes in sacking hang against the sky on short ropes, and turn a little when the wind comes up the river, and turn back. A few people stand about at the Bridge End looking up, the way people stop to look at a fire, and then go on. A paper is nailed to the gate-ward’s door in the tower, in a clerk’s round hand with the reeve’s seal at the foot. Annis Pye, a girl with a tray of pies on a strap round her neck, is doing good business with the crowd. She has her back to the gatehouse. Pinch’s tally lies open on the ledge of the booth: a column of entries in ink, very neat, and beside every few lines, so faint you would not see it unless the sun were on the page, a small mark in pencil, like a tick against a sum he is keeping somewhere else. The gate-ward’s door into the tower is shut and locked. Here are Bramble, a carter’s dog, brindled, grey in the muzzle; Amos Pinch, the Bridge Company’s collector, in a new black coat; Annis Pye, a pie-seller at the Bridge End, with a tray on a strap; and Alderman Prosper Gidley, the Master of the Worshipful Company of the Bridge, in a blue gown.

**› Read the reeve's decree on the gatehouse door** · *Day 1, 9:12 a.m.*

The reeve’s decree is nailed to the gatehouse door: the three are to hang on the rail until the lord’s captain comes, at dusk on the third day, so that she finds Wrayford in order. Whoever touches them shall hang beside them.

**› Ask Sergeant Gault to let you up on the leads to look at them** · *Day 1, 9:14 a.m.*

Gault looks down at you over the parapet for a while. Then he comes down and unlocks the door himself. ‘Look,’ he says. ‘Don’t touch.’ He stands at your shoulder the whole time. The leads of the gatehouse: grey lead sheet, a parapet, a brazier, the riders’ saddlebags. The river is a long way down, brown and quick, and the whole city is laid out from here: Northgate’s smoke, the Hill’s gardens, the bridge running out on its arches to Overbridge and the church tower on the far bank. Close to, the sacking is old grain sacks, stitched. The boots below it are a carter’s big boots, a pair mended with wire, and a boy’s with new laces. The boy’s hands are tied in front of him with cord. There is nothing on them. No ring. The gate-ward’s door into the tower is shut and locked. Along the river parapet three men hang on short ropes from iron hooks driven into the coping, under sacking the reeve allowed. Their boots show below the sacking: a carter’s big boots, a pair mended with wire, a boy’s. Here are Sergeant Gault, the riders’ sergeant, a square man with a broken nose; and the lord’s riders on the gate, four of the lord’s riders, in buff coats, with the lord’s badge.

**› Talk to Sergeant Gault** · *Day 1, 9:14 a.m.*

Sergeant Gault looks at you the way a dog looks at another dog, without much interest either way.

**› Ask Gault how he knew which road they'd take** · *Day 1, 9:14 a.m.*

‘Word came.’ He does not look at you. ‘Midnight, a lad with a letter. Back road to the fells, the cart with the red wheels, before light.’ He shrugs. ‘Somebody in this town wanted it done quiet. They all want it done quiet. Then they all come and stand at the gate and look.’

**› To Bridge Street, on the arches** · *Day 1, 8:29 p.m.*

Bridge Street runs out over the river on the arches, three storeys of shops leaning toward each other across a lane so narrow the upper floors nearly meet: a glover, a cook-shop, a seller of spectacles, a seller of Saint Wade’s badges, a tooth-drawer. Between the houses you see the river below through gaps, going by brown and fast. Halfway along, a chapel is built into the middle pier, and beyond it the drawbridge, and beyond that Overbridge and its church. The first house past the gatehouse is the toll-keeper’s, narrow, with a shut door. Its shutters are closed in the middle of the day. On a tray outside the badge-seller’s, tin Saint Wades at a farthing each: a stooping man up to his knees in tin water, with a child on his shoulders. ‘Carry me over,’ says the badge-seller to nobody, the way a fishwife says ‘fresh’. The drawbridge is down. The toll-keeper’s door is shut. Knock, and a woman’s voice inside says, ‘Go away,’ without anger, the way you would say it to the rain. Annis Pye, a pie-seller at the Bridge End, with a tray on a strap, is here.

**› Into Saint Wade's chapel on the bridge** · *Day 1, 8:30 p.m.*

A small chapel of grey stone built into the pier, so that the river is loud through the floor. Candles. A trapdoor in the floor, propped open, with a ladder going down into the dark and the noise of water. Over the altar, the old painting: Saint Wade up to his chest in a black river, bent nearly double under a grey child, looking down at his own feet, where the painter has made the riverbed darker in one place than anywhere else. Under it, scratched into the plaster by many hands at many times, the same three words: CARRY ME OVER.

**› Into the starling steps** · *Day 1, 8:32 p.m.*

The starling: a ledge of stone round the foot of the middle pier where it breaks the river, wet, green, wide enough for two to sit with their legs over. Under the arch the light is green and moving. On the stone someone, a long time ago, has cut two sets of initials with a knife. Hal Hulme is sitting on the starling with his legs over the water and his back to the pier, where the two sets of initials are cut, as if he had been there every evening for three years. Hal Hulme, the reeve’s son, who writes his father’s papers, is here.

**› Talk to Hal Hulme** · *Day 1, 8:32 p.m.*

Hal Hulme looks up, and for a moment he is twelve years old, and then he is not.

**› Tell Hal you have read his letter in Gault's coat** · *Day 1, 8:32 p.m.*

He goes white, and then he goes very still, the way his father does. ‘Yes,’ he says. ‘I wrote it.’ He does not look away. ‘My father said the riders would take them for the lord’s court. That’s what he believed; he hadn’t seen the warrant. The young ones were going to burn the counting-house, and the chain was already gone, and I thought: if the old ones are taken quietly on the road there’ll be no more fire, and they’ll be fined at the court, and that will be all.’ His hands are shaking and he puts them flat on his knees. ‘They hanged them on the road at sunrise. I have not slept. I have written the decree that keeps them there, in my best hand, because my father told me to and I had already done the other thing.’ He looks at you. ‘Do what you like with it. I’d tell Kit myself if I thought he’d let me finish.’ Brand gains 400 experience: found out who sent the riders.

**› Tell Hal he must tell his father** · *Day 1, 8:32 p.m.*

He nods, slowly, as if you had told him the time. ‘Yes.’ He gets up. ‘Tonight. When he comes in.’ At the door he stops. ‘He’ll resign,’ he says. ‘Or he won’t, and that’ll be worse.’

**… (waiting, to Day 4, 9:02 a.m.)**

> THE SECOND DAY. The heat comes up off the river before the sun is over the fells, and by seven the Market smells of dung and hot stone and, when the wind is from the bridge, of something sweeter underneath that nobody mentions. The crows have found the rail. Somewhere upstream Dunch's mill-wheels are turning; the whole city can hear them when the bell stops.
> In the middle of the day Simon Hulme comes out of the Guildhall alone, without his chain of office, and walks down to the Bridge End, and the crowd parts for him. He goes up the gate-ward's stair. Gault meets him at the top and says something, and the reeve says something back, and Gault stands aside. And the reeve of Wrayford cuts the three down from the rail with his own knife, one by one, and hands them down to the carters below, and comes down after them, and goes back up the Cartway to the Guildhall, and shuts the door.
> In Wade's Acre old Corbin has taken his coat off and is cutting turf by the church door, and the sound of the spade carries over the water.
> In Wade's Acre, in the afternoon, the three are buried by the church door, with Wrayford standing round the wall and on the wall and on the roofs of the tan-yards. The Abbess says the office herself.
> Word comes before dawn, the way it comes in a city, from nowhere: the young carters went for the Company's counting-house at midnight with lamp-oil, and Sergeant Gault was waiting in the dark for them, and the fire was out before it was lit, and Kit Pardoe and two others are in the Guildhall cellar.
> THE THIRD DAY. Thunder in the fells before light, a long way off, like carts going over a bridge. The air is thick and yellow and the swallows are flying low over the water. The dogs of Wrayford will not settle. It is the third day; everybody knows it is the third day, and nobody says so.
> At noon a boy comes running over the drawbridge from Overbridge shouting that there are riders on the south road, a long way off, a dozen of them and a woman in grey, and the whole of the Bridge End turns to look south.
> At dusk the captain rides in over the drawbridge with her riders behind her, and does not look up at the gatehouse as she passes under it, and goes to the Guildhall, where the Bench has laid supper.
> THE THIRD NIGHT. The storm comes over the bridge at the curfew bell, and the rain with it, straight down, on the empty rail and on the Acre, where the new turf is darkening by the church door. In Northgate people stand in their doorways and let it rain on them, for no reason they can give.
> THE FOURTH MORNING. Everything is washed: the stones of the Market shine, the river is high and brown and full of sticks, and the air smells of nothing at all for the first time in a week. The captain's riders are saddling in the Bridge End. At nine, the Guildhall.
> The captain hears at supper that the reeve of Wrayford cut the rebels down from the lord's rail with his own hands. In the morning she gives her verdict: that the city is in order, and its reeve is not. Simon Hulme hands her his chain of office on the Guildhall steps, and she takes it, not unkindly, and rides home with it and the toll money. He goes on living in the house on the Hill, and keeps a garden, and on the third night of every month walks down to the Bridge End and stands under the gatehouse for an hour, and nobody asks him why.
> Kit Pardoe goes south with the captain, tied to a rider's saddle, for the lord's justice at Harbury. He looks back once, at the bridge.
> Hal Hulme, it is said, told his father what he did on the night of the rising, and his father sat with his head in his hands until dawn; and it was the next day that the reeve went down to the Bridge End with his knife.
