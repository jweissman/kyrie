## day1

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:30 a.m.*

You set out from the drove road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. Northgate is where the drove roads come into the city: a wide street of yards and stables and inns, horse-dung and straw and the smell of hot iron from the farrier’s, carts drawn up in rows with their shafts on the ground. Nobody is loading anything. The carters are standing about in twos and threes in the yard gates with their hands in their pockets, not talking, and the drovers bringing cattle down to the Shambles go past them very politely.

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 8:40 a.m.*

The Market is a sloping square with a stone cross in the middle where the hiring is done, the Guildhall along the top side with its clock and its arcade, and the Bridge Company’s counting-house next to it, newly painted blue, with a bridge in gold over the door. The Mercer Steps go up behind to the Hill. Down Shambles Lane you can hear cattle, and smell them. On the Cross itself, on every post of the arcade, and on the counting-house door, the same printed sheet is pasted.

**› To the Bridge End, under the gatehouse** · *Day 1, 8:45 a.m.*

The brindled dog under the gatehouse lifts his eyes to you, and not his head. Under the gatehouse wall, directly below the rail, a brindled dog lies with his chin on his paws, as close to the wall as he can get, and does not move when people step over him. The road comes out of the Market into the shadow of the gatehouse, a square tower of red stone over the bridge’s first arch, with the lord’s arms over the arch and the lord’s chain across it, new, the links still bright. Beside the arch, where a house stood, a heap of black timbers. In front of the chain, a booth of planks with a board nailed up beside it, very freshly painted. And up on the tower’s river side, along the parapet of the leads, three shapes in sacking hang against the sky on short ropes, and turn a little when the wind comes up the river, and turn back. A few people stand about at the Bridge End looking up, the way people stop to look at a fire, and then go on. A paper is nailed to the gate-ward’s door in the tower, in a clerk’s round hand with the reeve’s seal at the foot. Annis Pye, a girl with a tray of pies on a strap round her neck, is doing good business with the crowd. She has her back to the gatehouse. Pinch’s tally lies open on the ledge of the booth: a column of entries in ink, very neat, and beside every few lines, so faint you would not see it unless the sun were on the page, a small mark in pencil, like a tick against a sum he is keeping somewhere else. The gate-ward’s door into the tower is shut and locked. Here are Bramble, a carter’s dog, brindled, grey in the muzzle; Amos Pinch, the Bridge Company’s collector, in a new black coat; and Annis Pye, a pie-seller at the Bridge End, with a tray on a strap.

**› To Bridge Street, on the arches** · *Day 1, 8:46 a.m.*

At the chain Amos Pinch takes your farthing. There being no farthings left in Wrayford since the riot, he takes a penny, and writes you a tally-stick for the three farthings he owes you, which he says the Company will honour at Michaelmas. Bridge Street runs out over the river on the arches, three storeys of shops leaning toward each other across a lane so narrow the upper floors nearly meet: a glover, a cook-shop, a seller of spectacles, a seller of Saint Wade’s badges, a tooth-drawer. Between the houses you see the river below through gaps, going by brown and fast. Halfway along, a chapel is built into the middle pier, and beyond it the drawbridge, and beyond that Overbridge and its church. The first house past the gatehouse is the toll-keeper’s, narrow, with a shut door. Its shutters are closed in the middle of the day. On a tray outside the badge-seller’s, tin Saint Wades at a farthing each: a stooping man up to his knees in tin water, with a child on his shoulders. ‘Carry me over,’ says the badge-seller to nobody, the way a fishwife says ‘fresh’. The drawbridge is down. The toll-keeper’s door is shut. Knock, and a woman’s voice inside says, ‘Go away,’ without anger, the way you would say it to the rain.

**› To Overbridge Street** · *Day 1, 8:56 a.m.*

Overbridge Street comes off the drawbridge into another town: older, lower, poorer and louder, timber houses with their upper floors leaning on their neighbours, the tan-yards stinking at one end and the bear-garden roaring at the other, and in the middle Saint Wade’s, a squat old church with a tower, and the Abbess’s lodging behind a wall. A painted post at the bridge foot says THE LIBERTY OF SAINT WADE. The reeve’s writ ends here, and everybody walks a little differently. One lane off Overbridge Street is all of a piece, raw brick, every house the same, built in one year: the Liberty calls it the New Row, though it is thirty years old, and nobody under forty knows why it was needed. The drawbridge is down.

**› Into the Abbess's lodging** · *Day 1, 8:57 a.m.*

The Abbess’s court-room: a long room with a dais, a chair under a canopy, a table with a green cloth, and on the walls the charters of the Liberty, framed, all of them, from the oldest, which is a scrap of vellum with a seal like a biscuit, to the newest, which is a letter from the lord’s steward that she has had framed upside down. On the green cloth, rolled and tied: a raw cowhide, red side out, with a ticket on it in the Abbess’s hand: KEMP v. CROUCH. Dame Isobel Crane, the Lady Abbess of Saint Wade’s, who holds the Liberty of Overbridge, is here.

**› Talk to Dame Isobel Crane** · *Day 1, 8:57 a.m.*

The Abbess looks at you over a pair of spectacles. ‘Sit,’ she says. ‘Or stand. Only be brief, and be accurate.’

**› Ask the Abbess about the dead on the bridge** · *Day 1, 8:57 a.m.*

‘A question of jurisdiction,’ says the Abbess, ‘first, and of charity second, and I am honest enough to say so in that order.’ She takes her spectacles off. ‘Wrayford has buried its dead in Wade’s Acre since before the bridge, and every one of them crossed the river to get here, and not one paid the lord a penny. If those three reach the Liberty, they are mine, and I will bury them, and the reeve can write to the lord.’ She folds the spectacles. ‘I will not fetch them. If I send my brothers over that bridge for them I have admitted that the bridge is the city’s road and not the Liberty’s, and my predecessors have not admitted it for three hundred years.’ She looks at you. ‘I might put my claim in writing. For someone I had cause to trust.’

**› Ask the Abbess about the Bridge Company** · *Day 1, 8:57 a.m.*

‘The Worshipful Company of the Bridge.’ Very dry. ‘I have known Prosper Gidley since he sang in my predecessor’s choir. He sang very sweetly, and he was the boy who carried the collection. He has not changed. He has only grown.’ She turns a page. ‘They will vote for anything that costs them nothing they can be seen to lose. Remember that, if you have business with them. It is the only thing about them worth knowing.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 8:57 a.m.*

‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Dame Isobel Crane, who was not trying to.

**› Offer to say the office of the dead for the three, on the bridge, as a priest of no parish** · *Day 1, 9:07 a.m.*

The Abbess takes off her spectacles. ‘A priest of no parish,’ she says. ‘Who can stand on the lord’s bridge and say the words over Wrayford’s dead without it meaning that the Liberty crossed it.’ For the first time she looks at you as if you were of use. ‘Yes. Do. Tonight, at the curfew, under the rail. And come back to me after.’

**› Out to Overbridge Street** · *Day 1, 9:08 a.m.*

The drawbridge is down. Overbridge Street: leaning houses, the church, the post that says THE LIBERTY OF SAINT WADE.

## bench

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 2, 9:13 a.m.*

The Market Cross: the Guildhall clock, the blue counting-house, the sheets on every post. It is market day: farm carts, egg-women, a man selling eels live out of a barrel, and at the Cross the hiring, labourers standing with a tuft of wool or a whip in their hats for what they do.

**› Into the Guildhall** · *Day 2, 9:14 a.m.*

The Guildhall’s long hall: a hammer-beam roof, the city’s arms in plaster, a long table on a dais where the Bench sits, and at the far end the reeve’s chair and his clerk’s desk, where he keeps court. Along one wall hang the portraits of fifty years of the Bridge Company, Masters and Wardens, in blue gowns, each more prosperous than the last. An easel stands at the end of the row. On the easel, the present Bench of the Bridge Company, five aldermen in blue gowns round a table with a model of the bridge on it, blocked in and half painted. It is extremely good. Alderman Gidley is in the middle, with one hand on the model bridge as if blessing it and the other, which the painter has not finished, very near the money-box. Here are Tom Lumley, a painter, in a coat with paint on the elbows; Simon Hulme, the Reeve of Wrayford, grey and careful; and Hal Hulme, the reeve’s son, who writes his father’s papers.

**… (waiting, to Day 2, 12:04 p.m.)**

**› Rise, and move that the dead be commended to the Liberty's charity, at the Liberty's charge, the Company's rights being reserved** · *Day 2, 12:14 p.m.*

You put it as Inchbold would: the dead commended to the Liberty’s charity, at the Liberty’s charge, the Company’s rights in all things being reserved and its loyalty to the lord unquestioned. There is a pause while the Bench looks for what it costs. It costs nothing. Gidley’s face lights up. ‘Charity,’ he says, ‘at the Liberty’s charge,’ and the motion is carried by acclamation, and the Bench congratulates itself at some length on its humanity. Inchbold writes it in. He looks up at you once, with something like professional respect. Sister Elin gains 300 experience: moved a clause the Bench passed.

**… (waiting, to Day 2, 2:14 p.m.)**

**› Lay before the reeve the Bench's leave, the Abbess's claim, and the ruling at the chain** · *Day 2, 2:24 p.m.*

He reads the Abbess’s letter, and the Bench’s clause in Inchbold’s copperplate, and the line in Pinch’s tally, slowly, all three, twice. Then he puts them square on the desk, edge to edge, the way a clerk does. ‘Cover,’ he says. ‘There it is.’ He does not look pleased. He looks like a man who has been let off something and knows it. He calls his constable. ‘Cut them down,’ he says. ‘Give them to the Abbess’s brothers at the chain. They cross as the Liberty’s tithe, by the Bench’s leave.’ And then, to you, so low you barely hear it: ‘Thank you.’

## verdict

**… (waiting, to Day 4, 9:04 a.m.)**

> In Wade's Acre old Corbin has taken his coat off and is cutting turf by the church door, and the sound of the spade carries over the water.
> In Wade's Acre, in the afternoon, the three are buried by the church door, with Wrayford standing round the wall and on the wall and on the roofs of the tan-yards. The Abbess says the office herself.
> Word comes before dawn, the way it comes in a city, from nowhere: the young carters went for the Company's counting-house at midnight with lamp-oil, and Sergeant Gault was waiting in the dark for them, and the fire was out before it was lit, and Kit Pardoe and two others are in the Guildhall cellar.
> THE THIRD DAY. Thunder in the fells before light, a long way off, like carts going over a bridge. The air is thick and yellow and the swallows are flying low over the water. The dogs of Wrayford will not settle. It is the third day; everybody knows it is the third day, and nobody says so.
> At noon a boy comes running over the drawbridge from Overbridge shouting that there are riders on the south road, a long way off, a dozen of them and a woman in grey, and the whole of the Bridge End turns to look south.
> At dusk the captain rides in over the drawbridge with her riders behind her, and does not look up at the gatehouse as she passes under it, and goes to the Guildhall, where the Bench has laid supper.
> THE THIRD NIGHT. The storm comes over the bridge at the curfew bell, and the rain with it, straight down, on the empty rail and on the Acre, where the new turf is darkening by the church door. In Northgate people stand in their doorways and let it rain on them, for no reason they can give.
> THE FOURTH MORNING. Everything is washed: the stones of the Market shine, the river is high and brown and full of sticks, and the air smells of nothing at all for the first time in a week. The captain's riders are saddling in the Bridge End. At nine, the Guildhall.
> The captain finds nothing on the rail but three cut ropes, and a letter from the Abbess claiming her dead, and the Bench's clause in Inchbold's copperplate, and the collector's ruling, and three new graves in Wade's Acre, dug square by old Corbin, who says it is the best work he has done in forty years. She reads the papers at supper with an expression nobody can read. In the morning she gives her verdict, that Wrayford is in order, and goes home with the toll money. The reeve keeps his office. The Company keeps its farm, and has its charity entered in its minutes, at the Liberty's charge. Everyone in Wrayford is a little ashamed, and the rail is empty, and on the whole it is a better morning than the city deserved.
> Kit Pardoe goes south with the captain, tied to a rider's saddle, for the lord's justice at Harbury. He looks back once, at the bridge.
> Lancelot Inchbold finds you in the crowd with a brass token on a ribbon and a paper to sign. 'The freedom of the bridge,' he says. 'By the Bench's resolution of this morning, in recognition of your services to the Company's charity.' He does not smile. 'It is entered in the minutes. Everything is.'
