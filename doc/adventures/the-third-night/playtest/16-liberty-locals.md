## tull

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:30 a.m.*

You set out from the drove road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. Northgate is where the drove roads come into the city: a wide street of yards and stables and inns, horse-dung and straw and the smell of hot iron from the farrier’s, carts drawn up in rows with their shafts on the ground. Nobody is loading anything. The carters are standing about in twos and threes in the yard gates with their hands in their pockets, not talking, and the drovers bringing cattle down to the Shambles go past them very politely.

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 8:40 a.m.*

The Market is a sloping square with a stone cross in the middle where the hiring is done, the Guildhall along the top side with its clock and its arcade, and the Bridge Company’s counting-house next to it, newly painted blue, with a bridge in gold over the door. The Mercer Steps go up behind to the Hill. Down Shambles Lane you can hear cattle, and smell them. On the Cross itself, on every post of the arcade, and on the counting-house door, the same printed sheet is pasted.

**› To Cutler's Lane** · *Day 1, 8:45 a.m.*

Cutler’s Lane: a narrow street of knife-grinders and small shops behind the Market, with the clatter of a press coming from one door and three gilt balls hanging over another.

**› Into Tull's cutlery** · *Day 1, 8:46 a.m.*

A cutler’s shop: knives in rows on green baize, a grindstone, and a woman at a bench putting an edge on a cleaver with great violence. Goodwife Tull, a cutler’s widow, who keeps the shop, is here.

**› Talk to Goodwife Tull** · *Day 1, 8:46 a.m.*

Goodwife Tull does not stop grinding. ‘If you’ve come to tell me where he is, I know where he is.’

**› Ask Goodwife Tull who he is, and where** · *Day 1, 8:46 a.m.*

‘Robin. My boy. Apprenticed to me, articled, sealed, and run off four days ago across the bridge to the bear-garden in Overbridge to be a bear-ward’s boy, because a bear is more interesting than knives.’ The cleaver shrieks on the stone. ‘I can’t go and fetch him. It’s the Liberty; the reeve’s man won’t go, and if I go myself I’ll take the bear-ward’s ears off and then where will I be.’ She holds the cleaver up to the light. ‘Fetch him home. I’ll give you the best knife in the shop.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 8:46 a.m.*

‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Goodwife Tull, who was not trying to.

The brindled dog under the gatehouse lifts his eyes to you, and not his head. The road comes out of the Market into the shadow of the gatehouse, a square tower of red stone over the bridge’s first arch, with the lord’s arms over the arch and the lord’s chain across it, new, the links still bright. Beside the arch, where a house stood, a heap of black timbers. In front of the chain, a booth of planks with a board nailed up beside it, very freshly painted. And up on the tower’s river side, along the parapet of the leads, three shapes in sacking hang against the sky on short ropes, and turn a little when the wind comes up the river, and turn back. A few people stand about at the Bridge End looking up, the way people stop to look at a fire, and then go on. **› To Bridge Street, on the arches** · *Day 2, 9:08 a.m.*

At the chain Amos Pinch takes your farthing. There being no farthings left in Wrayford since the riot, he takes a penny, and writes you a tally-stick for the three farthings he owes you, which he says the Company will honour at Michaelmas. Bridge Street runs out over the river on the arches, three storeys of shops leaning toward each other across a lane so narrow the upper floors nearly meet: a glover, a cook-shop, a seller of spectacles, a seller of Saint Wade’s badges, a tooth-drawer. Between the houses you see the river below through gaps, going by brown and fast. Halfway along, a chapel is built into the middle pier, and beyond it the drawbridge, and beyond that Overbridge and its church. The first house past the gatehouse is the toll-keeper’s, narrow, with a shut door. Its shutters are closed in the middle of the day. The drawbridge is down, its chains slack, and Bridge Street runs on over it to Overbridge. The toll-keeper’s door is shut. Knock, and a woman’s voice inside says, ‘Go away,’ without anger, the way you would say it to the rain.

**› Ask Saul Kemp why the hide is his** · *Day 2, 10:19 a.m.*

‘Beast died in my pens, in the Shambles, in the city,’ says Kemp. ‘I skinned it. It’s mine.’

**› Ask Barnaby Crouch why the hide is his** · *Day 2, 10:19 a.m.*

‘Came over in the offal cart,’ says Crouch. ‘Anything in the offal cart is the tan-yards’ by the Liberty’s privilege. It’s mine.’

**› Examine — Look closely** · *Day 2, 10:21 a.m.*

You look closely at the disputed hide. Unrolled, the hide has two marks on the flank: a fresh nick in the ear, the Shambles’ tally-mark for a beast sold, and under it, older, a drover’s brand burned into the hair: H.O., and a little curlew. Ham Ostler’s. The beast was never sold. It died in the pens and somebody skinned it. It is Joan’s woodcraft that makes it out.

**› Give the hide to neither: it is Ham Ostler's, by his brand** · *Day 2, 10:23 a.m.*

You show her the curlew. The Abbess takes her spectacles off and looks at it for a long time, and then at the butcher, who is looking at the floor, and at the tanner, who is looking at the butcher. ‘A beast that died in the pens before market,’ she says, ‘and was skinned, and sent over the bridge in the offal cart, and argued over by two men who both knew whose it was.’ She puts her spectacles back on. ‘The hide is Ham Ostler’s, and the Liberty is obliged to whoever noticed.’ She writes. ‘That is the first sensible thing anyone has said to me this week.’ Joan gains 150 experience: judged the hide in the Abbess’s court. Joan gains 150 experience: found the hide’s true owner. The hide has gone from the table.

**› To the bear-garden** · *Day 2, 10:34 a.m.*

The bear-garden: a ring of raised benches round a sanded pit, a post in the middle with a chain, and on the chain an old brown she-bear, sitting on her haunches with her forepaws in her lap like a farmer’s wife. A boy of twelve or so is combing the burrs out of her coat, and she lets him. Here are Robin Tull, a cutler’s boy of twelve, with bear-hair on his sleeves; and Sim Clout, the bear-ward, with a strap at his belt.

**› Ask Robin Tull why he will not go home** · *Day 2, 10:34 a.m.*

‘Because Margery won’t dance for anyone else.’ He goes on combing. ‘Not for him. Only me. And if she won’t dance he’ll sell her to the baiting at Harbury at Michaelmas, he says so, and they’ll set dogs on her.’ He looks at the strap on Clout’s belt, and away. ‘So I can’t go home. Can I.’ He combs. ‘I like knives,’ he says, as if it were a confession. ‘I do. I’d rather bears.’

**› Show Robin how a drover settles a beast with a low whistle, so anyone can work her** · *Day 2, 10:54 a.m.*

It is the whistle a drover uses for a nervous cow, low and two notes, and the old bear lifts her head at it. You have Robin teach it to Sim Clout, who is bad at it, and then Clout does it, badly, and Margery gets up on her hind legs and turns round once, as if humouring him. Robin watches with his mouth open. ‘She’ll do it for him now,’ he says. ‘She’ll do it for anyone.’ He sounds, for a moment, bereft. Then he goes home. Joan gains 200 experience: settled the bear-ward’s boy.
