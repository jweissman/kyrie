## home

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:30 a.m.*

You set out from the drove road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. Northgate is where the drove roads come into the city: a wide street of yards and stables and inns, horse-dung and straw and the smell of hot iron from the farrier’s, carts drawn up in rows with their shafts on the ground. Nobody is loading anything. The carters are standing about in twos and threes in the yard gates with their hands in their pockets, not talking, and the drovers bringing cattle down to the Shambles go past them very politely.

**› To Wain Row** · *Day 1, 8:35 a.m.*

Wain Row is a lane of carters’ houses, each with its yard and its cart-shed behind, the doors painted the colours carters paint them, red and green and a blue that was once expensive. Four doors along, the Pardoes’ is red. Its step has been scrubbed this morning, hard. Two women talking on the next step stop talking when they see you, and go in.

**› Into the Pardoe house** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

The kitchen is as it was: the long table, the settle, the bread oven, the hook by the door where your father’s coat hangs, which is the first thing you look at and the last thing you will be able to stop looking at. It smells of bread. It always smelled of bread. Your mother is at the table with her sleeves up and flour to the elbow, and she looks at you in the doorway for a long moment, and then goes on kneading. There is more bread than one woman could eat in a month: loaves on the dresser, on the settle, under cloths on the windowsill. She makes bread when she cannot think. Maudie Pardoe, your mother, is here.

**› Talk to Maudie Pardoe** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

Maudie Pardoe does not stop kneading.

**› Ask your mother what happened** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

‘You got the letter, then.’ She turns the dough and pushes it away from her with the heels of her hands. ‘He went out with the others, the night they broke the chain. He was the oldest of them, he went to see none of the young ones did anything stupid.’ Push, turn. ‘The riders took him on the back road before light. Him and Dunstan Fenner and Joss Wilder. They hanged them off the gatehouse rail at sunrise and the reeve says they stay there till the lord’s captain comes, so she’ll see the city’s in order.’ She stops, with both hands flat in the dough. ‘Simon Hulme. Who stood godfather to your brother. He came and told me himself. I’ll give him that. He came himself.’

**› Ask your mother about Kit** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

‘He was at the chain. He came in at dawn with his hands black and hasn’t said ten words since. He’s at the yards all day doing nothing to the same wheel, and at night he goes off somewhere.’ She looks at the door. ‘Under the bridge, I’d say. He always went under the bridge when his father shouted at him, from when he was six. Go and find him. He’ll talk to you, or he won’t talk to anyone.’

**› Ask Maudie about the third night** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

‘They watch the dead three nights in Wrayford and bury them on the third day. My mother’s mother said if you don’t, they go looking.’ She cuts the dough in two with the edge of her hand. ‘I don’t believe it. I’m telling you so you’ll know I don’t believe it.’ The other half. ‘And I won’t have it. He is not going to be out on that rail a third night with the crows at him and that dog under him. If he’s there when it comes, I’ll go up myself with the bread knife and cut him down, and Simon Hulme can hang me off the rail beside him, and I’d like to see him look at me while he does it.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

‘Be careful,’ says Maudie Pardoe. ‘I know you won’t, but be careful.’

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 8:52 a.m.*

The Market is a sloping square with a stone cross in the middle where the hiring is done, the Guildhall along the top side with its clock and its arcade, and the Bridge Company’s counting-house next to it, newly painted blue, with a bridge in gold over the door. The Mercer Steps go up behind to the Hill. Down Shambles Lane you can hear cattle, and smell them. On the Cross itself, on every post of the arcade, and on the counting-house door, the same printed sheet is pasted.

**› To the Guildhall yard** · *Day 1, 8:53 a.m.*

The Guildhall yard, behind the hall: the city’s fire-cart, a stack of hurdles, and along the wall three carts drawn up with their shafts down and a chalked notice on each: IMPOUNDED. GOODS OF A FELON. Wilder’s is a light cart. Fenner’s is old. A big four-wheeled carter’s wagon with red wheels and PARDOE, WAIN ROW painted on the board, impounded.

**› Examine — Look closely** · *Day 1, 8:55 a.m.*

You look closely at Wat Pardoe’s cart. You go over it: the wheels first, because that is what everyone says. The wheels are clean. A wheel that had gone over a boy would not be clean, and nobody has washed these; there is the fell road’s red mud dried on every spoke. Then the tailboard, and the bed of the cart behind it. There is blood there, a good deal of it, dried brown into the boards, along the length of the bed, where something was laid down and lay a long while. Not where a wheel goes. Where a boy is lifted in, and held.

**› Ask the farrier who was on the box of Wat Pardoe's cart when it came back off the gate** · *Day 1, 9:11 a.m.*

The hammer stops. He looks at the iron for a long time, and puts it back in the fire. ‘I’ll tell you,’ he says, ‘because you’ve looked at that cart, I can see it on you, and you’d find out.’ He works the bellows. ‘The boy. Kit. Wat was at the chain with the old ones. The boy had the reins, and the horse went forward in the press, and he couldn’t hold her.’ The bellows. ‘Then Wat came running and got up and took them off him. They’ll not get the son off me.’ Joan gains 300 experience: found out who was driving.

The Wharf runs along the river west of the bridge: warehouses with hoists, barges tied two deep, eel-traps, nets drying, a smell of tar and river. Downstream the bridge goes over on its arches, and the water piles up white against the cutwaters and goes through roaring.

**› To the bank under the first arches** · *Day 1, 9:38 a.m.*

The bank under the bridge’s first arch: a strip of mud and stones where the city’s boys fish and its drunks sleep, and the great pier going up out of the water beside you into the shadow, green to a man’s height and red stone above. High overhead, the gatehouse parapet. Out from the bank, under the brown water, you can just make out a line of big squared stones going across: the old ford. Halfway over, where two of the ford-stones are gone, the water goes suddenly deep and fast. The Wray runs deep and brown over where the old ford was. Nobody wades it while the mills are grinding.

**› Tell Alderman Gidley who was really driving Wat Pardoe's cart** · *Day 1, 10:13 p.m.*

> At two the Bench rises. The Remonstrance goes to the lord with one clause added at the end, which the clerk read in and nobody opposed: that the dead remain upon the rail until the Company's loss in the riot shall have been reckoned by the lord's captain, the Company's claims being thereby secured. The reeve, it is said, read it in his court with his face like a shut door.
> At noon a boy comes running over the drawbridge from Overbridge shouting that there are riders on the south road, a long way off, a dozen of them and a woman in grey, and the whole of the Bridge End turns to look south.
> At dusk the captain rides in over the drawbridge with her riders behind her, and does not look up at the gatehouse as she passes under it, and goes to the Guildhall, where the Bench has laid supper.
> Word comes out of the Guildhall supper: the Company, having given the lord's justice a live ringleader, has asked the captain's mercy for the dead, as a kindness to the city, and she has granted it, with a look at Alderman Gidley that goes on a little too long. The three come down from the rail by torchlight and are carried over the bridge to Wade's Acre, to wait for morning.
> In Wade's Acre old Corbin has taken his coat off and is cutting turf by the church door, and the sound of the spade carries over the water.
> In Wade's Acre, in the afternoon, the three are buried by the church door, with Wrayford standing round the wall and on the wall and on the roofs of the tan-yards. The Abbess says the office herself.
> In the morning the reeve's men take Kit Pardoe from the yards, because he is Wat's son and the city knows what it would have done in his place, and he does not deny it, which he could have, and goes with them to the cellar with his head up.
> The captain takes Kit Pardoe south with her at noon, tied to a rider's saddle, for the lord's justice at Harbury, which is a rope. The Company, as it promised, asks the captain's mercy for the dead, justice having been done, and she grants it with a look at Alderman Gidley that he will not forget, and the three are buried that afternoon in Wade's Acre. Maudie Pardoe stands at the graveside and does not look at you once. You have forty crowns. The Company prints a ballad of the son who let his father die for him, at a penny, and it sells well.
