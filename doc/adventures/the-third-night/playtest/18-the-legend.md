# Saint Wade, the Michaelmas Burning, and the hymn: the city's past, told in pieces

Campaign `data/campaigns/the-third-night.campaign.json`, seed `wray`, steps `test/fixtures/wrayford/legend.json`. Each step is the label the player chose; under it, what the page said. Waits are folded.

## hill

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:30 a.m.*

You set out from the drove road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. Northgate is where the drove roads come into the city: a wide street of yards and stables and inns, horse-dung and straw and the smell of hot iron from the farrier’s, carts drawn up in rows with their shafts on the ground. Nobody is loading anything. The carters are standing about in twos and threes in the yard gates with their hands in their pockets, not talking, and the drovers bringing cattle down to the Shambles go past them very politely.

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 8:40 a.m.*

The Market is a sloping square with a stone cross in the middle where the hiring is done, the Guildhall along the top side with its clock and its arcade, and the Bridge Company’s counting-house next to it, newly painted blue, with a bridge in gold over the door. The Mercer Steps go up behind to the Hill. Down Shambles Lane you can hear cattle, and smell them. On the Cross itself, on every post of the arcade, and on the counting-house door, the same printed sheet is pasted.

**… (waiting, to Day 1, 9:00 a.m.)**

**› Into the Guildhall** · *Day 1, 9:01 a.m.*

The Guildhall’s long hall: a hammer-beam roof, the city’s arms in plaster, a long table on a dais where the Bench sits, and at the far end the reeve’s chair and his clerk’s desk, where he keeps court. Along one wall hang the portraits of fifty years of the Bridge Company, Masters and Wardens, in blue gowns, each more prosperous than the last. An easel stands at the end of the row. On the easel, the present Bench of the Bridge Company, five aldermen in blue gowns round a table with a model of the bridge on it, blocked in and half painted. It is extremely good. Alderman Gidley is in the middle, with one hand on the model bridge as if blessing it and the other, which the painter has not finished, very near the money-box. Here are Tom Lumley, a painter, in a coat with paint on the elbows; Simon Hulme, the Reeve of Wrayford, grey and careful; and Hal Hulme, the reeve’s son, who writes his father’s papers.

**› Talk to Simon Hulme** · *Day 1, 9:01 a.m.*

Simon Hulme looks up, and you watch him decide, in the space of a breath, to be the reeve and not anything else.

**› Ask the reeve why the dead must stay on the rail** · *Day 1, 9:01 a.m.*

‘Because the captain comes at dusk on the third day,’ says Simon Hulme, ‘and asks one question: is Wrayford in rebellion. If she finds the city quiet and its ringleaders on the rail, she takes the toll money and goes home. If she finds it in rebellion, the lord’s law is ten hanged for every rider hurt and the carters’ row burned to the stones.’ He says it as if he had said it many times, to himself. ‘Three men on a rail, or thirty. I have kept this city twenty years.’ His hand is flat on the desk. ‘I did not hang them. I would have brought them to the lord’s court. The riders carried a warrant I was not shown.’ He looks at you for a long time. ‘You were in my house every day of your childhood,’ he says. ‘You and Hal. Tell me, then. You’ve come home and found your father on the rail, and you’re the only one in this city who can say anything to me I’d listen to.’ His voice does not rise. ‘What would you have me do?’

**› Ask the reeve whether he has seen a captain's verdict before** · *Day 1, 9:01 a.m.*

He is quiet a long time. ‘Michaelmas fair,’ he says. ‘I was twelve. A clerk’s boy, my father’s. He sent me to the Bridge End with a message and I stayed to watch, because I was twelve.’ His hand moves on the desk as if it were writing. ‘They used the drawbridge chains. Ten. I counted. I was a clerk’s boy, I counted everything. And then the wind went round to the south and they fired Water Lane, and the people in the end houses would not come out, because they were the families of the ten.’ He looks at the decree on the wall in his son’s hand. ‘I can smell it now. Thirty-one years. So when you ask me why three men stay on that rail, that is why. Three is not ten. And nobody is in Water Lane.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 9:01 a.m.*

Simon Hulme lifts a hand, once.

**› Out to the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 9:02 a.m.*

The Market Cross: the Guildhall clock, the blue counting-house, the sheets on every post.

## bridge

**› To the Bridge End, under the gatehouse** · *Day 1, 9:07 a.m.*

The brindled dog under the gatehouse lifts his eyes to you, and not his head. Under the gatehouse wall, directly below the rail, a brindled dog lies with his chin on his paws, as close to the wall as he can get, and does not move when people step over him. The road comes out of the Market into the shadow of the gatehouse, a square tower of red stone over the bridge’s first arch, with the lord’s arms over the arch and the lord’s chain across it, new, the links still bright. Beside the arch, where a house stood, a heap of black timbers. In front of the chain, a booth of planks with a board nailed up beside it, very freshly painted. And up on the tower’s river side, along the parapet of the leads, three shapes in sacking hang against the sky on short ropes, and turn a little when the wind comes up the river, and turn back. A few people stand about at the Bridge End looking up, the way people stop to look at a fire, and then go on. A paper is nailed to the gate-ward’s door in the tower, in a clerk’s round hand with the reeve’s seal at the foot. Annis Pye, a girl with a tray of pies on a strap round her neck, is doing good business with the crowd. She has her back to the gatehouse. Pinch’s tally lies open on the ledge of the booth: a column of entries in ink, very neat, and beside every few lines, so faint you would not see it unless the sun were on the page, a small mark in pencil, like a tick against a sum he is keeping somewhere else. The gate-ward’s door into the tower is shut and locked. Here are Bramble, a carter’s dog, brindled, grey in the muzzle; Amos Pinch, the Bridge Company’s collector, in a new black coat; Annis Pye, a pie-seller at the Bridge End, with a tray on a strap; and Alderman Prosper Gidley, the Master of the Worshipful Company of the Bridge, in a blue gown.

**› To Bridge Street, on the arches** · *Day 1, 9:08 a.m.*

At the chain Amos Pinch takes your farthing. There being no farthings left in Wrayford since the riot, he takes a penny, and writes you a tally-stick for the three farthings he owes you, which he says the Company will honour at Michaelmas. Bridge Street runs out over the river on the arches, three storeys of shops leaning toward each other across a lane so narrow the upper floors nearly meet: a glover, a cook-shop, a seller of spectacles, a seller of Saint Wade’s badges, a tooth-drawer. Between the houses you see the river below through gaps, going by brown and fast. Halfway along, a chapel is built into the middle pier, and beyond it the drawbridge, and beyond that Overbridge and its church. The first house past the gatehouse is the toll-keeper’s, narrow, with a shut door. Its shutters are closed in the middle of the day. On a tray outside the badge-seller’s, tin Saint Wades at a farthing each: a stooping man up to his knees in tin water, with a child on his shoulders. ‘Carry me over,’ says the badge-seller to nobody, the way a fishwife says ‘fresh’. The drawbridge is down. The toll-keeper’s door is shut. Knock, and a woman’s voice inside says, ‘Go away,’ without anger, the way you would say it to the rain.

**› Into Saint Wade's chapel on the bridge** · *Day 1, 9:09 a.m.*

A small chapel of grey stone built into the pier, so that the river is loud through the floor. Candles. A trapdoor in the floor, propped open, with a ladder going down into the dark and the noise of water. Over the altar, the old painting: Saint Wade up to his chest in a black river, bent nearly double under a grey child, looking down at his own feet, where the painter has made the riverbed darker in one place than anywhere else. Under it, scratched into the plaster by many hands at many times, the same three words: CARRY ME OVER.

**› Out to Bridge Street, on the arches** · *Day 1, 9:10 a.m.*

The drawbridge is down. The toll-keeper’s door is shut. Knock, and a woman’s voice inside says, ‘Go away,’ without anger, the way you would say it to the rain. Bridge Street on the arches: the leaning shops, the gaps with the river in them, the chapel on the middle pier.

**› To Overbridge Street** · *Day 1, 9:20 a.m.*

Overbridge Street comes off the drawbridge into another town: older, lower, poorer and louder, timber houses with their upper floors leaning on their neighbours, the tan-yards stinking at one end and the bear-garden roaring at the other, and in the middle Saint Wade’s, a squat old church with a tower, and the Abbess’s lodging behind a wall. A painted post at the bridge foot says THE LIBERTY OF SAINT WADE. The reeve’s writ ends here, and everybody walks a little differently. One lane off Overbridge Street is all of a piece, raw brick, every house the same, built in one year: the Liberty calls it the New Row, though it is thirty years old, and nobody under forty knows why it was needed. The drawbridge is down.

**› Into Saint Wade's** · *Day 1, 9:21 a.m.*

Saint Wade’s: a low, old, whitewashed church, cold even in this heat, smelling of damp stone and tallow. Every pew has a carved wheel on its end, for the carters, and on the oldest pews the wheels have been touched smooth. The saint over the chancel arch is newer than the church: the Company paid to have him repainted in the year the bridge was opened, and he is up to his ankles in a blue stream, smiling, with a rosy child sitting up on his shoulders like a lord at a fair.

**› Out to Wade's Acre** · *Day 1, 9:22 a.m.*

Wade’s Acre: the burying ground of Wrayford since there was a Wrayford, between the church and the river, gravestones leaning every way like a crowd looking at something. By the north wall, apart from the rest, ten small stones in a row, all the same size, all with the same date and no names, and moss on all of them except where somebody keeps it scraped off the dates. By the church door, three graves’ worth of turf marked out with string and pegs, uncut. Corbin, the sexton of Saint Wade’s, old as his spade, is here.

**› Talk to Corbin** · *Day 1, 9:22 a.m.*

Corbin looks at you from his seat on the Acre wall without getting up. ‘Not dead, are you,’ he says. ‘No business of mine, then.’

**› Ask Corbin what the dead go looking for** · *Day 1, 9:22 a.m.*

He takes the pipe out of his mouth. ‘Folk think it’s vengeance. It’s not vengeance.’ He looks across the water at the gatehouse. ‘They go looking for a back. Someone to carry them over, the way Wade did. That’s all the dead ever want of you, a back and a hole.’ He puts the pipe back. ‘I’ve the hole. I’ll not dig it while they’re up there for show. Somebody else can find the back.’

**› Ask Corbin about the ten stones by the north wall** · *Day 1, 9:22 a.m.*

‘I dug them.’ He does not look round at the north wall. ‘I was nineteen and my father was sexton and he couldn’t, after the third. So I did the other seven. In the rain, it was, it rained all that week.’ He knocks his pipe out on the wall. ‘And then forty years of ordinary dead, which is the best work there is. Old women and drowned boys and babies.’ He looks at you. ‘That’s why I’ll not dig for show. I’ve dug for a captain once.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 9:22 a.m.*

‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Corbin, who was not trying to.

**› Into Saint Wade's** · *Day 1, 9:23 a.m.*

Saint Wade’s: whitewash, the cheerful saint, the carved wheels on the pews.

**› Out to Overbridge Street** · *Day 1, 9:24 a.m.*

The drawbridge is down. Overbridge Street: leaning houses, the church, the post that says THE LIBERTY OF SAINT WADE.

**› Into the Abbess's lodging** · *Day 1, 9:25 a.m.*

The Abbess’s court-room: a long room with a dais, a chair under a canopy, a table with a green cloth, and on the walls the charters of the Liberty, framed, all of them, from the oldest, which is a scrap of vellum with a seal like a biscuit, to the newest, which is a letter from the lord’s steward that she has had framed upside down. On the green cloth, rolled and tied: a raw cowhide, red side out, with a ticket on it in the Abbess’s hand: KEMP v. CROUCH. Dame Isobel Crane, the Lady Abbess of Saint Wade’s, who holds the Liberty of Overbridge, is here.

**› Talk to Dame Isobel Crane** · *Day 1, 9:25 a.m.*

The Abbess looks at you over a pair of spectacles. ‘Sit,’ she says. ‘Or stand. Only be brief, and be accurate.’

**› Ask the Abbess who Saint Wade was** · *Day 1, 9:25 a.m.*

‘A ferryman.’ She does not look up from her letter. ‘A ferryman without a ferry, who carried people over the Wray on his back for nothing, which is the first thing about him the Company has forgotten.’ She sands the page. ‘On the third night after the great flood a drowned child knocked at his door and asked to be carried over to be buried. Halfway, at the deep place, the child grew heavy: as heavy as all the dead of the vale, the old life says, which is a theologian’s way of saying very. Wade went down to his chin at the Step that has his name and did not set him down.’ She looks up. ‘That is what WE BEAR THE CROSSING means, on a counting-house. My predecessors objected to the motto in writing. It is framed, behind you, upside down.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 9:25 a.m.*

‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Dame Isobel Crane, who was not trying to.

**› Out to Overbridge Street** · *Day 1, 9:26 a.m.*

Overbridge Street: leaning houses, the church, the post that says THE LIBERTY OF SAINT WADE. The drawbridge is down.

**› To Bridge Street, on the arches** · *Day 1, 9:36 a.m.*

Bridge Street on the arches: the leaning shops, the gaps with the river in them, the chapel on the middle pier. The drawbridge is down. The toll-keeper’s door is shut. Knock, and a woman’s voice inside says, ‘Go away,’ without anger, the way you would say it to the rain.

## wheels

**› Into the Nine Wheels** · *Day 1, 9:53 a.m.*

The carters’ inn: a long low room with nine cart-wheels nailed to the beams for a ceiling, one for every carter the fell road has killed since the inn was built, the oldest black with a century of smoke and the newest with its paint still showing, smoke, a fire with a kettle, benches worn into the shape of carters. Over the settle by the fire someone has hung a black ribbon on a nail, and under it nobody sits. Pell Saxby, who keeps the Nine Wheels, is here.

**… (waiting, to Day 1, 7:13 p.m.)**

**› Talk to Pell Saxby** · *Day 1, 7:13 p.m.*

Pell Saxby wipes her hands on her apron and looks you over. ‘Bed, beer or bread,’ she says, and then, because she is honest: ‘The bread’s Maudie Pardoe’s. She’s sent us forty loaves since Tuesday. I can’t sell it and I can’t stop her.’

**› Ask Pell about the wheels in the beams** · *Day 1, 7:13 p.m.*

‘One for every carter the fell road’s had.’ She does not look up. ‘Fallen off the drop at Scar Top, mostly, or the ice, or a wheel going on the bad bend above the shielings. The first’s from my grandmother’s time.’ She looks up at them. ‘There’s no wheel for being hanged. There’s never been need of one.’ She wipes the bar. ‘There will be now. The carters have already asked me for the nails.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 7:13 p.m.*

‘Off you go, then,’ says Pell Saxby, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

**› Sit down with Sergeant Gault and buy his third jug** · *Day 1, 8:13 p.m.*

He looks at you and the jug and says, ‘Suit yourself.’ He drinks. He talks about horses, and a woman in Harbury, and the cold on the fell road at four in the morning, and how carters are all the same, and in the end, looking into the jug, how he has hanged eleven men in nineteen years and these were the first who went quiet. ‘The big one sang,’ he says. ‘On the road. Some hymn. Carry me over, something, carry me over.’ He looks into the jug. ‘Till the rope.’

**› Talk to Sergeant Gault** · *Day 1, 8:13 p.m.*

Sergeant Gault looks at you over the brazier the way a dog looks at another dog, without much interest either way.

**› Ask Gault about the captain who is coming** · *Day 1, 8:13 p.m.*

‘Sefton.’ He turns the cup. ‘Old Ralf Sefton’s girl. He burned half a street on this river before I was in breeches. She was with him, twelve years old, holding the horses.’ He drinks. ‘She’s been trying not to be him for thirty years. She’s a fair captain. Fair’s not the same as kind. If she has to choose between the old man’s way and getting it wrong, she’ll get it wrong his way, and hate it.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 8:13 p.m.*

Sergeant Gault lifts a hand, once.

**› Out to Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:14 p.m.*

Northgate: the yards, the stables, the carters standing in the gateways.

**› To Wain Row** · *Day 1, 8:19 p.m.*

Wain Row is a lane of carters’ houses, each with its yard and its cart-shed behind, the doors painted the colours carters paint them, red and green and a blue that was once expensive. Four doors along, the Pardoes’ is red. Its step has been scrubbed this morning, hard. Two women talking on the next step stop talking when they see you, and go in.

**› Into the Pardoe house** · *Day 1, 8:20 p.m.*

The kitchen is as it was: the long table, the settle, the bread oven, the hook by the door where your father’s coat hangs, which is the first thing you look at and the last thing you will be able to stop looking at. It smells of bread. It always smelled of bread. Your mother is at the table with her sleeves up and flour to the elbow, and she looks at you in the doorway for a long moment, and then goes on kneading. There is more bread than one woman could eat in a month: loaves on the dresser, on the settle, under cloths on the windowsill. She makes bread when she cannot think. Maudie Pardoe, your mother, is here.

**› Talk to Maudie Pardoe** · *Day 1, 8:20 p.m.*

Maudie Pardoe does not stop kneading.

**› Tell your mother that he sang on the road** · *Day 1, 8:20 p.m.*

Her hands stop in the dough. ‘Carry me over,’ she says. It is not a question. ‘He sang it to you when you were small, on the box, coming down off the fells in the dark, when you were frightened of the drop. Carry me over, Wade.’ She stands with her floury hands held away from her apron. ‘He was never frightened of anything in his life. He sang it for you.’ Then she goes back to the dough, and works it very hard, and does not say anything else for a long time.

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 8:20 p.m.*

‘Be careful,’ says Maudie Pardoe. ‘I know you won’t, but be careful.’

## supper

**… (waiting, to Day 3, 7:20 p.m.)**

> THE SECOND DAY. The heat comes up off the river before the sun is over the fells, and by seven the Market smells of dung and hot stone and, when the wind is from the bridge, of something sweeter underneath that nobody mentions. The crows have found the rail. Somewhere upstream Dunch's mill-wheels are turning; the whole city can hear them when the bell stops.
> At two the Bench rises. The Remonstrance goes to the lord with one clause added at the end, which the clerk read in and nobody opposed: that the dead remain upon the rail until the Company's loss in the riot shall have been reckoned by the lord's captain, the Company's claims being thereby secured. The reeve, it is said, read it in his court with his face like a shut door.
> Word comes before dawn, the way it comes in a city, from nowhere: the young carters went for the Company's counting-house at midnight with lamp-oil, and Sergeant Gault was waiting in the dark for them, and the fire was out before it was lit, and Kit Pardoe and two others are in the Guildhall cellar.
> THE THIRD DAY. Thunder in the fells before light, a long way off, like carts going over a bridge. The air is thick and yellow and the swallows are flying low over the water. The dogs of Wrayford will not settle. It is the third day; everybody knows it is the third day, and nobody says so.
> At noon a boy comes running over the drawbridge from Overbridge shouting that there are riders on the south road, a long way off, a dozen of them and a woman in grey, and the whole of the Bridge End turns to look south.
> At dusk the captain rides in over the drawbridge with her riders behind her, and does not look up at the gatehouse as she passes under it, and goes to the Guildhall, where the Bench has laid supper.

Wain Row: cart-sheds, painted doors, the Pardoes’ red one with its scrubbed step.

The Market Cross: the Guildhall clock, the blue counting-house, the sheets on every post. Over the fells to the north the sky is the colour of a bruise, and the Guildhall clock looks very white against it.

**› Into the Guildhall** · *Day 3, 7:37 p.m.*

On the easel, the present Bench of the Bridge Company, five aldermen in blue gowns round a table with a model of the bridge on it, blocked in and half painted. It is extremely good. Alderman Gidley is in the middle, with one hand on the model bridge as if blessing it and the other, which the painter has not finished, very near the money-box. Captain Lys Sefton, the lord’s captain, in a grey riding-coat with the lord’s badge, is here. The Guildhall’s long hall: the Bench’s table, the reeve’s chair, the row of blue-gowned portraits and the easel at the end. The Bench’s supper for the captain: candles down the long table, the aldermen in their best gowns, a swan nobody is eating, and at the head of the table a woman in a grey riding-coat with the road’s dust still on it, eating bread and cheese and listening to Gidley with no expression at all. The reeve sits at her right hand and does not eat.

**› Talk to Captain Lys Sefton** · *Day 3, 7:37 p.m.*

Captain Sefton looks at you down the length of the table, past the aldermen. ‘You,’ she says. ‘You are the only person in this hall I have not been told what to think of. Is Wrayford in rebellion?’ The table goes quiet. Gidley opens his mouth, and she lifts one finger without looking at him, and he shuts it. Captain Sefton looks at you as if you were a road she had to decide whether to take.

**› Ask the captain whether she remembers Water Lane** · *Day 3, 7:37 p.m.*

For the first time something moves in her face. ‘I held the horses,’ she says. ‘At the Bridge End. My father’s grey, and his second’s, and they would not stand for the smoke.’ She puts her cup down very exactly. ‘He wrote in his report that Wrayford was pacified. He was right. It has been pacified for thirty-one years.’ She looks down the table at the Bench, who are not listening. ‘I have the same warrant he had, and the same law. I had rather not have the same report.’ Joan gains 300 experience: spoke to the captain for Wrayford.

**› Take your leave** · *Day 3, 7:37 p.m.*

Captain Lys Sefton lifts a hand, once.
