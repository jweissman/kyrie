## day1

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:30 a.m.*

You set out from the drove road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. Northgate is where the drove roads come into the city: a wide street of yards and stables and inns, horse-dung and straw and the smell of hot iron from the farrier’s, carts drawn up in rows with their shafts on the ground. Nobody is loading anything. The carters are standing about in twos and threes in the yard gates with their hands in their pockets, not talking, and the drovers bringing cattle down to the Shambles go past them very politely.

**› To Wain Row** · *Day 1, 8:35 a.m.*

Wain Row is a lane of carters’ houses, each with its yard and its cart-shed behind, the doors painted the colours carters paint them, red and green and a blue that was once expensive. Four doors along, the Pardoes’ is red. Its step has been scrubbed this morning, hard. Two women talking on the next step stop talking when they see you, and go in.

**› Into the Pardoe house** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

The kitchen is as it was: the long table, the settle, the bread oven, the hook by the door where your father’s coat hangs, which is the first thing you look at and the last thing you will be able to stop looking at. It smells of bread. It always smelled of bread. Your mother is at the table with her sleeves up and flour to the elbow, and she looks at you in the doorway for a long moment, and then goes on kneading. There is more bread than one woman could eat in a month: loaves on the dresser, on the settle, under cloths on the windowsill. She makes bread when she cannot think. Maudie Pardoe, your mother, is here.

**› Talk to Maudie Pardoe** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

Maudie Pardoe does not stop kneading.

**› Ask your mother what happened** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

‘You got the letter, then.’ She turns the dough and pushes it away from her with the heels of her hands. ‘He went out with the others, the night they broke the chain. He was the oldest of them, he went to see none of the young ones did anything stupid.’ Push, turn. ‘The riders took him on the back road before light. Him and Dunstan Fenner and Joss Wilder. They hanged them off the gatehouse rail at sunrise and the reeve says they stay there till the lord’s captain comes, so she’ll see the city’s in order.’ She stops, with both hands flat in the dough. ‘Simon Hulme. Who stood godfather to your brother. He came and told me himself. I’ll give him that. He came himself.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

‘Be careful,’ says Maudie Pardoe. ‘I know you won’t, but be careful.’

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:42 a.m.*

**› To the Market Cross** · *Day 1, 8:52 a.m.*

The Market is a sloping square with a stone cross in the middle where the hiring is done, the Guildhall along the top side with its clock and its arcade, and the Bridge Company’s counting-house next to it, newly painted blue, with a bridge in gold over the door. The Mercer Steps go up behind to the Hill. Down Shambles Lane you can hear cattle, and smell them. On the Cross itself, on every post of the arcade, and on the counting-house door, the same printed sheet is pasted.

**› To the Shambles** · *Day 1, 8:57 a.m.*

The Shambles: a steep lane of butchers’ shops with their shutters down for counters, blood in the gutters, flies, drovers’ beasts in hurdle pens at the bottom, and at the top a cart waiting to take the day’s leavings over the bridge to the tan-yards. The Liberty’s offal cart: a tumbril with high sides, loaded to the brim with the Shambles’ leavings under a dripping tarpaulin. Here are Saul Kemp, a butcher, master of the Shambles; Jack Kemp, the butcher’s boy, who drives the offal cart; and Ham Ostler, a drover from the fells, in a smock and a hat with a curlew feather.

**› To the Wharf** · *Day 1, 8:59 a.m.*

The Wharf runs along the river west of the bridge: warehouses with hoists, barges tied two deep, eel-traps, nets drying, a smell of tar and river. Downstream the bridge goes over on its arches, and the water piles up white against the cutwaters and goes through roaring.

**› To the ferry-stage** · *Day 1, 9:04 a.m.*

The ferry-stage: a jetty of black piles with a ferry-boat tied to it, broad and old and very well kept, and a hut with a stove-pipe. A painted board: MUDD’S FERRY. BY CHARTER. OVER THE WRAY SINCE BEFORE THAT BRIDGE. ONE PENNY. And under it, smaller and newer: NO FARTHINGS. Dorcas Mudd, the mistress of the ferrymen’s company, broad as her boat, is here.

**› Talk to Dorcas Mudd** · *Day 1, 9:04 a.m.*

Dorcas Mudd looks you up and down from the stern of her boat. ‘Over?’ she says. ‘A penny. Not a farthing. I’m not a bridge.’

**› Ask Dorcas whether she would carry the dead over the river** · *Day 1, 9:04 a.m.*

‘For nothing,’ she says at once. ‘Gladly. At night, under the rail, if somebody’ll get them down to me. I’d row the three of them to the Acre steps and spit on the bridge as I went.’ Then her face shuts. ‘When that old fool at the Acre asks me himself. Corbin. Forty years he’s not asked me for anything, and he’ll not start now, and I’ll not row his dead for him till he does.’ She coils a rope very hard. ‘He won’t.’

**› Take your leave** · *Day 1, 9:04 a.m.*

**› Out to Wade's Acre** · *Day 1, 9:28 a.m.*

Not for the toll-keeper’s boy, God rest him. Not for Gidley’s aunt, God rest her, at treble fees.’ He looks at you. ‘Let them feel it. Let the Hill smell it.’
