## home

**› To Northgate** · *Day 1, 8:30 a.m.*

You set out from the drove road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. Northgate is where the drove roads come into the city: a wide street of yards and stables and inns, horse-dung and straw and the smell of hot iron from the farrier’s, carts drawn up in rows with their shafts on the ground. Nobody is loading anything. The carters are standing about in twos and threes in the yard gates with their hands in their pockets, not talking, and the drovers bringing cattle down to the Shambles go past them very politely.

**› To Wain Row** · *Day 1, 8:35 a.m.*

Wain Row is a lane of carters’ houses, each with its yard and its cart-shed behind, the doors painted the colours carters paint them, red and green and a blue that was once expensive. Four doors along, the Pardoes’ is red. Its step has been scrubbed this morning, hard. Two women talking on the next step stop talking when they see you, and go in.

**› Into the Pardoe house** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

The kitchen is as it was: the long table, the settle, the bread oven, the hook by the door where your father’s coat hangs, which is the first thing you look at and the last thing you will be able to stop looking at. It smells of bread. It always smelled of bread. Your mother is at the table with her sleeves up and flour to the elbow, and she looks at you in the doorway for a long moment, and then goes on kneading. There is more bread than one woman could eat in a month: loaves on the dresser, on the settle, under cloths on the windowsill. She makes bread when she cannot think. Maudie Pardoe, your mother, is here.

**› Talk to Maudie Pardoe** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

**› Ask your mother what happened** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

He was the oldest of them, he went to see none of the young ones did anything stupid.’ Push, turn. ‘The riders took him on the back road before light. Him and Dunstan Fenner and Joss Wilder. **› Ask your mother about Kit** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

‘He was at the chain. He came in at dawn with his hands black and hasn’t said ten words since. He’s at the yards all day doing nothing to the same wheel, and at night he goes off somewhere.’ She looks at the door. ‘Under the bridge, I’d say. He always went under the bridge when his father shouted at him, from when he was six. Go and find him. He’ll talk to you, or he won’t talk to anyone.’

**› Ask Maudie about the third night** · *Day 1, 8:36 a.m.*

My mother’s mother said if you don’t, they go looking.’ She cuts the dough in two with the edge of her hand. ‘I don’t believe it. You walk with her the length of Bridge Street and up the Cartway and down Wain Row, and people stop in their doorways to watch the toll-keeper’s widow go to the carter’s widow’s door. Maudie Pardoe opens it with flour on her hands. The two women look at each other. Then Maudie stands aside, and Jennet goes in, and the door shuts, and you are not asked in, and you do not ask. After an hour Maudie comes to the door and says to you, quite briskly, as if arranging a wash-day: ‘In the morning. Both of them. Over the bridge, in the light, with the city behind us. And let them stop us.’ Joan and Kit Pardoe each gain 500 experience: brought the two widows together. Wain Row in the dark. A lamp in the Pardoes’ kitchen, and in no other window.

## walk

**… (waiting, to Day 2, 12:44 p.m.)**

> THE SECOND DAY. The heat comes up off the river before the sun is over the fells, and by seven the Market smells of dung and hot stone and, when the wind is from the bridge, of something sweeter underneath that nobody mentions. The crows have found the rail. Somewhere upstream Dunch's mill-wheels are turning; the whole city can hear them when the bell stops.
> In the morning Maudie Pardoe and Jennet Hobday walk down the Cartway together to the Bridge End, arm in arm, in black, and behind them comes Wrayford: Wain Row and Northgate, the yards, the Shambles, the fish-wives from the Wharf, Annis Pye with her fist shut, Win Fenner with four children, people from the Hill with their hats in their hands, so many that the Bridge End will not hold them and they stand back up the Cartway to the Cross. At the gate-ward's door Sergeant Gault looks at them for a long time. Then he takes his key off his belt and unlocks the door himself, and stands aside, and says to his riders, 'Let them be.' The toll-keeper's widow goes up first. When the three come down from the rail it is into the arms of the city, and they cross the bridge on carters' shoulders, and Amos Pinch, at the chain, lifts it without a word, and does not write anything down. Behind them, carried on a hurdle, comes Ned Hobday. Halfway over, by the chapel on the middle pier, somebody starts the carters' hymn, and then the whole bridge is singing it, badly, in the heat: carry me over, Wade, carry me over.
> In Wade's Acre old Corbin has taken his coat off and is cutting turf by the church door, and the sound of the spade carries over the water.
> In Wade's Acre, in the afternoon, the three are buried by the church door, with Wrayford standing round the wall and on the wall and on the roofs of the tan-yards. The Abbess says the office herself.

## verdict

**… (waiting, to Day 4, 9:04 a.m.)**

> In the evening the reeve of Wrayford takes off his chain of office in the Guildhall before the Bench, and lays it on the long table, and goes home.
> THE THIRD DAY. Thunder in the fells before light, a long way off, like carts going over a bridge. The air is thick and yellow and the swallows are flying low over the water. The dogs of Wrayford will not settle. It is the third day; everybody knows it is the third day, and nobody says so.
> At noon a boy comes running over the drawbridge from Overbridge shouting that there are riders on the south road, a long way off, a dozen of them and a woman in grey, and the whole of the Bridge End turns to look south.
> At dusk the captain rides in over the drawbridge with her riders behind her, and does not look up at the gatehouse as she passes under it, and goes to the Guildhall, where the Bench has laid supper.
> THE THIRD NIGHT. The storm comes over the bridge at the curfew bell, and the rain with it, straight down, on the empty rail and on the Acre, where the new turf is darkening by the church door. In Northgate people stand in their doorways and let it rain on them, for no reason they can give.
> THE FOURTH MORNING. Everything is washed: the stones of the Market shine, the river is high and brown and full of sticks, and the air smells of nothing at all for the first time in a week. The captain's riders are saddling in the Bridge End. At nine, the Guildhall.
> When the captain comes up the south road at dusk on the third day there is nothing on the rail. There is a funeral: four new graves by the church door in Wade's Acre, the toll-keeper's boy among them, carried over the bridge on the city's shoulders, the way Wade carried the child, and the whole of Wrayford still standing about in the Liberty in its black, not going home. She looks at it a long time from her horse. In the morning she gives her verdict in one line: that the city is in order. She takes the toll money and goes home. The toll stands; the Company keeps its farm; Simon Hulme is not reeve any more. On Bridge Street, in the toll-keeper's house, Jennet Hobday has opened her shutters, and Maudie Pardoe comes on Sundays, with bread.
> Kit Pardoe goes back to the yards, and to his father's place on the box, and the horse that went forward in the press, which he will not let anyone else drive.

*(the story ends)*
