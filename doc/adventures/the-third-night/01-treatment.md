# The Third Night — the first treatment

*Stage 1 of the commission. Written 2026-10-26, before re-reading the engine for this purpose: what the work wants to
be, not yet what Kyr finds easy. Later documents record where and why it changed; this one is not edited after the
fact except for typos. Spoilers throughout.*

## 0. From the proposal to the commission

The repertoire survey ([../repertoire-2026-10-26.md](../repertoire-2026-10-26.md) §7.1) proposed *The Third Night* as a
small tragedy: one town, three days, nothing to discover. The commission asks for something larger: a metropolitan
region, many small interconnected adventures, an overarching mystery, and satire or romance. These pull against the
proposal, and the treatment has to decide how.

**The decision.** The tragedy stays the spine and keeps its rule: *nothing discovered changes what must be decided.*
Three men hang on the bridge whatever the protagonist learns; the mother will go on the third night whatever the
protagonist proves. Around that spine the commission's other wishes are given room:

- **The city** is a real one: a bridge-city of carters, drovers, aldermen, ferrymen, butchers and printers, where the
  hanging is also a spectacle, a commercial opportunity, a legal puzzle and a political embarrassment. Most of its
  stories have nothing to do with the dead.
- **The mystery** is human and local: *what happened at the toll-house on the night of the rising.* Three questions,
  three answers, each a person the protagonist knows: who was driving the cart, who told the riders, who set the fire.
  None of the answers takes a body off the bridge. Each changes what someone will do.
- **The satire** is of a city's respectable men: the company of aldermen who farm the lord's toll while petitioning
  the lord against it. It is played as games, not speeches: a toll schedule to argue, a remonstrance to amend, a
  portrait to finish, a broadside to write.
- **The romance** is three courtships at three ages: the protagonist and their oldest friend, the reeve's son, who
  has a secret; a pie-seller and the dead boy she was to marry; and the sexton and the ferry-woman, who have hated
  each other on principle for forty years.

## 1. The one paragraph

Wrayford is a city at a bridge. The drove roads of the north meet there to cross the Wray by nineteen stone arches
with a street of houses on them. This spring the lord, who lives elsewhere, put a toll on the bridge and sold the
farm of it to the city's own aldermen. Four nights ago the carters rose, burned the toll-house and broke the chain. In
the press the toll-keeper's boy went under a cart. The lord's riders took three carters on the back road before dawn
and hanged them from the gatehouse rail under the lord's warrant. The reeve, who has kept the peace for twenty years,
has decreed that they stay there until the lord's captain comes, so that she finds Wrayford in order and not in
rebellion; whoever touches them will hang beside them. The protagonist comes home that morning because their mother
wrote that their father went out on the road with the cart and did not come home. He is the one in the middle.

## 2. The city

Wrayford is built on both banks of a broad, brown, tidal-feeling river (it is not tidal; it floods). The north bank is
the city proper, under the reeve and the Bench of aldermen. The south bank, Overbridge, is the Liberty of Saint Wade's,
which has been the Abbess's since before the bridge, and where the reeve's writ does not run. The bridge joins them.
Its north half is the city's; its gatehouse, at the south end, is the lord's.

### Districts

**Northgate** is where the carters live: yards, stables, wheelwrights, a horse-pond, smoke. Wain Row is a lane of
carters' houses with their yards behind; the Pardoe house is the fourth. The Nine Wheels is the carters' inn, where
the riders' sergeant drinks because nobody will serve him anywhere else and the Nine Wheels' landlady considers
refusal beneath her. The Wainwrights' hall is the carters' fellowship, its old men and its young men no longer
agreeing about anything.

**The Market** is the city's centre: the Market Cross, the Guildhall where the Bench sits and the reeve keeps court,
the Shambles (butchers, offal, flies, drovers' beasts in pens), the Corn Exchange, and the Bridge Company's
counting-house, newly painted. Ezra Quoin's print shop is in Cutler's Lane behind it.

**Mercer Hill** is where the money lives: the reeve's house, Alderman Prosper Gidley's house with its ice-house dug
into the hill, the other aldermen's houses, gardens, a view of the river and of the gatehouse, which the aldermen's
wives have asked to have screened.

**The Wharf** is the waterfront west of the bridge: wharves, warehouses, the fish-stair, and Dorcas Mudd's
ferry-stage. Before the bridge, the ferry was the crossing; the ferrymen's company still has its charter and two
boats. Upstream is the mill-weir; under the bridge, at low water, the old ford-stones show, which is where the city got
its name and which nobody has used in two hundred years.

**The Bridge** is a district in itself: Bridge Street on the arches, three storeys of shops leaning over the water,
with the drawbridge span in the middle and Saint Wade's chapel built into the middle pier. Two pie-sellers have
pitches by the gatehouse. At the south end the gatehouse: the lord's toll-board, the burned toll-house beside it, the
new chain, the gate-ward, and on the river rail, under sacking the reeve allowed, three men.

**Overbridge** is the Liberty: Saint Wade's church and Wade's Acre, where Wrayford has buried its dead since the ford;
the Abbess's lodging; the tan-yards, which stink; the bear-garden; the Ferry Inn; the stews. The reeve's men may not
arrest anyone here. The Abbess's lay-brothers may, and do, for her reasons.

### The city's life without the dead

Wrayford has more going on than a hanging, and most of it is not about the bridge.

- **Market days** (the second and fifth of the week): drovers' herds in the Shambles pens, hiring at the Cross, the
  Corn Exchange loud.
- **The Bridge Feast** is in five days: the Company's annual procession across the bridge, with the aldermen in their
  blue gowns, the Abbess meeting them at the gatehouse to receive the "bridge penny" (a symbolic toll the city has
  paid the Liberty since the bridge was built). This year the gatehouse has three dead men on it. The aldermen have
  asked the reeve whether the procession could go by water.
- **Evenings** at the Nine Wheels, the Ferry Inn and the Guildhall's Bench supper. Bells from Saint Wade's.
- **Ordinary troubles**: an apprentice who has run away to the bear-garden; a lost dog; a butcher and a tanner suing
  each other over a hide; the painter of the Company's portrait, unpaid and drunk; a printer who will print anything.

## 3. The night of the rising, and what is true

What Wrayford believes:

1. The carters rose against the toll, burned the toll-house and broke the chain.
2. Wat Pardoe's cart ran down the toll-keeper's boy, Ned Hobday, fourteen, in the press at the gate.
3. The riders caught the ringleaders on the back road to the fells before dawn: Wat Pardoe, Dunstan Fenner and Joss
   Wilder. They hanged them at the gatehouse under the lord's warrant.

What is true:

1. **The fire.** The carters broke the chain and threw the toll-board into the river. They did not burn the
   toll-house. Amos Pinch, the Bridge Company's collector, set it alight in the confusion. He had been taking a penny
   in five from the toll for four months, and the toll-house held the tally-sticks and the day-book. Nobody looked for
   another fire-raiser when there was a riot to blame.
2. **The cart.** Wat was not driving. His son Kit, seventeen, had taken the reins while Wat was at the chain. The cart
   went over Ned when the crowd pushed. Wat took the reins back, and took the boy up into the cart, and drove for the
   fells to find a bone-setter, and Ned died on the way. When the riders stopped them, Wat said he had been driving.
   Kit, who had run, knows. Nobody else does, except Dunstan, who is dead.
3. **The riders.** They knew which road, because someone sent word. Hal Hulme, the reeve's son, wrote to the riders'
   sergeant at midnight. His father had told him the riders would only *take* the ringleaders, and the lord's court
   would fine them; Hal believed that a night of taking was better than a night of burning that would bring the
   captain's whole troop. The riders hanged them on the road at first light, under a warrant Hal did not know they
   carried.

Nothing here takes the dead off the bridge. Each truth changes what one person will do:

- Pinch's fire, proved, changes what the Bench and the captain can say about a *rising*.
- Kit's driving, told to Jennet Hobday, changes the two mothers.
- Hal's letter, known to Kit, is a killing.

## 4. The people

### The Pardoes

**Maudie Pardoe**, fifty, carter's widow as of four days. Practical, unsentimental, speaks in instructions, makes
bread when she cannot think. She will not have Wat out a third night: in Wrayford the dead are watched three nights and
buried on the third day, and one who is not "goes looking." She does not believe this, she says, and she will not have
it. If nobody else goes on the third night, she goes alone, with the bread knife for the cords.

**Kit Pardoe**, seventeen, the protagonist's younger brother. He loves them and resents their three years away. He
drove the cart. He wants to burn the Company's counting-house because burning something is the only thing big enough
to stand in front of what he did. His arc is from fire to confession: to his sibling, to his mother, to Jennet.

**Wat Pardoe**, dead, a carter of thirty years, who said he was driving.

**Bramble**, Wat's old cart-dog, who has lain under the gatehouse for four days and will not eat.

### The city's officers

**Simon Hulme**, Reeve of Wrayford, Maudie's cousin and Kit's godfather. Twenty years a good reeve: careful, tired,
honest about his reasons, afraid. The decree is his bargain: bodies on the rail are proof of order, and order means
the captain takes the toll money and goes. He has all the facts but two (his son's letter, Kit's driving), and no
evidence will move him. Shame can, and love for Kit, and Jennet's grief, and the city turning its back on him at
Saint Wade's, and exhaustion. He asks the protagonist *What would you have me do?* and means it.

**Hal Hulme**, his son, the protagonist's oldest friend and, before they went away, something nearer. He wrote the
letter. He has not slept. He is kind, clever, and certain that a good outcome justifies a hard act, which is his
father's faith without his father's twenty years of finding out otherwise.

**Captain Lys Sefton**, the lord's captain, who comes up the south road at dusk on the third day with twenty riders.
A professional who wants no trouble and will make a great deal of it if she finds any. She has one question: *Is
Wrayford in rebellion?* Everything turns on what she sees and who tells her.

**Sergeant Gault**, the riders' sergeant left in Wrayford with four men to keep the gatehouse. Drinks at the Nine
Wheels. Did the hanging. Is not sorry. Is not cruel, either: he has a warrant and a view of carters.

### The Bridge Company

**Alderman Prosper Gidley**, Master of the Worshipful Company of the Bridge. Fat, warm, pious, sentimental, and
entirely sincere in his belief that the toll is a scandal the Company has bravely taken on to spare the city worse.
The Company farms the toll from the lord for three hundred crowns a year and collects five hundred. It is drafting a
Remonstrance to the lord against the toll. Gidley weeps at the gatehouse every morning, publicly.

**Amos Pinch**, the Company's collector, who set the fire. Thin, precise, frightened, a pedant of the toll schedule,
which he knows by heart and recites under stress. The new toll-booth is a hut with a board.

**The Bench**: four other aldermen, each with a small comic vice and a share in the toll.

### The Liberty

**The Lady Abbess, Dame Isobel Crane**, of Saint Wade's. Sixty, dry, worldly, and the only person in Wrayford who has
read every charter. She wants the city to acknowledge the Liberty's rights, which it has been eroding for a century;
the dead are, to her, a question of jurisdiction first and charity second, and she is honest about the order.

**Corbin**, sexton of Saint Wade's for forty years, who has dug every Wrayford grave since he was twenty. He will dig
none while a Wrayford man hangs on the bridge. So Ned Hobday cannot be buried either, nor Alderman Gidley's aunt, who
died on Tuesday. Corbin is dry, funny and immovable, and has quarrelled with Dorcas Mudd every day since the bridge
took her father's ferry trade.

### The river

**Dorcas Mudd**, mistress of the ferrymen's company, sixty, loud, who hates the bridge on principle and would carry
the Devil across for the pleasure of not taking him over it. She and Corbin are the bridge's two oldest enemies and
have been in love, in the Wrayford way, for forty years.

### The others

**Jennet Hobday**, the toll-keeper's widow. Her son Ned lies in her front room on Bridge Street in the August heat,
unburied because of Corbin's strike. She wants him buried and Wat Pardoe left to the crows. Her husband was the
lord's toll-keeper and died last winter; Ned took his place at the booth for the Company, at a penny a day.

**Annis Pye**, a pie-seller on the bridge, who was to marry Joss Wilder at Harvest. She sells pies to the crowd that
comes to look at him, because she has her mother to keep and the crowd is there. His ring is not on his hand.

**Win Fenner**, Dunstan's widow, a laundress in Northgate with four children, who wants nothing from anybody and will
take the parish's bread only if nobody watches her take it.

**Ezra Quoin**, printer, who has printed *The True and Last Confession of Wat Pardoe, Carter*, written by the Company's
clerk, at a penny a sheet. It sells well.

**Tom Lumley**, painter, commissioned to paint the Company's portrait for the Guildhall in time for the Bridge Feast,
unpaid, and painting what he sees.

**Ham Ostler**, a drover with sixty head in the Shambles pens, who must cross by the bridge and will not pay the toll
for cattle at a penny a head; his beasts know the ford.

**Old Pell Saxby**, the Nine Wheels' landlady, who serves the sergeant because refusing would be a kind of politics
and she has never had any.

## 6. The larger conflict and the shape

### The powers

| Power | Wants | Does if left alone |
|---|---|---|
| **The reeve** | The captain to find order | Keeps the dead on the rail; arrests anyone who touches them; hands Kit over if he must |
| **The Bridge Company** | To keep the toll farm, look virtuous, and avoid an audit | Drafts the Remonstrance; sells the broadside; offers the captain the carters' ringleaders and Kit; holds the Feast |
| **The carters** | The toll gone, their dead buried, revenge | The young burn the counting-house on night 2; the old go to the Guildhall and are refused |
| **The Liberty** | Its rights acknowledged | Waits; claims the dead if they reach Overbridge; refuses the bridge penny at the Feast |
| **The captain** | No rebellion | Arrives at dusk day 3; finds what she finds |

### Movements

The adventure is not divided into acts the player must pass, but it has four movements of feeling:

- **I. Homecoming** (day 1): the facts, the city, the people; the small stories offered; the mystery opened.
- **II. The Heat** (day 2 and night 2): positions harden; the Remonstrance; Kit's night.
- **III. The Riders** (day 3 and night 3): the captain; the deadline.
- **IV. The Feast** (days 4–5): what the city has become.

## 7. The two principal approaches

**By day: the law.** Persuade, argue, petition, shame and bargain until the dead can be carried across the bridge in
daylight with the city watching. Its instruments: the toll schedule and the Ford charter (the dead cross free); the
Abbess's Liberty (once across, they are hers); the Remonstrance's clause (cover for the reeve); Jennet and Maudie
together (the reeve's men will not lay hands on the toll-keeper's widow); Pinch's fire (there was no rising, only a
riot and a thief); the captain persuaded. Its cost: time, and every concession made to the Company and the Abbess.

**By night: the act.** Take them down and bury them before the third night ends. Its instruments: the gatehouse (the
stair, the rail, the drawbridge machinery, Gault's men's habits, the moon, the hour they sleep); the crossings (the
ferry, the ford, the butcher's cart); Corbin's spade. Its cost: someone will be arrested at dawn, and the captain will
find an act of rebellion.

A third approach, **by fire**, is Kit's: the rising again.

Hulme resigns. The toll stands |

## 9. Character arcs

- **Kit**: fire → confession. Changes whether the protagonist hears it.
- **Hulme**: decree → (resignation | too late | vindicated, which is worse).
- **Hal**: secret → (confession to Maudie | flight | killed by Kit | a public confession that ruins his father).
- **Jennet**: crows → carrying both.
- **Corbin and Dorcas**: forty years → a question asked first.
- **Annis**: selling pies at the gate → at the funeral, with the ring.
- **Gidley**: does not change. That is the satire.
- **The protagonist**: late → whatever they make of it.

## 10. Rewards

- *The freedom of the bridge*: a brass token the Abbess or the Bench gives (free crossing for life); in a generated
  world, a mark of standing in Wrayford.
- *Kit*: as a companion (kin), if he lives free.
- *Bramble*: as a companion (dog).
- *The Company's purse*: forty crowns for silence, if taken.
- *The Liberty's sanctuary*: a token that Overbridge will shelter you.
- *Dorcas's ferry*: free at any hour.
- Experience for what is resolved (milestones), not for what is killed.

## 11. Voice

Wrayford is brick, river-mud, horse-dung, tar, river-fog, bread, gutted fish, the blue gowns of aldermen. The prose is
the house voice at its plainest, with one difference: the city is funny. Comedy comes from people being exactly
themselves at the wrong moment: Gidley weeping beautifully at the gatehouse; Pinch reciting the toll on a dead man;
Dorcas and Corbin's daily quarrel across the river. The tragic scenes are told short. The dead are under sacking and
are not described.
