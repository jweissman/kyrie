# The Third Night — editorial review of the first treatment

*Stage 2. The treatment ([01-treatment.md](01-treatment.md)) read as an editor would, against the commission's
questions and against the repertoire survey that proposed it. Each finding ends with a decision; the revised design is
[03-revised-design.md](03-revised-design.md).*

## 1. Is the city alive apart from its central story?

**Not yet.** Of sixteen small adventures, twelve serve the dead: crossings, permissions, graves, the mystery. The
four that do not (Bramble, the portrait, the bear-garden, the hide) are the thinnest in the table: the bear-garden
apprentice is a fetch quest with a bear, and the hide is a joke without a game. The city's "life without the dead"
(§2) is a list of things that happen: market days, a feast, evenings, troubles. A list is not a life. The player has to
*meet* the city doing its own business at the hours it does it.

**Decision.**

- At least five stories with no bearing on the dead, each with the full set of parts (person, object, uncertainty,
  approaches, complication, consequence). The hide becomes a real judgement with evidence on the hide itself. The
  bear-garden becomes a choice about a boy, not a fetch. Win Fenner's pride (new) and Bramble stay. The portrait
  stays, and earns its place as the satire's creative-labour game.
- The city keeps hours that the player sees without asking: the market's pens fill and empty; the Bench sups; Saint
  Wade's rings; Dorcas and Corbin quarrel across the river at the same hour every evening; the crowd at the gatehouse
  grows by day and changes its temper.

## 2. Are the small adventures substantial and varied?

**Varied in subject, monotonous in verb.** Read down the "approaches" column: *persuade, flatter, bribe, steal* recur
in nearly every row. Several stories are routes to the same need (ferry, ford, butcher's cart are three crossings),
and two are one story told twice (Corbin's strike and the ferry-and-sexton).

**Decision.**

- Merge Corbin's strike and the ferry-and-sexton into one story: *Corbin and Dorcas*.
- Give the crossings each a story of its own, not only a route: the ford is Ham Ostler's beasts and a night river; the
  butcher's cart is a comic smuggle with a boy who talks; the ferry is Dorcas.
- Widen the verbs: carry, climb, dig, cool a body with ice, coax a dog, track by night, paint, write and set type,
  judge, lower a rope, herd. Each build should find a verb that is theirs.

## 3. Does the plot grow from the setting?

**Mostly, and in one place it grows from the collection's habit instead.** The bridge, the toll, the Liberty, the
river and the gatehouse are physical and political at once, and the conflict is made of them. But the **Ford
charter** ("the dead of Wrayford cross free to Saint Wade's"), found in an old document, is exactly the misread
inheritance the survey criticised: an old text that unlocks the present. So are the "old ford-stones nobody has used
in two hundred years".

**Decision.** Cut the charter. The ford stays, but as *living* knowledge: drovers' beasts use it, Ham Ostler knows it,
the city merely pretends not to. The satire of the law rests on the Company's own new schedule, not on an ancient
one. Where the dead may go is decided by people who are alive now: the Abbess, Dorcas, Pinch, the reeve.

## 4. Is the satire a game, or a long passage of dialogue?

**At risk of dialogue, and at risk of a vote.** The Remonstrance "meeting", as written, is a council moved by
flattery, bribery and a portrait: the Board of *Tessary* again, which the survey said to rest. The toll schedule is
closer to a game, because it is a classification the player chooses. The portrait and the broadside are games only if
their choices have consequences someone acts on.

**Decision.** Every satirical set-piece is a decision with a rule the player can learn:

- **The Remonstrance.** The aldermen will append any clause that *costs them nothing they can be seen to lose*. The
  game is phrasing. "That the dead be released" fails: it costs them face with the lord. "That the dead be commended
  to the Liberty's charity, at the Liberty's charge, the Company's rights being reserved" passes at once, and is
  enough to give the reeve cover. The satire is that they pass the one that costs them nothing, and congratulate
  themselves.
- **The toll schedule.** Pinch must rule how a dead man crosses, and he rules by the board. The player chooses the
  category: *goods of a felon* (forfeit to the lord, not to cross); *carcass, by the hundredweight* (allowed, priced,
  and unbearable to Maudie); *pilgrim to Saint Wade's* (free, but "on his own feet"); *the Abbess's tithe* (free, if
  she claims him). It is funny and it is terrible, and paying for your father by the hundredweight is a real choice.
- **The jurisdiction of the water.** New, and better than the charter: the gatehouse is the lord's, the north half of
  the bridge the city's, the south bank the Liberty's, and *the river between the stairs is the ferry's by its old
  charter*, a living one, because Dorcas will tell anyone. A body lowered on a rope from the rail into Dorcas's boat is
  neither the lord's nor the city's. It is a night act with a legal cover, joining the two approaches.
- **The portrait** and **the broadside** each produce an object that is used later: carried at the funeral, posted at
  the Cross, hung at the Feast, or burned.

## 5. Are there real alternatives?

**In the endings, yes; in how the endings are reached, not enough.** Seven endings, but the treatment does not say
which small adventures prepare which, so the endings risk becoming a menu at the end. "By the Book" also leans on the
charter, now cut.

**Decision.** Each ending names the small adventures that make it reachable, and each needs at least two of them.
"By the Book" becomes **The Liberty's Charity**: the Remonstrance clause, the Abbess's claim and Pinch's ruling,
together, give the reeve leave to release the dead to the Liberty before the captain comes.

## 6. Does it reward different builds?

**The treatment never asks.** It assumes a talker.

**Decision.** A build matrix is part of the revised design. Each principal approach must be completable by a
protagonist without high charisma and without scholarly training, and each build must have at least one thing only
it does well:

- strength: carry a body alone, dig, hold the rope;
- stealth and climbing: the gatehouse, Pinch's room, leaving bread unseen;
- animals: Bramble, the beasts on the ford;
- rites (a cleric): the vigil, the burial words, the Abbess's regard;
- violence: Gault's four, Kit's night, honestly and at a cost;
- lore and reading: the burned day-book, the hide's brands;
- speech: the Remonstrance, the captain, the broadside.

## 7. Is there too much exposition?

**The mystery is three confessions.** Each truth in §3 is a person who knows and will say. That is a mystery solved by
asking the right person the right question once.

**Decision.** Each truth is found in the world first and confessed second:

- **The fire.** An oil flask in the toll-house ash, of the Company's kind. Pinch could not bear to burn his own
  day-book, and it is under his mattress, its last pages in his small hand. The pedant's character is the evidence.
- **The cart.** Blood on Wat's tailboard where a boy was *lifted in*, not where a wheel went over. Kit's account of
  where he was does not survive a walk round the yards. Bramble finds Kit at night where he goes to be alone.
- **The letter.** Gault keeps his warrant and the message in his coat, and will show the message to a soldier who
  asks him plainly. A thief can take it. Hal's hand is in it.

Confession then becomes a choice the confessor makes, because of who is asking and what they have done: not a topic
unlocked by a fact.

## 8. Are the comic moments earned?

**Some are set-pieces.** Gidley weeping at the gatehouse is a joke the treatment tells; the player does not cause it.

**Decision.** Comedy comes from consequence:

- Pinch recites the schedule because the player pressed him.
- The new toll-board ends up in the river a second time because the player redirected Kit's fire.
- Dorcas and Corbin say the thing because the player carried the right message wrongly.
- Gidley weeps when, and because, he sees the portrait.

## 9. Does a character meaningfully change?

**Kit, Jennet, Hulme, Corbin and Dorcas, yes. The protagonist: undefined.** And in a world where an existing
protagonist arrives from elsewhere, the protagonist is not Wat's child.

**Decision.** The work has two entrances, written for, not patched:

- **The homecoming** (the standalone campaign): the protagonist is Wat's elder child, come home on their mother's
  letter. Late. The full tragedy; Hal as their oldest friend.
- **The traveller** (placed in a generated world): an existing protagonist comes to Wrayford by the drove road,
  having heard at an inn that three hang on Wrayford bridge. They are a stranger. The city's small stories are open to
  them as to anyone. The Pardoes' story is someone else's grief, and the work admits it: Maudie will not ask a stranger
  for anything, but a stranger is the one person in Northgate Gault's men are not watching. Kit can be talked to as a
  stranger talks to a boy. The endings are the same; the cost lands on others.

The protagonist's change is in what they do on the second and third nights. The work's job is to make that choice
heavy, not to narrate an arc.

## 10. Other faults

- **Scope.** Sixteen small adventures, three secrets, three romances and five powers in three days. A player can do a
  third of it, which is right, but an implementer cannot build all of it to the same depth. **Decision:** fourteen
  small adventures, built properly. Three days and three nights for the tragedy, and the city goes on after, so that
  the small stories have a fourth and fifth day, and a generated world has a city to come back to.
- **The default ending.** Maudie hanged beside her husband is the decree's logic and the strongest tragic default. It
  must be foreshadowed early and plainly (the decree is on the gatehouse door) and told in one line.
- **Hal and the romance.** The romance has no verbs. **Decision:** a place that is theirs (the starling ledge under the
  middle arch where they fished as children), a night when it matters, and a choice when the letter comes out:
  forgive, expose, keep it, kiss, leave. In the traveller's entrance there is no romance with Hal; the romances are
  Annis's and Corbin's.
- **Repetition of the collection's furniture.** A sexton (fine), a printer (new), an abbess (the collection has
  prioresses and Mother Ysolt; the Abbess must be worldly and legal, not a keeper of memory), a ledger (Pinch's
  day-book: a risk; it is kept because it is about Pinch's character, not the city's past). No inscriptions in capitals
  except the decree and the toll-board, which are notices, not inheritance.
