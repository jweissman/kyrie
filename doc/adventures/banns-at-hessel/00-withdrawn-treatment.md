# A Frost at Hessel — the original treatment

*Stage 1. Written before reading the engine for this purpose, and deliberately unconstrained by it. Stage 2
([02-critique.md](02-critique.md)) attacks it; Stage 3 ([04-adaptation.md](04-adaptation.md)) reads it against Kyr.*

## 0. What the commission asked, and where this starts

A metropolitan region; many small adventures with real insides; an overarching mystery, satire, intrigue or romance;
a tone the collection does not have. Three seeds were offered: *The Banns at Hessel* (romance and domestic comedy over
a wedding week), *A Winter in Carth* (horror in a snowbound farmhouse), *The Road to Orm* (a philosophical walk with a
heretic going to trial).

The collection now has two cities. **Wrayford** is a tragedy on a bridge: the dead on the rail, the third night, no
coda. **Pentrice** is a comedy of the theatre in a minster city of licences and precedence. A third city must not be a
third minster town, must not satirise an Office, and must not end in a bench, a vote or a hearing (the repertoire asks
that these rest). The repertoire's list of what Kyrie has not yet learned to say is the brief behind the brief:
*wanting; fault; a person as an end; making; comedy as structure; fear that lasts; the small that stays small; minds
that change because of people.*

So: a **romantic comedy of manners in a frozen merchant city**, with a ghost story in its hinterland and a philosopher
on its ice. Its tone is warm, quick, amorous and arch. Its narrator has opinions. It is the first Kyrie work whose
centre is a marriage.

## 1. The premise in a paragraph

Hessel is a rich, cold, litigious river city of wool-houses and notaries, where nothing binds unless it is sealed,
and a marriage is a deed between two houses. This winter the river Orm has frozen hard for the first time in
thirty-one years, and the city has built a second city on it: the **Frost Fair**, booths of oars and blankets, an ox
on a spit, printing presses, skittles, gin, skaters. And on the ice, the old saying goes, *no parish runs*: the river
belongs to no parish, so on the frozen river there are no banns, no guilds, no seals. A defrocked priest called
**Aubrey Thaw** has set up a booth with a painted sign — WEDDINGS WHILE YOU WAIT — and marries anybody, masked or not,
for a shilling. Half the young of Hessel have done it for a lark. Some meant it. Nobody knows whether any of it will
hold when the ice goes.

The banns of **Ismay Fennel** and **Rob Wenlock**, which unite the two great wool-houses of the Wool Quay, have been read
twice in Saint Ebba's. The third reading is on Sunday, the wedding on Monday, and the thaw is coming. By the time the
company has been in Hessel a day, it is plain that the bride was married on the ice three nights ago, in a fox mask,
to somebody, and that she believes it was the man she loves, and that it was not.

## 2. The protagonist, and why they are wanted

The protagonist comes over the snow from the south, a stranger, and does what every stranger to Hessel does that
winter: buys a hood and a mask at the fair, because the cold off the ice takes the skin off your face and everyone
is masked. **They buy a sable hood very like the one Ismay's ice-husband wore.** Ismay, on the ice at dusk, takes
them for him, and says so — "You came. I said you wouldn't. I'm not sorry, are you?" — in front of a printer, a
pie-man and her future sister-in-law.

That is the hook, and it puts the protagonist at fault from the first scene. They can say *I am not him* at once,
and be dragged into the search for who he was; or they can say nothing, or something kind, and be, for an evening,
the husband of the richest girl in Hessel; or they can lie outright, and find out what that costs. Every route makes
them a person in the comedy, not a reader of it. They are wanted, desired, owed and blamed. Their own wanting is
offered, not imposed: money (a dowry is a dowry), love (several people in Hessel can be courted), or simply the
pleasure of being in the middle of it.

### The districts

**The Wool Quay.** The two great houses face each other across the Quay: **Fennel's** (gabled, old, money that
smells of sheep) and **Wenlock's** (new brick, money that smells of ink). Fennel's kitchen is the engine of the
wedding: a cook with a temper, a bride-cake that can fail, forty guests, two families who have not dined together in
nine years. Dame Agnes Dumbrell, Ismay's grandmother, keeps her bed in the room over the gate and runs the house from
it by bell.

**Notaries' Row and the Guildhall.** The satire's home. Hessel's Notaries seal every promise that matters, at fees
painted on their doors (a betrothal, four crowns; a will, by the page; a promise of payment, a tenth). The
Corporation sits in the Guildhall and cannot decide whether the ice is in the city. Its standing answer, entered in
the minutes every day since the frost, is that the matter is *under consideration*. The Recorder, Master Gilbey,
holds that the ice is the city's when it will bear the Lord Mayor's coach, and has been trying for a fortnight to get
the Lord Mayor to drive it on.

**Saint Ebba's.** The parish church where the banns are read. Its curate, Mr Pyne, twenty-four, has read banns for
two years and has never once been answered at "If any of you know cause, or just impediment". He has a recurring
dream in which he is. This Sunday, he will be.

**The Shambles and the Market.** Where the feast is fetched: the butchers, the poulterers, the spice-seller who is a
Wenlock tenant and will not sell to Fennels, the fishwives who cannot fish.

**The Frost Fair.** A city of its own, built in a fortnight, that will be gone in a night. **Blanket Row**, the
street of booths; **the Ox**, roasting whole, sold by the slice, with a man whose job is to keep the ice under the
fire from melting; **the Press**, a printer who prints your name on a card, *printed on the river Orm in the Great
Frost*, sixpence; **the Valentine booth**, where a public letter-writer called Valentine writes love letters for
people who cannot, or dare not; **Thaw's booth**, the weddings; **the skating reach**, where the young of Hessel skate
in masks at dusk and everyone is someone else; **the thin ice at the piers**, where the current runs under the bridge
and the ice is a hand thick and grey.

**Hessel Bridge.** Nineteen arches and houses all along it. The current through the arches is why the ice is thin
there, and why the bridge-houses' privies have, this winter, become a public question.

**The Staithes.** The watermen's quarter on the south bank. The frost has put every waterman out of work, so the
Watermen's Company has declared the ice its river and charges a penny to walk onto it, at every stair. This is
satire with teeth: the watermen are starving. **Kit Marrable**, a waterman's son, drives meat sledges down the ice
road from the farms upriver.

**The Bell and Basin and the Assembly Rooms.** Southwark's inn, where travellers stay, and the long room above the
corn exchange where the Assembly dance is held on Friday. Who dances with whom on Friday is the city's business by
Saturday.

**The ice road and Carth.** The frozen Orm is a road now: sledges, skaters, carters. Half a day upriver, above the
bend, is **Carth**, a steading on the fell-side where the Brannagh family farm, and where Dame Dumbrell was born.
Beyond Carth, another half day, is **Orm**, the bishop's town, where Aubrey Thaw has been summoned to answer for the
weddings.

## 5. The overarching story

### The comedy

Comedy as structure means complications that compound, each solution making a new problem. The spine:

1. **The mistaking.** Ismay takes the protagonist for her ice-husband. Mopsa sees it.
2. **The truth, in the wrong order.** If the protagonist says they are not him, Ismay realises her husband was not
   Kit (Kit would have come); if Kit was not on the ice that night, who was? If the protagonist lets her believe,
   she tells Kit she is married to *him* (the protagonist), and Kit, who loves her, goes off to Carth with the
   sledges and does not come back.
3. **Valentine's three wives.** The masked groom is Valentine; and Ismay is not the only bride. **Bess Corder**, the
   chandler's heiress, and **Lettice Amory**, the alderman's ward, were married by Thaw the same way, to a note from
   their lovers. Each believes herself the only one. Each has told one person.
4. **The houses.** Mistress Wenlock hears a rumour and means to break the match and sue for the cost of the
   bride-cake. Dumbrell means the wedding to go on whatever it costs. Rob means to be on a ship. Mopsa means to find
   out.
5. **The law.** Whether any of it binds depends on whether the ice was in the city, which depends on whether the
   Lord Mayor's coach goes onto it, and on what the bishop at Orm says about Thaw.
6. **The banns.** Sunday. "If any of you know cause." Someone must speak or nobody will.
7. **The thaw.** The ice is going. The fair must be struck in a night. Valentine means to go with it.

### The mystery

Who married Ismay on the ice? It is solved not by an exhibit but by people: by finding out who Valentine wrote for;
by Thaw, who never looks at the faces but always at the hands, and remembers ink; by Bess Corder and Lettice Amory,
who will not speak to a stranger but will speak to each other if the protagonist can get them in a room; by Kit's
absence, which Carth can vouch for. There is no ledger. The parson keeps no register: "The ice remembers. So do the
married. It is enough."

### The satire

Hessel's comedy is that a city which believes only in sealed promises has discovered, for a fortnight, a place where
promises are only promises. Everyone in the city has a different interest in what the ice means:

- the Notaries sell seals that make ice promises good "after the thaw";
- the Watermen claim the ice as their river and toll it;
- the Corporation will not decide whether the ice is the city's;
- the Recorder wants the Lord Mayor's coach on it;
- the Church at Orm wants Thaw silenced and every ice wedding void;
- Valentine wants his marriages good;
- Nan and Tom want theirs good;
- Ismay wants hers void, then good, then void again, depending on who she thinks she married.

The satire is a game because the player can **use** these interests: buy a seal, ride a coach, bribe a toll, carry a
bishop's letter, drive the Lord Mayor onto the ice, or get a waterman to break it.

### The romance

There is a great deal of it, and none of it is an affection meter:

- **Ismay and Kit.** The love story the comedy is for. Each thinks the other does not care.
- **Rob and the sea.** The groom's romance is with a ship.
- **Mopsa and the protagonist,** if the player wishes: a courtship of wits, conducted in insults, across the week.
  She is the only person in Hessel who sees through everyone, and she likes being surprised.
- **Dumbrell and Hepworth.** Not a romance: an old wrong, between an old lady and her old cook, that can be put right
  over a bride-cake.
- **Nan and Tom,** the laundress and the cobbler, whose ice marriage is the only uncomplicated love in the city and the
  thing most at risk from the law.
- **The protagonist and anyone.** On the ice, for a shilling, the protagonist can marry. Someone. Masked, or not.

### The philosophy

**Aubrey Thaw** is a philosopher of the Flow, and his question is the work's question: *does a promise bind the person
who made it, when that person is gone?* The ice is his argument made physical: a city that will not be there on
Thursday, where people make vows on water. He argues it with the protagonist in his booth, and if the protagonist walks
him to Orm, all the way up the ice road.

### The horror

**Carth** is a different kind of winter. Last winter, in a blizzard, the Brannaghs turned a family away from the door
to save their own food, and the family died in the snow under the shieling. Ma Brannagh now lays a place each night
for the Hungry Guest. Someone in the house takes the food out at night. The sheep are going one by one. Fairweather
came up the road a week before, very good with the children. Sixty years ago, Agnes Dumbrell, a girl at Carth, did
the same thing to a pedlar in the snow, and the ring she wants is the one he gave her for a crust before she shut the
door.

## 6. The small adventures (as first imagined)

Each should have a person with a motive, a thing or place that matters, something misunderstood, more than one way,
a complication, a consequence, and a later effect.

1. **The Sable Hood.** The mistaking on the ice. Own it, deny it, or exploit it. Affects every later scene with Ismay,
   Mopsa and Kit.
2. **The Bride-Cake.** Hepworth's cake, which needs saffron the Wenlock-tenant spice-seller will not sell to Fennels,
   eggs in a frost, and an oven that will hold heat. Fetch the saffron by charm, by trade, by theft, or by Mopsa; or
   find that Hepworth will not bake for a Dumbrell at all, and why. A cake that can fail, judged at the feast.
3. **Dumbrell and Hepworth.** An old lady and her old cook who have not spoken in forty years. Find out why (a stolen
   spoon that was not stolen), and either make Dumbrell apologise (which nobody has ever made her do) or let it lie.
   The cake depends on it.
4. **The Penny at the Stairs.** The Watermen's toll. Pay it, dodge it, argue it, or take up the watermen's cause with
   the Corporation; the watermen remember who paid and who climbed round.
5. **The Lord Mayor's Coach.** The Recorder's scheme to make the ice city ground. Get the Mayor onto the ice (by
   flattery, by his wife, by a wager, by driving the coach yourself). If the coach goes on, the Liberty ends, ice
   weddings are city weddings, and the Notaries win. If it goes through, a different comedy.
6. **The Valentine Booth.** Have a letter written; watch the letter-writer; see who he writes for; find his ink on
   Ismay's note. Commission a letter of your own, to someone you mean.
7. **The Three Brides.** Bess Corder and Lettice Amory, each married to a note. Get them together. They will either
   cry, or laugh, or go after Valentine with a skate.
8. **Nan and Tom.** The laundress and the cobbler whose ice marriage is at risk. Seal it with a Notary; or get Thaw to
   say it again in Saint Ebba's; or persuade them it does not matter.
9. **Rob's Ship.** Rob wants to go to sea. Help him tell his mother; or help him run; or hold him to the match.
10. **Mr Pyne's Dream.** The curate fears the banns being answered. Steady him, frighten him, or give him the words.
11. **The Assembly.** The Friday dance. Who dances with whom. Mopsa; Ismay and Kit; Rob and the ship's master's
    daughter; the protagonist and whoever they choose. Masks are worn. Things are said.
12. **The Ice Road.** Fetch the ring from Carth for Dumbrell; or find Kit there; or walk Thaw to Orm.
13. **The Hungry Guest.** The night at Carth. Who takes the food. What Fairweather is. Whether to feed the Guest.
14. **The Road to Orm.** Thaw and the protagonist on the ice road, arguing. Deliver him, or let him go at the Gibbet
    Reach, or bring him back to Hessel to speak at the banns.
15. **The Thin Ice at the Piers.** A child goes through at the bridge. Save her, or not; the city remembers.
16. **The Ox.** The man who keeps the fire from melting the ice under it is drunk. A comic catastrophe in waiting.
17. **The Press.** The printer will print anything on a card. Print a broadside? Print Valentine's name?
18. **The Bridge-Houses' Privies.** The question of the season: what the bridge-houses do now that the river does not
    carry it away. The Corporation's committee. A small satire in a small room.

## 7. Acts

- **Act I, the Frost (days 1–2).** Arrival over the snow; the fair; the mistaking; the two houses; the first
  complications; the penny at the stairs; the Recorder's coach scheme.
- **Act II, the Ice Road (days 3–4).** To Carth, for the ring, for Kit, or with Thaw. The night of the Hungry Guest.
  Meanwhile in the city the rumours run.
- **Act III, the Banns (days 5–6).** The Friday Assembly; the three brides; Valentine found out or not; the cake; the
  third reading on Sunday and its answer.
- **Act IV, the Breaking (day 7).** The thaw. The fair struck in a night. Valentine on the ice with a dowry. The
  wedding, or two, or none.

## 8. How it can end

- **The Wedding As Planned.** Ismay and Rob, unhappy; the ice wedding hushed; Valentine paid off; Rob does not go to
  sea.
- **Two Weddings.** Ismay and Kit, and the protagonist and Mopsa (or someone). Rob on his ship.
- **The Ice Holds.** Thaw's weddings ruled good. Nan and Tom stay married. Ismay is Valentine's wife until the law
  can unmake it, and the comedy turns bitter.
- **The Ice Goes.** All ice weddings void. Ismay freed. Nan and Tom unmade; they marry again in Saint Ebba's, for the
  price of the banns, and Thaw gives her away.
- **Valentine Through the Ice.** Running at the breaking, with a dowry, at the piers.
- **No Wedding, and a Feast Anyway.** The cake eaten. Dumbrell dies on Sunday night, and the comedy holds a death.

## 9. What is new for Kyrie in it

- Romance and marriage as the centre.
- A protagonist mistaken for someone else, and at fault for it.
- A narrator with opinions; letters; verse (Thaw's doggerel service; the Press's cards).
- Satire as an interest-map the player can use.
- A season (the frost) as the clock, rather than an enemy's deadline.
- A hinterland episode of horror, tonally opposite, joined by a person (Dumbrell), not by a mechanism.
- A philosopher as a companion for a road.
- No inherited mechanism; nothing older than living memory except Dumbrell's secret; ends in the city it began in.
