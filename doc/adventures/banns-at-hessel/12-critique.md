# The Banns at Hessel: a final critique

*Written after playing the journeys as a reader rather than as their author. A second, literary pass
([14-second-pass.md](14-second-pass.md)) answers much of what follows: Hartopp's thinness, Carth's mechanical danger,
the illegible days, and the lack of foreshadowing. It says what the region does well,
what it does not, and what I would do next with more time.*

## What works

**It is tonally its own thing.** No other region in Kyrie is a comedy of manners. The house adventures are about
dread and inheritance; Low Water is about systems; Pellam, in progress on its own branch, is a fair. Hessel's
narrator has opinions and timing ("'That,' she says, 'is the second time in my life a stranger has thrown me at a
church.'"), and its stakes are social: who stands up, who is let in, who dances. It still lets one strand
(Carth House) go dark, and one (the Orm road) go philosophical. The three seeds are not bolted on: *A Winter in
Carth* is the house at the end of the Crescent, and *The Road to Orm* is how Ottery gets his licence. The bride with
banns in *The Banns at Hessel* is the region's spine.

**The strands really interlock.** Ismay's courage is one promise ("I'll be in the room"), and it opens two
resolutions in other strands. The chairmen's goodwill is assembled from small kindnesses and spent in four places.
Mrs Scrope makes any secret public, always quicker and always costlier. Mrs Cherrett's failing turns the company's
dawdling into a measure, and the closing page is chosen by it. In the Rooms transcript almost every scene is a
consequence of an earlier one.

**The two ways produce different towns.** This is tested, not claimed. The Rooms resolve things in drawing rooms and
tell the Season about it at the Ball. The Old Town resolves them in the dark and leaves the Rooms knowing only about
Hartopp's boots. Both reach a good ending; neither reaches the other's.

**Builds without charm or learning are full players.** The soldier plays the whole Rooms way without a roll. Every
key in Hessel can be had by asking the right person at the right hour. Skills make some keys quicker or open a second
door: lockwork and the coal-hole, climbing and the window, woodcraft and the sheep-path, lore and the bottle,
mending and the ankle, rites and the prayers.

**The world goes on without you, and the ending says what you changed.** Left alone, the banns are read and the
wedding is broken on the church steps by a woman with a baby; the knock goes on; Mrs Cherrett does not wake. Every
strand the company touches gets its own line in the closing account. Two Seasons with the same ending read as
different stories.

**It is placed, not selected.** A company in a generated world hears of the Season and walks over the march into it,
and walks out again afterwards.

## What does not

**Too much of it is talking to people in the right order.** About 110 topics against 51 actions and 8 checks. The
Rooms way in particular is a chain of conversations, and the prose carries it, but the verbs are "Ask" and "Tell".
Persuasion, the bard's signature, appears once (the plain apology), and the form route makes it unnecessary. For a
comedy of manners that is defensible, because talk is the action. But a player who wants to *do* things has fewer
of them than the length of the region suggests.

**The clock was not legible enough, and is only partly so now.** Hessel is a schedule, and the schedule is the
puzzle: Ismay's noon walk, Rupert's noon at Quay Lane, Ottery's dawn departure, the area gate at eight, the knock at
two. A first reading of the transcripts found nothing that gathered these. In the final revision Mopsa will now give
the round ("Ask Mopsa how a day of the Season goes") once the company is Mrs Cherrett's legs. Mrs Scrope will
tell anyone who asks about the Hospital on the first day that Ottery walks to Orm at first light. That covers the
Season's hours and the one appointment that cannot be made up. The people's own rounds are still learned one person
at a time. The transcripts still show runs of
fourteen and seventeen waits in which nothing is told.

**Carth House is less frightening than designed.** The design asked for "one night's dread". The prose gives the hall
of stopped clocks, the heat, the greatcoat on the chair and the knock, and those are good. But Sir Gervase's threat
is mechanical: an intruder is taken prisoner and thrown out, and the window nailed. There is no moment when the
company is in danger and must talk him down with something it learned. The sit-up in the hall is the best scene in
the region, and it is entered from a menu.

**The Ball is watched more than played.** Five dances happen to the company. Its own moves are to dance with Mopsa
against the Rule and to ask for the last dance. Hartopp's second and third dances, Twiss's warning and Kit on the
lawn are set up beforehand and then *occur*. A set piece this central should offer a choice per dance: whom to stand
up with, whom to cut, whom to introduce.

**The presentation stumbles in three places the module cannot fix.** Taught facts print before the scene that teaches
them; events print before a fight's blow-by-blow; Take shows an empty page. I rewrote the taught facts as short
journal lines so the order matters less, but a careful reader will still see "Ismay Cherrett watches Kit Danby from
the parlour window" before seeing her do it. These are in the friction report with remedies.

**Some outcomes are too binary.** Mrs Cherrett's drops are a five-step measure, but her ending reads it as alive or
dead. A Season in which she was saved on the last night ought to read differently from one in which she was saved on
the first. The objection at the reading (Ismay stands and says she does not consent) is lawfully nothing. That is
truthful, but the player who arranged it gets only a kind word from the curate and no other consequence.

**The romance with Mopsa is the thinnest strand.** It has good lines and a proper refusal ("Not to save me"), but its
acts are few and mostly topics. Her yes on the Ball night is earned by about five clicks.

**Dr Hartopp is a thin villain.** He is meant to be small, and he is pleasant, which is the joke, but he has few scenes
of his own. A player can finish the Season without having spoken to him.

## What I would do next

1. **Waiting by the round.** A "Wait until…" offer keyed to the Season's hours (the Pump Room, noon, the Rooms, two
   o'clock) would cut the runs of empty waits.
2. **The Ball as five choices**, one per dance.
3. **Sir Gervase in the dark.** An intruder found at night should be a conversation with a man holding a sword, won
   with something learned: the letter was from her brother; Celia remembers the snow.
4. **Mrs Cherrett's thanks graded by `failing`.**
5. **A Hartopp scene**: the Circus at five, his rounds, a man who sees the company coming.
6. Engine: the presentation order (friction 2 and 3), routine-yield (5) and a containment query (13). These would
   each remove machinery from every future authored region, not just this one.

## Verdict

Hessel is a complete, distinct, placed region with eleven interlocking strands, two genuinely different principal
ways through, five endings and a line-by-line closing account, playable by any build. Its prose is its strength and
its verbs are its weakness. I would ship it as it is, and do items 1 and 2 above next.
