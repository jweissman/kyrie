# Turned Away

Played by Tamsin, a bard; seed `h1`, steps `test/fixtures/hessel/gossip.json`; 120 steps; ended at watch 600, at the ending "Turned Away". Written by `bun scripts/hessel-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The south road comes down out of the green country to a river, and on the far side of the river is Hessel, all of it at once, going up its hill: first the Old Town's roofs, wet and close, with steam coming out of them as if the whole place were a kettle; then a great dark church and a long pale pillared building in a haze of more steam; then terraces, honey-coloured, curving round the hill like the rings of a sawn log; and on the top of the hill, against the sky, a castle that is, when you look at it properly, only a wall with holes in it. The air smells of warm iron.

**To the foot of Lim Bridge**

The foot of Lim Bridge: fish-baskets, a tar-pot, washing strung between upper windows, and the river going by, brown and quick. A board on the toll-house says WELCOME TO HESSEL, THE QUEEN OF HEALING, and somebody has added in chalk AND OF HEALERS.

**To Quay Lane**

Quay Lane: narrow houses, a milliner’s with three bonnets in the window on wooden heads, a lodging-house with a card in its window, and at the end the steam of the laundries rolling across the lane like weather. Number Four is locked, with a card in the window: TO LET, A GOOD LODGING-HOUSE, APPLY MR GILMOUR, DAYTIMES.

**To Stall Street, by the chairmen's stand**

Stall Street: the chairmen’s stand, a long bench under a penthouse roof, and the chairs themselves, black, glossy, like great beetles with their poles laid by them; men in blue coats sitting on the bench with their arms folded, every one of them built like a door. Big Lanyon, the chairmen’s spokesman, a huge mild man with a voice like a bell under water, is here.

**To the Abbey Yard, at the foot of the Parade**

Somebody at the top of the Parade screams, and then everybody does. Down the hill toward you, gathering speed on the ridged paving, comes a bath-chair, a great wicker thing on three iron wheels, and in it, upright and furious and holding on to her bonnet, a very large old woman in purple. Behind it, much too far behind it, a chairman in a blue coat is running and shouting. The chair is aimed at the Pump Room colonnade, and the colonnade is stone. The Abbey Yard, at the foot of the Parade: on one side the great dark front of Saint Ebba’s, carved all over with angels climbing ladders; on another the colonnade of the Pump Room; and between them the Parade going up the hill so steeply that the paving is cut into ridges for the chairmen’s feet. Everyone here is either ill, or with someone who is, or selling something to both. Here are Mrs Abigail Cherrett, a vast old woman in purple, with a laugh like a dropped coal-scuttle; and Kit Danby, a young chairman in a blue coat, with a book in his pocket.

**Seize the side of the chair and drag it round into the church railings**

You catch the side of the chair as it comes level and haul it round, and it leaves the colonnade and goes instead into the railings of Saint Ebba’s with a noise like a dropped dresser, and stops, half in and half out of them, with a wheel going round in the air. Your arm is wrenched half out of its socket. The old woman in it straightens her bonnet. ‘That,’ she says, ‘is the second time in my life a stranger has thrown me at a church.’ Tamsin resists (dexterity). Tamsin gains 100 experience: stopped Mrs Cherrett’s chair.

**Ask the old woman if she is hurt**

‘Hurt? I cooked for four hundred navvies on the Delve cut through the wettest winter God ever sent,’ says the old woman. ‘I am Abigail Cherrett, and I am not hurt. I am dying, but that’s another thing, and not your fault.’ She looks at you again, longer. ‘You’ve got legs,’ she says. ‘Mine are done, and that one’s are’ (the chairman, scarlet, holding his hat) ‘evidently not to be trusted. I’ve a granddaughter to marry, and a great-nephew, and a companion, by the Closing Ball on Monday, and I’m in a town where I can’t get up a hill. I’ll pay you to be my legs for the Season. Fetch and carry. Go where I can’t. Tell me what’s what.’ She sticks out a hand the size of a spade. ‘Mrs Cherrett’s lodgings, the Circus. Come up when you’ve a mind to say yes.’

**To the top of the Parade**

The top of the Parade, where the hill levels: you can see the whole of Hessel below you from here, the Baths smoking and the Old Town smoking and the river. Two terraces go off from here, the Crescent to the left and the Circus to the right, and a path goes on up to the downs and the Beacon.

**To the Circus**

The Circus: a ring of tall houses round a round lawn, every one alike, columns on columns, so that wherever you stand you are looking at the same house. On one side, the Assembly Rooms; on another, a door with a brass plate, MR S. TWISS, M.C.; on another, the lodgings Mrs Cherrett has taken for the Season. Mr Twiss’s door bears a card, beautifully lettered: THE MASTER OF THE CEREMONIES RECEIVES BETWEEN TEN AND SIX. The Assembly Rooms are dark, and their great door is shut.

**Wait › An hour**

On the Circus lawn the chairmen sit on the poles of Mrs Cherrett’s chair waiting for her to want it. The young one is reading a book with his back to her windows, and every so often, without lifting his head, he looks up at them. An hour passes.

**Into Mrs Cherrett's lodgings**

Ismay Cherrett watches Kit Danby from the parlour window. Mrs Cherrett’s parlour: rented furniture, too good for anyone to sit on, and on top of it everything Mrs Cherrett has brought from home, which is a great deal, including a stuffed pike, a canal map of three counties framed in oak, and a footstool made of an elephant’s foot. The window looks down over the Circus lawn. Mrs Cherrett sits in the middle of it all like the stuffed pike’s mother, with her stick across her knees, and misses nothing. Ismay is standing at the window with her ledger open in her hands. Down on the Circus lawn the chairmen are waiting with Mrs Cherrett’s chair, sitting on its poles, and one of them, the young one, is reading a book. Ismay does not turn a page all the time you are in the room. Mrs Cherrett’s bedroom door is shut, and her maid sits on a chair in front of it with her knitting, and looks at you over it. Here are Mrs Abigail Cherrett, a vast old woman in purple, with a laugh like a dropped coal-scuttle; Ismay Cherrett, a quiet young woman with a ledger and ink on her finger; Rob Cherrett, a long young man with ink on his cuffs and his hair in his eyes; and Mopsa Gedge, a thin dark woman in grey, with a book, a shawl over her arm and an expression.

**Tell Mrs Cherrett you will be her legs for the Season**

‘Good,’ she says, and counts five coins into your hand out of a purse like a pudding-cloth. ‘That’s for now. There’s more for results.’ She settles back. ‘Now. Here’s what I want. Ismay, my granddaughter, is to marry the Honourable Rupert Wenlock on Monday; the banns are read the third time on Sunday, at Saint Ebba’s; you’ll see it isn’t spoiled. Rob, my great-nephew, has my money after me and nothing in his head but poetry, and he’s to be engaged by the Ball on Monday to a sensible girl, I’ve three in mind. And Mopsa, my companion, is twenty-eight and has no money and a tongue on her, and she must be married off before she’s a poor relation, which is the worst thing a woman can be, I’ve seen it.’ She folds her hands on her stick. ‘And Mr Twiss has barred me from his Rooms, for laughing at a minuet, so none of it can be done at the Ball unless that’s mended. Well? Go on.’ Tamsin gains 50 experience: became Mrs Cherrett’s legs.

The Abbey Yard: the church, the Pump Room, the Parade going up the hill, and everybody looking at everybody.

**Into the Pump Room**

The Pump Room: a long cool room of pale stone, with tall windows, a gallery for musicians, and at one end a marble urn from which a man in a waistcoat dispenses the hot waters of Hessel by the glass, threepence. Over the urn, in gold letters, WATER IS BEST. Everyone in Hessel comes here at eight in the morning to drink a glass, and grimace, and look at everybody else, and write their names in the great book by the door. Here are Mrs Abigail Cherrett, a vast old woman in purple, with a laugh like a dropped coal-scuttle; Ismay Cherrett, a quiet young woman with a ledger and ink on her finger; Mrs Tabitha Scrope, a small bright widow in lavender, with a glass of the waters she never drinks; Lady Wenlock, a long thin lady in black with a face like a closed umbrella; and the pumper, an in a striped waistcoat, at the urn.

**Tell Mrs Scrope, in confidence, that the Cherrett money is all in canal shares, and the canals are failing**

It is not true. Mrs Scrope does not know that; neither, you suspect, would she much mind. ‘Canals,’ she says, with a beautiful look of sorrow. ‘Oh, the poor things. And the Wenlocks so very particular about money.’ She pats your hand. ‘You were right to tell me. Who else would you tell?’

**Ask Mrs Scrope what the news is**

‘Oh, the news,’ says Mrs Scrope, and tells you about a colonel’s gout, a bishop’s niece, a pair of twins in the King’s Bath who are not, after all, the same person, and a bonnet. Then she leans close. ‘And they’re saying the Cherrett girl’s banns are as good as read, and the Wenlocks will have the canal money by Monday, and Dr Hartopp has his eye on the companion, or the granddaughter, depending who you ask.’ She sips water she does not swallow.

The coaches go at noon from the Abbey Yard, and you watch them from the Pump Room colonnade, where nobody looks at you. Mrs Cherrett’s goes past with the blinds half down. In the second, Mopsa Gedge sees you, and looks at you for a long moment, and does not wave. The steam stands up off the Baths, and the bells of Saint Ebba’s ring for nothing in particular, and the Season is over, and you were in it, and it was not yours. Ismay Cherrett goes home unmarried, with her ledger, which is more than she came with. The Honourable Rupert Wenlock goes home with his mother, and his secret, and his beetle. Rob Cherrett goes home unengaged, with eleven bad poems, and looks back at the Crescent from the coach until it is gone. At the end of the Crescent the shutters of Carth House are closed and its chimneys smoke, and tonight at two someone will come up the Parade in a grey cloak and knock once. Dr Ottery packed his bag and went, and nobody in Hessel asked where. Mopsa Gedge goes home in the second coach with her book and Mrs Cherrett’s shawl, a companion still, and looks out of the window at Quay Lane as it goes by. Dr Hartopp is not seen at the Rooms, and his practice has fallen away by half, and he is very pleasant to everyone who is left.
