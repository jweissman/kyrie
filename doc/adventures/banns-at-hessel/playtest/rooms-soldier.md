*(The first page)*

The south road comes down out of the green country to a river, and on the far side of the river is Hessel, all of it at once, going up its hill: first the Old Town's roofs, wet and close, with steam coming out of them as if the whole place were a kettle; then a great dark church and a long pale pillared building in a haze of more steam; then terraces, honey-coloured, curving round the hill like the rings of a sawn log; and on the top of the hill, against the sky, a castle that is, when you look at it properly, only a wall with holes in it. The air smells of warm iron.

**To the foot of Lim Bridge**

The foot of Lim Bridge: fish-baskets, a tar-pot, washing strung between upper windows, and the river going by, brown and quick. A board on the toll-house says WELCOME TO HESSEL, THE QUEEN OF HEALING, and somebody has added in chalk AND OF HEALERS.

**To Quay Lane**

Quay Lane: narrow houses, a milliner’s with three bonnets in the window on wooden heads, a lodging-house with a card in its window, and at the end the steam of the laundries rolling across the lane like weather. Number Four is locked, with a card in the window: TO LET, A GOOD LODGING-HOUSE, APPLY MR GILMOUR, DAYTIMES.

**To Stall Street, by the chairmen's stand**

Stall Street: the chairmen’s stand, a long bench under a penthouse roof, and the chairs themselves, black, glossy, like great beetles with their poles laid by them; men in blue coats sitting on the bench with their arms folded, every one of them built like a door. Big Lanyon, the chairmen’s spokesman, a huge mild man with a voice like a bell under water, is here.

**To the Abbey Yard, at the foot of the Parade**

Somebody at the top of the Parade screams, and then everybody does. Down the hill toward you, gathering speed on the ridged paving, comes a bath-chair, a great wicker thing on three iron wheels, and in it, upright and furious and holding on to her bonnet, a very large old woman in purple. Behind it, much too far behind it, a chairman in a blue coat is running and shouting. The chair is aimed at the Pump Room colonnade, and the colonnade is stone. The Abbey Yard, at the foot of the Parade: on one side the great dark front of Saint Ebba’s, carved all over with angels climbing ladders; on another the colonnade of the Pump Room; and between them the Parade going up the hill so steeply that the paving is cut into ridges for the chairmen’s feet. Everyone here is either ill, or with someone who is, or selling something to both. Here are Mrs Abigail Cherrett, a vast old woman in purple, with a laugh like a dropped coal-scuttle; and Kit Danby, a young chairman in a blue coat, with a book in his pocket.

**Seize the side of the chair and drag it round into the church railings**

You catch the side of the chair as it comes level and haul it round, and it leaves the colonnade and goes instead into the railings of Saint Ebba’s with a noise like a dropped dresser, and stops, half in and half out of them, with a wheel going round in the air. Your arm is wrenched half out of its socket. The old woman in it straightens her bonnet. ‘That,’ she says, ‘is the second time in my life a stranger has thrown me at a church.’ Dick resists (dexterity). Dick gains 100 experience: stopped Mrs Cherrett’s chair.

**Ask the old woman if she is hurt**

‘Hurt? I cooked for four hundred navvies on the Delve cut through the wettest winter God ever sent,’ says the old woman. ‘I am Abigail Cherrett, and I am not hurt. I am dying, but that’s another thing, and not your fault.’ She looks at you again, longer. ‘You’ve got legs,’ she says. ‘Mine are done, and that one’s are’ (the chairman, scarlet, holding his hat) ‘evidently not to be trusted. I’ve a granddaughter to marry, and a great-nephew, and a companion, by the Closing Ball on Monday, and I’m in a town where I can’t get up a hill. I’ll pay you to be my legs for the Season. Fetch and carry. Go where I can’t. Tell me what’s what.’ She sticks out a hand the size of a spade. ‘Mrs Cherrett’s lodgings, the Circus. Come up when you’ve a mind to say yes.’

**Ask the young chairman how the chair got away from him**

He goes red to the ears. ‘I looked up,’ he says. ‘At the top of the Parade. I looked up and I let go the strap.’ He turns his hat round in his hands. ‘I’ve carried for nine years and never let go a strap. I looked up.’ He does not say at what, and you can see that he will not.

**To the top of the Parade**

The top of the Parade, where the hill levels: you can see the whole of Hessel below you from here, the Baths smoking and the Old Town smoking and the river. Two terraces go off from here, the Crescent to the left and the Circus to the right, and a path goes on up to the downs and the Beacon.

**To the Circus**

The Circus: a ring of tall houses round a round lawn, every one alike, columns on columns, so that wherever you stand you are looking at the same house. On one side, the Assembly Rooms; on another, a door with a brass plate, MR S. TWISS, M.C.; on another, the lodgings Mrs Cherrett has taken for the Season. Mr Twiss’s door bears a card, beautifully lettered: THE MASTER OF THE CEREMONIES RECEIVES BETWEEN TEN AND SIX. The Assembly Rooms are dark, and their great door is shut.

**Ask Mopsa what she makes of the household**

‘The household.’ Mopsa closes her book on her finger. ‘Mrs Cherrett is the best woman in Hessel and has been told she is the vulgarest so often she’s started to collect it, like china. Ismay is the cleverest and has been taught to be quiet about it. Rob is the kindest and has been taught nothing at all. I’m the companion.’ She opens the book again. ‘Companions don’t make anything of anything. We’re paid not to.’

**Ask Mopsa how one apologises to Mr Twiss**

‘In his parlour,’ says Mopsa at once. ‘Between ten and six. By one of the household, in so many words. You name the Rule. You use the word regret. Never sorry. Mr Twiss holds that sorry is for servants and dogs.’ She turns a page. ‘She’ll never do it. She laughed at the Countess of Arne’s minuet because the Countess of Arne was dancing it like a heron with gout, and she’ll die before she says she regrets it.’ A pause. ‘Which is soon, so we must hurry.’

**Ask Mopsa about Dr Hartopp**

‘Dr Hartopp.’ Mopsa smiles thinly. ‘Dr Hartopp attended Mrs Cherrett’s heart in March and attended me on the back stairs in April, by surprise, from behind, at night. I happened to be carrying the warming-pan up to her bed.’ She turns a page. ‘It was a good heavy brass one. He wore his hat lower for a fortnight.’ She looks up. ‘He’s doubled her drops since she put him in her will. I can’t prove it and I’m the companion and nobody would listen if I could. There.’

**Laugh, about the warming-pan**

You laugh, and after a moment so does she, properly, with her head back, a laugh that nobody in the Circus has ever heard out of Mrs Cherrett’s companion. ‘Nobody’s laughed,’ she says, wiping her eyes. ‘They’ve been shocked, or sorry for me, or they’ve asked if I gave him encouragement. You’re the first that laughed.’

**Ask Mopsa how Mrs Cherrett came to marry**

Mopsa looks at you with something like approval. ‘Now that’s a good question. Nobody asks it, because they think they know: money married money.’ She lowers her voice, not much. ‘Her father kept a public on the Wem road and meant her for a chandler with a wooden leg and a shop. At nineteen she went out of the window to Josiah Cherrett, who was a navvy digging the Wem cut, with a shovel and a laugh and nothing else in the world, and cooked for his gang in a hut for twenty years before there was a penny.’ She smiles, which is rare and alarming. ‘She’ll tell you she was a fool. Ask her if she’d do it again.’

**Ask Mopsa what she wants**

‘Nobody,’ says Mopsa, ‘has ever asked me that in Hessel. In Hessel I’m a piece of furniture that reads aloud.’ She considers you. ‘A house. Number Four, Quay Lane, in the Old Town. Six rooms, a copper, a yard, a view of the laundries. To let, forty down to Mr Gilmour and a character from somebody he thinks is somebody. I’d take in lodgers. Invalids’ maids and chairmen’s widows and the curate. I would answer the door to nobody I didn’t like.’ She looks at the window. ‘Mrs Cherrett means to marry me to a widower in Bath with five children. She means it kindly. Everybody means it kindly.’

**Ask Ismay what she makes of Hessel**

‘Hessel?’ Ismay considers it, the way she considers a column of figures. ‘Everybody here is buying something,’ she says. ‘Health, or a husband, or a reputation, or just a look at somebody who has one. Grandmother is buying all three for us.’ She turns her pen over. ‘I keep the accounts. I know to the penny what I’m costing.’

**Ask Ismay about the Honourable Rupert Wenlock**

‘He’s kind,’ says Ismay. ‘He’s very kind. He’s frightened of his mother and he likes beetles. He has never once looked at me as if he saw me.’ She is quiet. ‘I don’t think he wants to marry me any more than I want to marry him. I think he’s frightened of something else, and I don’t know what.’ She looks at the window. ‘The banns have been read twice. On Sunday it’s the third time. Then it’s done.’

**Tell Mrs Cherrett that Ismay will never stand up at a ball in Hessel unless she makes her peace with Mr Twiss**

‘Peace!’ Mrs Cherrett’s stick comes down on the floor. ‘With that pink-heeled dancing-master! I laughed because the old heron was funny, and so she was.’ She glares at the stuffed pike. Then she looks at Ismay, at her ledger by the window, and something in her face gives. ‘Oh, very well,’ she says. ‘Go and tell him I regret it. I don’t. But you can tell him so. I’ll not choke on the word myself, mind.’

**Into Mr Twiss's house**

**Ask Mr Twiss about the Rules of the Rooms**

‘The Rules.’ Mr Twiss touches the frame on his wall, the way a man touches wood. ‘You think them trifles. Everybody does, who has not thought. No boots after six: because a ballroom is a place where a duchess and a draper’s widow may stand up in the same set, and they must not be able to tell from the floor which is which. No lady to dance two running with one gentleman unless betrothed: because otherwise a girl may be compromised in twenty minutes by a man she would not let into her kitchen. No dancing after eleven: because the old must go home to bed, and if the music goes on, they will not.’ He folds his hands. ‘Every Rule of the Rooms is a kindness that has had to be made into a law, because people are not kind unless they are made to be. I am the Master of the Ceremonies. I am the one who makes them.’

**Express Mrs Cherrett's regret to Mr Twiss, in form, naming the Rule**

When you have done he opens them. ‘Regret,’ he says softly. ‘Not sorry.’ He takes a card from his desk and writes on it, in a hand like a garden in a frost. ‘Mrs Cherrett and her household,’ he says, ‘are welcome at the Rooms.
