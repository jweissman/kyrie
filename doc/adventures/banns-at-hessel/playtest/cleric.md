# The Prayers

Played by a dwarf cleric; seed `c1`, steps `test/fixtures/hessel/cleric.json`; 119 steps; ended at watch 600, at the ending "The Season's End". Written by `bun scripts/hessel-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The south road comes down out of the green country to a river, and on the far side of the river is Hessel, all of it at once, going up its hill: first the Old Town's roofs, wet and close, with steam coming out of them as if the whole place were a kettle; then a great dark church and a long pale pillared building in a haze of more steam; then terraces, honey-coloured, curving round the hill like the rings of a sawn log; and on the top of the hill, against the sky, a castle that is, when you look at it properly, only a wall with holes in it. The air smells of warm iron.

**To the foot of Lim Bridge**

The foot of Lim Bridge: fish-baskets, a tar-pot, washing strung between upper windows, and the river going by, brown and quick. A board on the toll-house says WELCOME TO HESSEL, THE QUEEN OF HEALING, and somebody has added in chalk AND OF HEALERS.

**To Quay Lane**

Quay Lane: narrow houses, a milliner’s with three bonnets in the window on wooden heads, a lodging-house with a card in its window, and at the end the steam of the laundries rolling across the lane like weather. Number Four is locked, with a card in the window: TO LET, A GOOD LODGING-HOUSE, APPLY MR GILMOUR, DAYTIMES.

**To Stall Street, by the chairmen's stand**

Stall Street: the chairmen’s stand, a long bench under a penthouse roof, and the chairs themselves, black, glossy, like great beetles with their poles laid by them; men in blue coats sitting on the bench with their arms folded, every one of them broad enough to stop the Parade. Big Lanyon, the chairmen’s spokesman, a huge mild man with a voice like a bell under water, is here.

**To the Abbey Yard, at the foot of the Parade**

Somebody at the top of the Parade screams, and then everybody does. Down the hill toward you, gathering speed on the ridged paving, comes a bath-chair, a great wicker thing on three iron wheels, and in it, upright and furious and holding on to her bonnet, a very large old woman in purple. Behind it, much too far behind it, a chairman in a blue coat is running and shouting. The chair is aimed at the Pump Room colonnade, and the colonnade is stone. The Abbey Yard, at the foot of the Parade: on one side the great dark front of Saint Ebba’s, carved all over with angels climbing ladders; on another the colonnade of the Pump Room; and between them the Parade going up the hill so steeply that the paving is cut into ridges for the chairmen’s feet. Everyone here is either ill, or with someone who is, or selling something to both. Here are Mrs Abigail Cherrett, a vast old woman in purple, with a laugh like a dropped coal-scuttle; and Kit Danby, a young chairman in a blue coat, with a book in his pocket.

**Wait › A while**

Time passes. The chair goes past you and hits the bottom step of the colonnade and turns over, and the old woman comes out of it like a sack off a cart. When the crowd has her up she is sitting on the step with her ankle swelling, swearing like a canal gang. They are going to carry her up to her lodgings in the Circus, in her own chair.

*(1 wait in which nothing is told)*

**Tell Mrs Cherrett you are new in Hessel, and want work**

Mrs Cherrett looks at you from where they have propped her, with her bad ankle up on somebody’s hat-box. ‘You were in the Abbey Yard,’ she says. ‘I saw you. You stood and watched, like the rest of them.’ You admit it. ‘Well, you’re honest, which is more than the rest of them, and you’ve come up the hill to say so, which is more again.’ She pats the coverlet. ‘I’m in a town where I couldn’t get up a hill, and now I can’t get up at all. I’ve a granddaughter to marry, and a great-nephew, and a companion, by the Closing Ball. I’ll pay you to be my legs. Fetch and carry; go where I can’t; tell me what’s what. And next time a chair comes down a hill at you, do something.’

**Strap Mrs Cherrett's ankle properly, and get her up**

You strap it firm, the way a bonesetter does, with a wet bandage that will shrink, and a stick under her other arm, and she gets up and stamps on it, and swears, and stamps again. ‘Well,’ she says. ‘Somebody in this town knows something.’

**Tell Mrs Cherrett it was not the chairman's fault**

‘Not his fault!’ says Mrs Cherrett, from where they have propped her. ‘He let go of me on the Parade! He let go of me like a hot potato!’ She glares at you. Then, because she is fair, she thinks. ‘What did he let go for, then?’ You do not know. ‘Well,’ she says. ‘Well. I’ll not turn a man off for a thing nobody can explain to me. Tell him he can carry me tomorrow. Tell him if he drops me again I’ll have him stuffed and put beside the pike.’ Agnes gains 50 experience: kept Kit Danby on the poles.

**To the top of the Parade**

The top of the Parade, where the hill levels: you can see the whole of Hessel below you from here, the Baths smoking and the Old Town smoking and the river. Two terraces go off from here, the Crescent to the left and the Circus to the right, and a path goes on up to the downs and the Beacon.

**To the Circus**

The Circus: a ring of tall houses round a round lawn, every one alike, columns on columns, so that wherever you stand you are looking at the same house. On one side, the Assembly Rooms; on another, a door with a brass plate, MR S. TWISS, M.C.; on another, the lodgings Mrs Cherrett has taken for the Season. On the Circus lawn the chairmen sit on the poles of Mrs Cherrett’s chair waiting for her to want it. The young one is reading a book with his back to her windows, and every so often, without lifting his head, he looks up at them. Mr Twiss’s door bears a card, beautifully lettered: THE MASTER OF THE CEREMONIES RECEIVES BETWEEN TEN AND SIX. The Assembly Rooms are dark, and their great door is shut. Kit Danby, a young chairman in a blue coat, with a book in his pocket, is here.

*(4 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Into Mrs Cherrett's lodgings**

Mrs Cherrett’s parlour: rented furniture, too good for anyone to sit on, and on top of it everything Mrs Cherrett has brought from home, which is a great deal, including a stuffed pike, a canal map of three counties framed in oak, and a footstool made of an elephant’s foot. The window looks down over the Circus lawn. Mrs Cherrett sits in the middle of it all like the stuffed pike’s mother, with her stick across her knees, and misses nothing. Ismay is standing at the window with her ledger open in her hands. Down on the Circus lawn the chairmen are waiting with Mrs Cherrett’s chair, sitting on its poles, and one of them, the young one, is reading a book. Ismay does not turn a page all the time you are in the room. Mrs Cherrett’s bedroom door is shut, and her maid sits on a chair in front of it with her knitting, and looks at you over it. On the canal map over the fireplace, one line is drawn in red pencil, and stops. Here are Mrs Abigail Cherrett, a vast old woman in purple, with a laugh like a dropped coal-scuttle; Ismay Cherrett, a quiet young woman with a ledger and ink on her finger; Rob Cherrett, a long young man with ink on his cuffs and his hair in his eyes; and Mopsa Gedge, a thin dark woman in grey, with a book, a shawl over her arm and an expression.

**Tell Mrs Cherrett you will be her legs for the Season**

‘Good,’ she says, and counts five coins into your hand out of a purse like a pudding-cloth. ‘That’s for now. There’s more for results.’ She settles back. ‘Now. Here’s what I want. Ismay, my granddaughter, is to marry the Honourable Rupert Wenlock on Monday; the banns are read the third time on Sunday, at Saint Ebba’s; you’ll see it isn’t spoiled. Rob, my great-nephew, has my money after me and nothing in his head but poetry, and he’s to be engaged by the Ball on Monday to a sensible girl, I’ve three in mind. And Mopsa, my companion, is twenty-eight and has no money and a tongue on her, and she must be married off before she’s a poor relation, which is the worst thing a woman can be, I’ve seen it.’ She folds her hands on her stick. ‘And Mr Twiss has barred me from his Rooms, for laughing at a minuet, so none of it can be done at the Ball unless that’s mended. Well? Go on.’ Agnes gains 50 experience: became Mrs Cherrett’s legs.

**Watch Ismay while the chairmen wait below, and read her face · an even chance**

You watch her for a quarter of an hour. Every time the young chairman below turns a page, Ismay stops writing. Every time he looks up at the windows, she looks down at the ledger. When he laughs at something one of the others says, she smiles, and does not know she has. It is not a puzzle. It is the plainest thing in the room. Agnes makes the insight check (d20 20 + wisdom 2 + insight 2 = 24 against 13).

**To Mrs Cherrett's bedroom**

Mrs Cherrett’s bedroom: a great curtained bed, a fire kept in in all weathers, and a dressing-table crowded with pins and powders and bottles. A little brown bottle of Hessel drops, a third full.

**Examine — Look closely**

You look closely at the little brown bottle on the dressing-table. You hold the bottle to the light. It smells of cloves and of something under the cloves, sweetish and heavy, that you would know again.

**Reckon the drops in the bottle against the date on its label**

The label is dated eleven days ago; the bottle held two hundred drops when it was filled, by the apothecary’s mark; there are not eighty left in it. Forty a night, every night, since it came up the hill. Whoever wrote TWENTY and scratched it out meant it once. Agnes gains 100 experience: found out what was in Mrs Cherrett’s drops.

*(15 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

An hour passes. It is a long time since you slept. You are weary, and will fight the worse for it until you sleep.

*(3 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

SATURDAY. The bells of Saint Ebba’s ring six over the steam. They have rung it since the abbey was a sty: Ebba was a swineherd’s daughter, the story goes, whose lame pigs wandered up the hill and came back whole and steaming, and she followed them to the hot spring; the pigs are carved among the angels on the west front, if you know where to look. Below the bridge the water-carts are filling from the river, because nobody in Hessel drinks the waters who can help it. Up on the Circus a maid is beating a carpet out of a window, and the sound comes down the hill a moment late.

*(23 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

In the Abbey Yard and on Stall Street and at the foot of the Parade, the chairmen are laying their poles crossways on their chairs. In Hessel that means no. SUNDAY: the third time of asking. Saint Ebba’s rings all eight bells for an hour before service, a peal the ringers of Orm have called wrong in the fourth change for two hundred years. The Season comes out in its second-best, keeping the best for the Ball, and the Pump Room is shut, so it has nowhere to look at itself but in church. At eleven Mr Pyne will read the banns for the last time, and come to the place where he asks if any of you know cause, and pause, as he always does, because he is young and dreads it.

*(23 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

MONDAY, the last day of the Season. At first light the Rooms’ five chandeliers are let down on their chains, and men on ladders fit eight hundred and forty new candles, the number the Rooms’ accounts have recorded for every Closing Ball since the old King came for his gout and could not dance at it. Every milliner in Quay Lane has her shutters down by six. At ten, if nobody has spoken, there is a wedding at Saint Ebba’s; at seven, the Ball; at eleven, Mr Twiss will lift his hand.

*(23 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

TUESDAY, the morning the Season ends. Before it is light the coaches are brought round into the Abbey Yard nose to tail, and the horses stand in the cold with their breath going up like the Baths. Trunks come down the Parade on the chairmen’s backs. Up at the Circus, at eight, Mopsa Gedge will take in Mrs Cherrett’s chocolate, as she does every morning, and knock, and go in.

*(5 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › An hour**

Mrs Cherrett did not wake on the last morning of the Season, and the coaches went without her at noon from the Abbey Yard, and her own went last, empty but for Mopsa, Ismay and Rob, and her stick, which nobody could bring themselves to put in the boot. They bury her at home, by Josiah, under a stone she wrote herself: ABIGAIL CHERRETT, WHO COOKED FOR THE DELVE CUT. It says nothing about the forty thousand pounds. Everybody who was at the Rooms that Season remembers the laugh, and nobody remembers why she was barred. Ismay Cherrett goes home unmarried, jilted at the altar, the Season says, though it was not her that was left. She sits in the coach with her ledger on her knee and looks out of the window at Stall Street as it goes by, at the chairmen’s bench, and does not turn a page. The Wenlocks left Hessel by the first coach after the scandal, Lady Wenlock in one and her son, his wife and child in another, going different ways. Mrs Scrope has told the story so often she has begun to improve it. Rob Cherrett goes home unengaged, with eleven bad poems, and looks back at the Crescent from the coach until it is gone. At the end of the Crescent the shutters of Carth House are closed and its chimneys smoke, and tonight at two someone will come up the Parade in a grey cloak and knock once. Dr Ottery packed his bag and went, and nobody in Hessel asked where. Mopsa Gedge goes home in the second coach with her book and Mrs Cherrett’s shawl, a companion still, and looks out of the window at Quay Lane as it goes by. Dr Hartopp has eleven new patients for next Season and a hundred pounds in prospect, and prescribes hope, which he never charges for.
