# The Banns at Hessel: a second pass, read as literature

*Written after the first final critique ([12](12-critique.md)), in answer to the question: is there real depth here, or
only exuberance? It is in three parts. First, what I was not happy with when I read Hessel as a story rather than as a
system. Second, what I changed. Third, where I would start if the ambition were ten times larger.*

## 1. What I was not happy with

### The surface was louder than the depths

Read straight through, the transcripts were witty, and the wit had a machine in it. Every paragraph reached for a
simile. Three people had a "face like a closed" something (an umbrella, a door, a shut Bible). The chairmen were
"built like a door". People looked at each other "for a long time" nine times. Mrs Cherrett was a run of zingers: the
dropped coal-scuttle, the stuffed pike, "I'm not crying, it's the dropsy". That is the comic grotesque, and some of it
is good: "the second time in my life a stranger has thrown me at a church" is a real line. But at that density it
becomes pulp exuberance. It is a voice performing itself, and it flattens everyone it describes into a type: the
Vulgar Rich Widow with a Heart of Gold, the Wicked Doctor, the Mad Baronet. The commission asked for obsessive weirdness
latched onto a form. The weirdness was there; the form was underneath it and mostly unconnected.

### Revelation without foreshadowing

Every secret in Hessel was learned the same way: the player asks the right person, and they tell. Nothing was
*noticed first and understood later*. The knocker, Rupert's marriage, the drops and Celia's lungs each arrived as a
paragraph of exposition. Literary depth in a mystery comes from details that mean one thing on the first reading and
another on the second. Hessel had almost none. The one it had, Mrs Cherrett's "thrown me at a church", was never paid
off. Kit's "I looked up" was never answered either.

### A world with names and no history

Hessel is Bath, fondly borrowed: the Circus, the Crescent, the Pump Room, WATER IS BEST, the Sham Castle, the King's
Bath, and a Master of the Ceremonies. The borrowing is honest, but the world beyond the town was a list of names: Orm,
Wem, the Delve cut, Ryle, the Countess of Arne, the Great Snow. They had no weight, because nothing connected them.
The Great Snow was only Carth's catastrophe. Josiah Cherrett was only a punchline about a navvy. The Countess of Arne
was only a heron with gout.

### The villain was cardboard

Dr Hartopp doubled a dose for a legacy and a marriage, and was defeated by a dress code. That was a deliberate joke,
but it left him without an interior. He had no origin, no case for himself, and no scheme beyond the bottle. A player
could finish the Season without speaking to him.

### The themes were latent and unsounded

Reading for pattern, I found three themes running through the strands without being used:

- **Asking.** Mopsa: "Nobody has ever asked me that in Hessel." Lanyon: "Nobody ever asked her why." Ismay: "I've
  never asked her for anything. Not once. Not a ribbon." Mrs Cherrett asks the stranger what she should do. Hessel is a
  town where nobody asks, and the protagonist's verb is asking. That is the region's real subject, and it is good.
- **Carrying.** The chairmen carry the Season uphill for ninepence. The company is hired as "legs". Ottery carries his
  bag to Orm, Nan carries the Season's linen, and Twiss holds a dying woman up for a minuet. Who carries whom, and who
  looks down to see?
- **The hour.** Carth's clocks are stopped at two. Twiss stops the band at eleven. The coaches go at noon. Mrs Cherrett
  wants to "die awake". Every strand is about a stopped or a running clock.

All three were present, and none was ever said or echoed on purpose.

### Little dramaturgy

The days blurred. There were no chapter breaks, and the transcripts show runs of fourteen and seventeen waits in
which nothing is told. There was a structure (arrival, the round, Sunday, the Ball, the morning), but the page did not
mark it. Each day did not open as a new movement with its own weather, sounds and purpose.

### Danger that was only a mechanism

The two fights (the footpads; Sir Gervase in his hall) were engine encounters. The only way past them was the
engine's generic one: pay, persuade, warn, fight. Nothing the company had *learned* could stop a sword. In a
story about secrets that is a waste: the most powerful thing a player holds in Hessel is a truth, and the truth could
not be used where it mattered most.

### What was already right

The structure holds. Eleven strands, two different principal ways, a closing account that reads every strand, a
region placed in a world. The best scenes are good, because they are quiet: the sit-up in the hall, Nan at the copper,
Ottery at the bucket with his back turned waiting to hear what you will tell the shepherd, Twiss holding Mrs Cherrett
up through the minuet. The Ball already offered dance choices that matter (standing up with Ismay cuts Hartopp out).
The first critique said the Ball was only watched; that was wrong.

## 2. What changed

### One buried history that ties the strands together

**The two tablets in Saint Ebba's.** A new thing to look at under the west window. One is a white marble memorial:
SACRED TO THE MEMORY OF HONORIA, LADY CARTH, TAKEN IN THE GREAT SNOW… HER HUSBAND, WHO DOES NOT ASK TO BE FORGIVEN,
RAISED THIS. It is a memorial to a woman who is alive and washing sheets at the bottom of the hill, with fresh
rosemary on its ledge. Beside it is a cheap slate one, HE CAME TOO LATE, signed *J. O.* in the corner. Read before
the knocker is found, the first is foreshadowing. Read after, it is horror and comedy at once: Nan puts rosemary
under her own name every Sunday, "and nobody looks at a laundress".

The slate is the hinge. Nan, asked about it, tells the thing nobody in the valley knows. She was carrying when she
was put out in the snow, and the child was born in the laundry yard that night and did not live. Ottery came through
the snow, came too late, cried, paid for the slate himself, and has kept her secret for nine years. That changes
three strands at once:

- **Carth House** gets a buried grief under the comedy of the stopped clocks.
- **The Road to Orm** gets a personal stake. Ottery, who tells every patient the truth and asks the stranger on the road
  whether the dying should be told, has kept one truth back for nine years at someone else's asking. Asked about the
  slate, he will not tell: "Some promises are worse than lies. They last longer." His three questions on the road are
  now about himself.
- **The ending** gets a new line. If the door is opened and the company knows about the child, Sir Gervase takes the
  marble down himself, with a crowbar, in front of the town; and Lady Carth asks for a name to be cut on the slate.

### The Great Snow is everyone's year

Josiah Cherrett died that same spring. He walked the line of his fourth canal up the Lim valley in the wet, to talk
the Corporation round, took a chill on the down, and died in the back room of the Two Poles. **The canal map** in
the parlour, which until now was a joke about Mrs Cherrett's furniture, has a red pencil line that stops a mile short
of Hessel, with *here* written beside the blot. Asked about it, Mrs Cherrett is quiet for the first time in the
region: "I've never known if he meant here's where the cut stops, or here's where I do. That's why I come to Hessel.
Not the waters. The waters are muck." So her thanks, Josiah's compass, now carries the red line with it: "He'd want
it to go somewhere that isn't a wall."

### Foreshadowing, and payoffs for what was left hanging

- **Cloves.** Hartopp's role always said he smelt of cloves; the drops bottle smelt of cloves under the cloves. Now
  the footpads' new purse smells of cloves, and the boy who confesses says the gentleman who paid them "smelt of
  cloves. Like a pudding." The game never says *Hartopp*; the player smells it.
- **"The second time a stranger has thrown me at a church"** is paid off. Mopsa's story and Mrs Cherrett's own memory
  now end with Josiah carrying her up the aisle at Wem "like a sack of meal, with Father's dogs in the porch".
- **"I looked up"** is paid off. Ask Ismay where she was when the chair ran away: on the Circus steps, and she waved,
  "and Grandmother went down the Parade on her own, faster than the mail coach, because I waved". She laughs, for the
  first time in the region.
- **Mopsa's "since I was nineteen"** is paid off as an echo. At nineteen her father died owing money and she went into
  service: "At nineteen Mrs Cherrett went out of a window. At nineteen I went into a drawer. I've been thinking,
  lately, that I'd like the window."
- **Twiss and the Countess of Arne.** The two portraits in his parlour (dancing; not dancing) are explained. In his
  first Season he let the band play past eleven for a young lady who asked, and was out of the Rooms for a year; he
  made the Rules when he came back, and has not danced since except to open the Ball. The young lady is the Countess of
  Arne, who has forgotten her feet in the second figure for thirty years without anyone being unkind enough to notice,
  until Mrs Cherrett. His offence at the laugh is now a wound, not a fussiness. When he bends the eleven o'clock Rule
  for Mrs Cherrett, it is the second time in his life.

### A villain with an origin and a case

The **pumper** knows: Ambrose Hartopp washed glasses under this counter as a boy, went to Orm on his father's
savings, came back with a brass plate and a plum coat, and has never drunk the water or said good morning since. "It's
a long way up that hill, and he walked it." Faced with the dose, Hartopp **does not deny it**. He makes his case:
she has three months and pain every night; the money will go to a boy who writes about lattices; a physician in Hessel
has "eleven brass plates in one street"; "Hope is the cheapest physic. I never charge for it. I could not afford to."
Told that the pumper says good morning, he goes to his window over Doctors' Walk: "They carry you up. Nobody carries
you down. You fall." He is now the darkest voice of the carrying theme, the man who climbed the hill on his own and
is terrified of the way back. His scheme also reaches beyond the bottle: he paid the footpads to keep Ottery from the
College.

### Dangerous fights, with ways around them that use what is known

- **The footpads at the gibbet** are three now, which is a real danger to a level-1 company; beaten, it is robbed and
  left. They are Hartopp's hirelings, and the smallest wears his father's chairman's coat under his smock. Ways
  past: pay; persuade or warn (the shared rules); fight, after which Ottery kneels to them and the youngest confesses;
  or, for a company the chairmen know, **"Ask the one in the chairman's coat whether Big Lanyon knows where he is this
  morning"**. Davy has Lanyon's ears. He tells everything, the three run, and Ottery laughs until he has to wipe his
  eyes. Taken back to Big Lanyon ("My sister's boy. In his father's coat… Cloves. There's one man in Hessel smells of
  cloves"), it is a second way to make the chairmen refuse Hartopp: no drops evidence needed.
- **Sir Gervase in his hall at night** is a level-3 warrior with a sword and a warning. Fighting him is still possible
  and still loses badly. The new ways past are knowledge and nerve. **Tell him the letter in his wife's work-box was
  from her brother**, if the company has learned it from Nan or Mrs Brannagh: the sword stops ("which is worse to
  watch"), falls, and he sits down where he stands. Or **put your hands where he can see them and ask him whom he
  sits up for** (insight, hard): "Nobody. I sit up for nobody. It's warmer by the fire." Either way the company is
  received, and the sit-up for the knock becomes possible from a night break-in, not only from a daytime call.

### The themes said aloud, once each, by the right person

- **Carrying**, by Big Lanyon when the company stands with the chairmen: "Everybody in this town is carried by
  somebody. Mostly they don't look down to see who." Answered by Hartopp at his window.
- **Asking** needed nothing new. With the payoffs above, the pattern is visible: Ismay asks for the first time, Mopsa is
  asked for the first time, and Nan has never been asked why.
- **The hour** is in the chapters (below), and in the hall clocks that start again after the door is opened (already
  built, and now set up by the tablets).

### Dramaturgy: the days as chapters

Each of the Season's days now opens, on the first page after six that the company spends in the valley, with a
chapter. Each has its own sound, its own history, and its purpose for the day:

- **SATURDAY** opens with Saint Ebba's bells and the legend of the swineherd's daughter whose lame pigs found the hot
  spring (the pigs are carved among the angels on the west front).
- **SUNDAY: the third time of asking** opens with the peal the ringers of Orm have called wrong for two hundred years,
  and Mr Pyne, who will pause because he is young and dreads it.
- **MONDAY** opens with eight hundred and forty candles, the number the Rooms' accounts have recorded since the old
  King came for his gout and could not dance.
- **TUESDAY** opens with the coaches nose to tail in the dark: "Up at the Circus, at eight, Mopsa Gedge will take in
  Mrs Cherrett's chocolate, as she does every morning, and knock, and go in." The sentence reads the same whether she
  wakes or not.

### Pruned

The three "face like" roles were rewritten for what each person is. Mrs Brannagh: "with a Bible in her apron pocket
and coal-dust in the lines of her hands". The footman: "who has stood at that door for eleven Seasons and seen
everything twice". The chairmen are "broad enough to stop the Parade". Six of the nine "for a long time" are gone.
The narrator still loves a simile, and I have left most of them where they carry character. Hessel's register is
comedy, and comedy needs its timing.

### Is it playable and compelling as written?

Playable: yes. Every addition is tested (37 journey and wander tests), the region lints clean, and the wider wander
passes. Compelling: more than before, because a player now has things to *notice*, and noticing pays later: the
cloves at the gibbet, the rosemary under the tablet, the red line, the two portraits. The weakest remaining stretch is
still the waiting between appointments. The chapters break it up, but do not fill it.

## 3. If the ambition were ten times larger

I would start with time, not size.

1. **Play the Great Snow.** *A Winter in Carth*, the seed, has become the backstory, and it should be playable: a
   prologue nine years earlier, on the one night. The company is snowbound in Carth House. There is a letter in a
   work-box, a husband who has drunk too much, a woman in her nightgown, a door, and a doctor called out through the
   drifts to the laundry yard. What the company does that night is fixed by history: it cannot save the child. But it
   can be the one who carried the letter, or the one who held the lamp in the yard. The Season, nine years later, then
   remembers the company. Both of Kyrie's existing kinds of adventure, a short horror and a long comedy, would be one
   story told at two times.
2. **Seasons, plural.** Hessel holds a Season every year. With a world clock, the company could come back: Mrs
   Cherrett's grave or her coach; Mopsa's lodging-house with the curate in the first-floor back; Celia at her first
   Ball; Kit on a canal barge; Hartopp's brass plate in a colder town; the Lim cut begun or never. The closing account
   becomes the next Season's opening state.
3. **The Lim cut as a campaign.** Josiah's red line is a regional plot that reaches far beyond the town: the
   Corporation and the view from the Crescent, the chairmen who would become bargees, the carriers who would be ruined,
   and the College of Orm, which owns the land at the stile. With the compass, the company can survey the line;
   finishing it changes the valley's economy (prices, who works where, which inn is busy).
4. **Orm as a second city.** The cathedral town at the end of the road: the College, the surrogate, the bell-ringers
   with their two-hundred-year grievance, the bishop to whom Mopsa's father owed money. Hessel's opposite: a town of
   learned men and old rules, with no Season.
5. **The Season as a generator.** Matchmaking, gossip, the Rules and the round are systems. Generate the eligible
   young people, the debts, the rumours and the secrets for any spa town in any generated world, and Hessel becomes
   the authored exemplar of a kind of place, not a single place.
6. **A rumour economy.** Mrs Scrope is a tool with six inputs. At ten times the scale, every fact the company knows could
   be told to anyone, travel by who talks to whom, mutate on the way, and come back as weather. That is the
   form a comedy of manners really has: what people know about each other, and when.

The first two are where the depth is; the last four are where the breadth is. I would build the Great Snow prologue
first. It turns three strands' backstory into play, and it tests whether Kyrie can tell one story at two times.
