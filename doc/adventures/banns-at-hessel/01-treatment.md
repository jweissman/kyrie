# The Banns at Hessel — the original treatment

*Stage 1. Written before reading the engine for this purpose, and deliberately unconstrained by it. A first concept,
a frost fair, was withdrawn for its closeness to an outstanding branch ([00-withdrawn-treatment.md](00-withdrawn-treatment.md));
this replaces it. Stage 2 is [02-critique.md](02-critique.md).*

## 0. Where this starts

The commission asks for a metropolitan region of many small adventures, an overarching mystery, satire, intrigue or
romance, and a tone the collection lacks. It offers three seeds: *The Banns at Hessel* (romance and domestic comedy
over a wedding week), *A Winter in Carth* (horror in a snowbound farmhouse), *The Road to Orm* (a two-person
philosophical walk with a heretic going to trial).

What is already built or building, and must not be built again:

- **Wrayford**: a bridge city, a tragedy, the dead on the rail.
- **Pentrice**: a minster city of licences and precedence; a comedy of the theatre.
- **Pellam** (outstanding): a wool city's Frost Fair; ice-law; a con; a preacher of the Flow tried on the ice.
- **Calamy**: a city of paper whose books talk.

So no ice, no fair, no wool, no Flow heretic, no tribunal, no con, no Office, no books that speak. And the
repertoire's list of what Kyrie cannot yet say is the brief behind the brief: *wanting; fault; a person as an end;
making; comedy as structure; fear that lasts; the small that stays small; minds that change because of people.*

**The answer: a spa city in the last week of its Season.** A city whose whole economy is the two oldest markets in the
world, the cure and the match: the old come to Hessel to be mended, and the young to be married. It is a comedy of
manners about **matchmaking**, in which the protagonist is a meddler who will get people wrong (themselves included);
it holds, at the end of its loveliest terrace, a house where it has been winter for nine years; and it has a doctor in
it who was thrown out for telling the dying the truth, who must be walked to Orm.

## 1. The premise in a paragraph

Hessel lies in a deep green valley where hot springs come up steaming through the old town, and a new town of pale
stone crescents has been built up the hillside for the people who come to drink them. For three months a year the
Season fills it: invalids, widows, half-pay officers, heiresses, fortune-hunters, and the Master of Ceremonies who rules
them all. This is the last week. On Thursday is the Closing Ball, and on Friday everyone goes home. **Mrs Abigail
Cherrett**, a canal-cutter's widow with a navvy's laugh and forty thousand pounds, has brought her granddaughter, her
great-nephew and her paid companion to Hessel to get all three of them married before she dies, which her physician
says she will not and she knows she will. On the morning the protagonist arrives, her bath-chair breaks loose on the
steepest street in the city with her in it.

## 2. The protagonist

A stranger who catches a runaway bath-chair — or fails to, and is blamed — and is taken up by the woman in it: "You'll
do. I need legs, and mine are done." For a week they are Mrs Cherrett's *cavalier*: the one who fetches, carries,
escorts, introduces, overhears and arranges. She wants three marriages. They are her hands in a city of manners they
do not know.

This makes the protagonist a **meddler**, which is a position with fault built into it. Every match they make or
break is their doing. They will be wrong — about who loves whom, about who is lying, about what Mrs Cherrett wants —
and the comedy is built out of their errors as much as anyone's. They also *want* things, if the player lets them:
Mrs Cherrett's gratitude is worth money; the city is full of people to love; and her companion, Mopsa Gedge, is the
only person in Hessel who will tell them, to their face, what they are doing.

### The districts

**The Crescent and the Circus.** Thirty houses in a single sweep of honey-coloured stone, built to be looked at.
Every window has a lodger and every lodger is looking at every other. At the far end, the last house, **Carth House**,
has had its shutters closed for nine years. In the heat of the Season smoke comes out of all its chimneys.

**The Assembly Rooms.** The ballroom (five chandeliers, a gallery for the musicians, a wall of mirrors), the card room
(where the old play and the young are discussed), the tea room. **Mr Septimus Twiss**, the Master of Ceremonies,
rules them by the **Rules of the Rooms**, framed by the door: no boots after six; no lady to dance two dances running
with one gentleman unless betrothed; the dancing to stop at eleven, though the King himself should ask; no
introductions but by the Master. He can admit anyone to the Season and can bar anyone from it. He is ridiculous,
vain, sixty, beautifully dressed, and the most powerful person in Hessel.

**The Pump Room and the Baths.** At eight in the morning all Hessel drinks a glass of the hot water, which tastes of
flat-irons, and looks at itself. The **Pump Room book** where every arrival writes their name. The **King's Bath**,
where gentlemen and ladies stand to their necks in steaming brown water in canvas gowns and hats, with little floating
trays for their snuff, and the galleries above full of people watching them. The **Cross Bath**, the old one, where the
poor and the laundresses go.

**Doctors' Walk.** Eleven physicians in eleven houses, each prescribing the waters in a different quantity at a
different price for every complaint. The fashionable one is **Dr Ambrose Hartopp**, who has Mrs Cherrett in his care.

**Saint Ebba's.** Where the banns of **Ismay Cherrett** and **the Honourable Rupert Wenlock** have been read twice,
and will be read for the third time on Sunday by **Mr Pyne**, the curate.

**The Old Town.** Below the baths, by the river: the people who carry, wash, cook and nurse for the Season. The
**chairmen** (who carry the sick up the hill in sedan-chairs, two to a chair, by a fare-table the Corporation
sets), their stand and their tavern, the **Two Poles**. The **laundries**, steaming, where the bath-linen is washed. The
**Hospital of the Waters**, where the poor are treated free: or were, until its physician, **Dr Jonas Ottery**, was
turned off last month.

**The Downs.** Above the town: the Beacon walk, where lovers walk and are seen walking; the **Sham Castle**, a castle
front with nothing behind it, built so that the Crescent would have something to look at; the sheep-walk; and the road
over the down to **Orm**, a day's walk, where the College of Physicians sits.

**Nan Bellamy is Lady Carth.** She lived; she was taken in by the laundresses; she has washed the Season's linen for
nine years in the town under her husband's windows, and comes up the hill at two every night to knock, once, at the
door he shut on her, and goes away. Whether to be let in or to make him live with it, she has never decided.

## 5. The overarching story

### The comedy of matchmaking

Mrs Cherrett's three intended marriages, and what the protagonist finds:

1. **Ismay and Rupert.** Rupert is married already. Ismay loves Kit. Lady Wenlock needs the money. Mrs Cherrett needs
   the name. The banns will be read for the third time on Sunday. If nobody speaks, Rupert will commit bigamy on
   Monday, and Hetty will be at the church door with the baby.
2. **Rob and somebody suitable.** Mrs Cherrett has three candidates. Rob loves the face at Carth House's window, and
   Celia Carth has never been introduced to anyone.
3. **Mopsa and anybody at all.** Mrs Cherrett thinks a companion should be married off before she becomes a poor
   relation. Mopsa wants a lease, not a husband. Dr Hartopp has suggested he might take her, if the dowry were large.

The protagonist begins by helping with Mrs Cherrett's plans, and the comedy is the discovery, one complication at a
time, that each plan is wrong, and that each right match needs something the protagonist must do with their hands, feet,
tongue or nerve.

### The mystery

Two, small and human, neither an inherited mechanism:

- **Who knocks at Carth House?** Found by watching at two in the morning, following the knocker down the hill, asking
  the chairmen (who carry everybody, and see everybody), or being told by Mrs Brannagh.
- **What is Dr Hartopp doing?** Found by noticing that Mrs Cherrett is sharper on mornings she missed her drops; by
  Jem Mallender, who mixes them; by Dr Ottery, who will name them at a sniff; by Mopsa, who has suspected for a month.

### The satire

Hessel's two markets, the **cure** and the **match**, are the same market. Physicians sell hope by the glass, the
Rooms sell introductions by the subscription, and the two meet in the person of a rich old woman whose body and
granddaughter are both for sale. The satirical figures:

- **Mr Twiss** and the Rules of the Rooms, which he enforces with the solemnity of scripture and which the protagonist
  can use: a rule broken in front of him is a public catastrophe.
- **Doctors' Walk**: eleven prescriptions for the same water.
- **The King's Bath**: the rich standing in hot water up to their necks, watched from the gallery.
- **The Sham Castle**: a castle built to be a view.
- **The chairmen's fare-table**, which charges by the distance and the weight of the passenger, and which the
  Corporation is debating whether to raise for "persons of size".

### The romance

None of it a meter. All of it built of acts.

- **Ismay and Kit**: the heiress and the chairman. Neither will speak first.
- **Rob and Celia**: the poet and the girl in the winter house.
- **Rupert and Hetty**: married already, and in need of courage, not love.
- **Mopsa and the protagonist**, if the player wishes: a courtship of insults across a week.
- **Mrs Cherrett and her own youth**: one dance.
- **Sir Gervase and Lady Carth**: a marriage nine years frozen.

### The philosophy

**Dr Ottery**'s question is the work's: *is it kinder to tell the truth, or to let people hope?* Hessel is a city built
on hope sold by the glass; Hartopp gives Mrs Cherrett hope and laudanum; Sir Gervase has told the world his wife is
dead for nine years; Rupert has let the banns be read; Ismay has not told her grandmother whom she loves. Every
deception in the city is a kindness to someone. Ottery's answer is that the truth is the only respect the living can
pay each other. The protagonist can argue the other side, and win.

### The horror

**Carth House** is winter in the middle of a Season. Shutters; fires in every grate; the heat of it in the hall like a
blow; furs on everyone; clocks stopped at two; the daughter grown up in candlelight; a man who has sat up every night
for nine years for a knock he dares not answer; a housekeeper who keeps her own counsel. It is *The Winter's Tale*
done as a ghost story, with no ghost.

## 6. The small adventures (as first imagined)

1. **The Runaway Chair.** On the Parade, the morning of arrival.
2. **Into the Rooms.** Mr Twiss has barred Mrs Cherrett (she laughed at a minuet). Without his leave none of the three
   can be at the Closing Ball, where matches are made.
3. **The Pump Room.** Eight o'clock, the waters and Mrs Scrope. Tell her something, and all Hessel knows it by dinner.
4. **The Chairman and the Heiress.** Kit and Ismay.
5. **Rob's Poems.** Delivering a poem to a shuttered window.
6. **A Winter in Carth.** Getting into Carth House; the house itself; the knock.
7. **The Laundress.** Who Nan Bellamy is.
8. **The Hessel Drops.** What Mrs Cherrett is being given.
9. **The Hospital of the Waters.** The poor without their doctor.
10. **The Road to Orm.** Dr Ottery walked to the College.
11. **Rupert's Secret.** The married bridegroom.
12. **The Third Reading.** Sunday at Saint Ebba's: "If any of you know cause, or just impediment…"
13. **Mopsa's Lease.** A lodging-house in the Old Town, and the money and the character it needs.
14. **The Chairmen's Fare.** The Corporation's table, and a strike.
15. **The Closing Ball.** Every match made or broken, in five dances.
16. **The Sham Castle.** A picnic, and an assignation.

## 7. Acts

- **Act I, Arrival (day 1).** The chair; Mrs Cherrett; the Circus; the Pump Room; the Rooms; the window.
- **Act II, the Season (days 2–3).** Matches pressed and resisted; Carth House at night; the drops; the third
  reading on Sunday.
- **Act III, the Road (day 3–4).** Dr Ottery to Orm, if the protagonist goes, and back.
- **Act IV, the Ball (day 4 night).** Five dances; Hartopp; the Carths; the last dance.
- **Coda, the morning after (day 5).** The Season ends. Weddings, departures, a death.

## 8. How it can end

An account, not a menu: each strand ends its own way, and the book closes on what all of them came to.

- **Ismay** marries Rupert (bigamously, until Hetty comes), Kit, Hartopp, or no one.
- **Rob** marries Celia, or a candidate, or goes home a poet.
- **Mopsa** takes her lease, marries the protagonist, or both, or goes on as a companion.
- **The Carths** reunited; or Celia taken out of the house and her father left in it; or the winter kept.
- **Dr Ottery** licensed again, struck off, or never delivered.
- **Mrs Cherrett** dies in her sleep on Hartopp's drops before the Ball; or dies after it, having danced; or lives,
  sharper, angrier and off the drops, to go home and frighten her lawyers.
- **Hartopp** exposed in the Rooms, quietly ruined by the chairmen, or prospering.

## 9. What is new for Kyrie in it

- Romance and marriage at the centre; six couples.
- A protagonist who meddles, and is at fault.
- Matchmaking as making.
- Gossip as a weapon (Mrs Scrope).
- Satire of a market in hope.
- A horror of a house, not a dungeon.
- A walk in which the argument is the play.
- No mechanism, no age, no other plane; ends in the city it began in.
