# A Frost at Hessel — the severe editorial review

*Stage 2. An attack on [01-treatment.md](01-treatment.md), written as if by an editor who did not like it much. The
revised design that answers it is [03-revised-design.md](03-revised-design.md).*

## The verdict in brief

The treatment has a premise worth building: a frozen city with a lawless second city on its river, and a bride
married in a mask to the wrong man. Its first scene puts the protagonist at fault, which no work in the collection
has done. Everything after that scene is overgrown. It names eighteen small adventures, and six of them are a
sentence each. It has three seeds and does not decide which is the spine. And it falls back, twice, on the
collection's habits that it says it is escaping.

## 1. Is the city alive without its mystery?

**Partly.** The Frost Fair is alive: a place with its own economy, built in a fortnight and gone in a night. The
watermen's penny toll is the best idea in the document, because it is satire with someone hungry at the end of it.
The Wool Quay is alive only as the wedding's backstage. Notaries' Row is a fee schedule and a joke. The Guildhall
is a joke told twice (the Corporation that will not decide; the Recorder who wants the coach on the ice). The South
bank has an inn and a dance and nothing else.

**What the city needs:** people who want things unrelated to Ismay's marriage, met in the course of ordinary
business: the watermen's hunger; the ox-roaster's fire; the printer's trade; a child on the ice. The city must have
things going on when the protagonist is not looking, on a clock of its own: the fair opens at noon and shuts at
dark, the skaters come out at dusk, the watermen meet at the stairs, the ice creaks at night.

## 2. Are the small adventures substantial and varied?

**No, not yet.** Measured against the commission's own list (a person with a motive, a place or thing that matters,
something misunderstood, more than one way, a complication, a consequence, a later effect):

| | Verdict |
|---|---|
| The Sable Hood | Strong. Keep as the inciting incident. |
| The Bride-Cake | Strong: making, judged later. But the saffron fetch is a fetch-quest. Needs a complication beyond "the seller won't sell". |
| Dumbrell and Hepworth | Strong as a relationship; weak as a mystery (the "stolen spoon that was not stolen" is the collection's misread-past device in miniature). Change it from a secret to a quarrel: both know exactly what happened; the play is getting either to say sorry. |
| The Penny at the Stairs | Strong. Needs its consequence (what the watermen do for those who stood with them). |
| The Lord Mayor's Coach | **A legal switch that decides the plot.** If the coach makes the ice city ground and so makes the ice weddings good, the climax is decided by procedure: a vote in disguise. Demote to a comic set piece with personal consequences; it must not decide whose marriage holds. |
| The Valentine Booth | Strong, and the place where the protagonist can *make* something (a letter). |
| The Three Brides | **Duplicated people.** Bess Corder and Lettice Amory are one character written twice. Merge into one second bride. |
| Nan and Tom | Strong emotionally; but "seal it with a Notary" makes the Notary the hero. Their marriage should be settled by them. |
| Rob's Ship | Good, but disconnected; it needs a scene where Rob must say it to his mother, and the protagonist's part in that scene. |
| Mr Pyne's Dream | A sketch. Fold into the banns. |
| The Assembly | Good occasion, no game yet. A dance must be a set of choices with consequences: who you dance with, and who sees. |
| The Ice Road | Three motives (ring, Kit, Thaw) for one road, which is good economy. Keep as one excursion. |
| The Hungry Guest | Strong premise; but see §6. |
| The Road to Orm | **A long passage of dialogue.** See §5. |
| The Thin Ice at the Piers | A rescue with no person. Who is the child? Whose? |
| The Ox | A gag. |
| The Press | A gag that could be a tool: print a broadside to answer Valentine. Keep only as a tool. |
| The Bridge-Houses' Privies | **Cut.** A cheap joke the work does not need. |

Eighteen is too many to build well; twelve with insides beat eighteen sketches.

## 3. Does the plot grow from the setting?

**The premise does; the resolution does not.** The ice makes masked weddings possible and gives the clock (the thaw).
But the treatment resolves the plot in three places at once: the coach (law), the bishop at Orm (the Church), and the
banns (the people). Two of those are institutions deciding for the people. The setting's own argument, which is
Thaw's argument, is that a vow is kept by the one who keeps choosing it. **The law should never decide.** The
Corporation should still have the ice *under consideration* when it melts. The bishop is a day's walk away and the
thaw is faster. Every marriage made on the ice must be settled by the people in it, and that is the comedy.

That also gives Valentine a proper weapon. If no court will rule, his leverage is not law but **scandal**: he means
to stand up in Saint Ebba's at the third reading and say "I know cause: she is my wife", unless he is paid to be
silent. That is a human threat with a date on it.

## 4. Is the satire, romance, horror, philosophy or comedy a game, or a long passage of dialogue?

- **Comedy** is a game only where it compounds. The treatment's compounding (the mistaking leads to Kit's leaving,
  leads to the search, leads to the second bride) is real. It must be in the clockwork, not in a summary: people act
  on what they believe, and what they believe is what the protagonist did.
- **Satire** is a game where the player can *use* the interests. The toll, the seal and the letter are usable. The
  coach is usable if it is a set piece. The Corporation is not usable at all; it is scenery. Keep it scenery.
- **Romance** is a dialogue tree unless it is built of shared acts. Mopsa's courtship must be a set of things done
  together, on different days, each remembered: *she saw you take the blame*, *you skated with her*, *you lied to her
  and she found out*. It must be able to fail, and it must not be a number.
- **Horror** in one night is a mood, not fear that lasts. Be honest: Carth is an interlude, not *The Winter at
  Scarth*. What it can do is a night where the danger is partly the people one is shut in with, and a morning where
  something must be decided.
- **Philosophy** is the treatment's weakest game. "They argue up the ice road" is a lecture with walking. See §5.

## 5. The Road to Orm, specifically

The seed asked for a two-person philosophical walk to a trial. The treatment has bolted it to a comedy as a side road.
Three problems:

1. **Time.** Two days to Orm and two back takes the protagonist out of the city for the wedding week's central days.
   Either it is the spine (and the comedy is the frame) or it is shortened to one day out and one back.
2. **Argument without stakes.** Thaw's question must matter to a decision the protagonist makes on the road. It does:
   whether to hand him over. But it should matter to the city too. It can: **Thaw is the only witness to every
   ice wedding** (he looks at the hands, not the faces, and remembers ink). If he goes to Orm, nobody can say who
   married Ismay; if he stays, he can. The philosophical question (does a vow bind the self that made it?) and the
   practical one (will you let the only witness go to be silenced?) are the same question.
3. **Positions not remembered.** The walk is only a game if what the protagonist says early is turned on them later:
   "You said at the booth that a promise is a promise. You are about to give me to men who will burn me because of
   one I made at nineteen." That needs positions held as facts and read later.

**Decision:** the walk is one day. Thaw is summoned to Orm on the fourth morning by the bishop's summoner. The
protagonist may walk him (the Church pays, and Dumbrell's ring is at Carth on the way, and Kit is at Carth). Carth is
the night. The second morning is the decision at the Gibbet Reach: on to Orm, back to Hessel, or away. Orm itself is
a gate, not a place; the trial is offstage, and the work never shows it.

## 6. The collection's habits, caught

- **An old woman who holds the key.** Dumbrell's sixty-year-old secret about Carth, the pedlar and the ring is the
  repertoire's device ("Gammer Coller, the Rememberer, Mother Saska, the Prioress… Old Gudrun"). Cut the secret. She
  wants her mother's ring because it was her mother's.
- **A misread past.** Carth's horror as written is a misread past (the turned-away family) found out. Keep the past
  (it is last winter, not an age ago, and it is a household's own guilt, not an inheritance), but put the horror in
  the present: what is in the shieling now; who takes the food now; what the household will do tonight.
- **A ledger as the exhibit.** The treatment avoids one, and says so; keep it so. The parson keeps no register.
- **A hearing.** The Corporation and the bishop were both trying to become one. Neither will sit.
- **The outsider who reads evidence.** The protagonist could still slide into it: "find out who married Ismay". The
  answer must not be the climax. Let the culprit be findable by the end of the second day, and make the rest of the
  week about what is done with it.
- **The custom-coda.** The endings in §8 are already drifting to "every winter since…". Forbid it. The last lines of
  every ending are about the people, the morning after the thaw.

## 7. Do people change their minds for reasons other than evidence?

The treatment wants them to, and does not yet say how. Each mind that changes must change for a named reason:

| Who | Changes how | Moved by |
|---|---|---|
| Ismay | from secrecy to saying aloud what she wants | being forgiven before she confesses (by Kit, or by the protagonist); or by being laughed with, not at, by Mopsa |
| Rob | from obedience to telling his mother | someone standing beside him when he says it |
| Mistress Wenlock | from rivalry to an alliance with Margery Fennel | being made to laugh at herself (the cake; the coach) or being asked for help instead of told |
| Dumbrell | from command to apology | Hepworth refusing, and the protagonist carrying the refusal back without softening it; or her own death coming |
| Hepworth | from grudge to baking | being asked by Dumbrell herself |
| Valentine | from scheme to flight, or to confession | being seen (Bess and Ismay together), or being frightened (the watermen), or being offered the thing he actually wants (to be the one who is loved, which he cannot have) |
| Thaw | from the Flow held as a joke to holding it as a cost | the protagonist's own positions turned on him; Carth |
| Kit | from silence to speaking | hearing she thought it was him she married |
| Mr Pyne | from terror to courage | someone telling him what he will hear before he hears it |

Evidence moves some of these (Bess and Ismay comparing notes), and that is fine. It must not move most of them.

## 8. Are there real alternatives? Does it reward different builds?

The treatment does not consider builds at all. A city comedy risks rewarding charm only. The revised design must
give each principal kind of character a way in:

- **Body.** The ice is physical: skating, hauling sledges, breaking through, pulling out, the ox's fire, a fight with
  Valentine's hired bully at the Staithes, the glutton at Carth.
- **Craft and stealth.** The Valentine booth's letters can be read by someone who gets in after dark; a lock; a mask.
- **Learning and faith.** Thaw argues; Pyne needs the right words; a cleric can bless an ice wedding in a way Thaw
  cannot (or refuse to).
- **The wild.** The ice road, the blizzard, tracks in snow at Carth.
- **Charm.** Mistress Wenlock, Mopsa, the Recorder.

And there must be at least two principal approaches to the central problem (Valentine and the banns) that differ in
kind, not in flavour.

## 9. Too much exposition?

Yes. The treatment explains the ice's law in its first page. In play it must be learned from a toll, a joke, a booth
sign and an argument, in that order. No one should explain "no parish runs on the ice" until the protagonist has
already been charged a penny by a starving waterman to walk on it.

## 10. Are the comic moments earned?

The mistaking is. The curate's dream is, if it pays off at the banns. The coach is, if it goes on the ice. The
privies are not. The ox is not, yet. Mopsa's lines are not written; she must be funnier than the narrator.

## 11. Does a character meaningfully change?

Ismay, Rob, Dumbrell and Thaw can. The protagonist can, if the vow they make or refuse on the ice is theirs. Valentine
should be able to change, or not; the treatment has him only run.

## 12. Doing nothing

The treatment does not say what happens if the protagonist does nothing. It must: Kit leaves for Carth on the second
morning believing Ismay married someone else; Valentine sends his note to the Fennels on the fourth day; Mistress
Wenlock hears it on the fifth; at the third reading Valentine stands up; the ice goes on the seventh night and takes
the fair with it. A protagonist who does nothing should see a comedy curdle in front of them for reasons that are
nobody's fault but Valentine's and their own.
