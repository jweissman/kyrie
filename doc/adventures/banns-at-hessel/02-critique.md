# The Banns at Hessel — the severe editorial review

*Stage 2. An attack on [01-treatment.md](01-treatment.md). The design that answers it is
[03-revised-design.md](03-revised-design.md).*

## 0. What the withdrawal taught

The first concept, a frost fair, was checked for collisions the way the repertoire says the collection checks them:
by **nouns**. The names were clean. The **form** was not: an outstanding branch already had a frozen wool city, an
ice-law, a con and a Flow preacher. The lesson is the repertoire's own (§4, "the collision check works on nouns"), and
it applies to this treatment too. So this review begins with a form ledger against everything built or building:

| Field | Wrayford | Pentrice | Pellam (outstanding) | **Hessel** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protagonist | the dead man's child, late | a player in a troupe | a con's partner | a stranger taken up as a dying woman's legs |
| Wants | a burial | a patent, a season | money, a brother | to be useful, then whatever they choose: love, money, a friend |
| Fault | late home | what they play | the lie | the matches they make |
| Central activity | moving people under a deadline | performing | lying | **arranging other people's lives** |
| Minds change by | grief, shame | the audience | belief | being stood beside, being laughed with, being asked |
| Inheritance | none | a ban | an ice-law | none |
| Mystery | none after day 1 | who wrote the play | the bondhouse | two small ones, solved by people, by day 2 |
| Clock | the third night | three nights | three days and a thaw | a Season's last four days |
| Space | a bridge city | a minster city and a road | a fair on a river | **a hill: new town above, baths between, old town below; a house shut inside it** |
| Ends | a verdict | a patent | the Landing | weddings, or not; a ball; a death, or not |
| Register | grief, anger | joy, satire | farce | **desire, tenderness, social fear, one gothic dread** |

The spa is distinct in its activity (matchmaking), its register (desire, tenderness) and its space (a vertical city
of classes). Its risk is that "a city of manners with a Master who rules a building" sounds like Pentrice's Office.
**Guard:** Mr Twiss must not be an office or a censor. He is one vain, kind, absurd man with a framed list, and his
Rules are a game the player plays, not an institution to be reformed.

## 1. Is the city alive independently of its mystery?

**More than the frost fair was.** A spa has a daily round that is the same for everyone and that a player can learn:
the Pump Room at eight, the baths till ten, the walks at noon, dinner at three, the Rooms at seven, the chairs
everywhere. That round must be **in the clock**, with people where it says they are: a city alive is a city whose
people are somewhere else at noon than at eight.

It needs **business unrelated to the Cherretts**: a chairman's fare disputed; a laundress's hands; a patient at the
Hospital without a doctor; an apothecary's boy who can count; a curate who has never been answered. The treatment has
these as names. Each must have an errand of its own.

## 2. Are the small adventures substantial and varied?

| | Verdict |
|---|---|
| The Runaway Chair | Strong opening: physical, comic, decides the protagonist's standing with Mrs Cherrett and Kit. Needs **real outcomes**: caught, steered, or failed (and then she is hurt, and Kit is blamed). |
| Into the Rooms | Strong satire. But "get Twiss's leave" is a gate unless there are several ways and each costs something. |
| The Pump Room | **Not an adventure: a tool.** Mrs Scrope is the city's gossip engine; she should be usable in many adventures, and her rumours must come back with consequences. |
| The Chairman and the Heiress | The work's heart. Must not be a dialogue-tree romance. |
| Rob's Poems / A Winter in Carth / The Laundress | **One strand, not three.** The poem gets the protagonist to the house; the house raises the knock; the knock leads to the laundress. |
| The Hessel Drops | Strong, and dangerous: it is the collection's "show the evidence" in a bottle. See §4. |
| The Hospital / The Road to Orm | One strand: Ottery. |
| Rupert's Secret / The Third Reading | One strand. The best comic set piece in the treatment. |
| Mopsa's Lease | Good: someone who wants something for herself that is not a person. |
| The Chairmen's Fare | **A sketch.** Fold it into Kit: the chairmen's grievance gives Kit a reason to be on the stand or off it, and gives the Old Town approach its muscle. |
| The Closing Ball | The finale occasion. Must be a game: five dances, partners chosen, rules that can be broken, people who see. |
| The Sham Castle | **A sketch.** Keep it as a place where assignations happen, not an adventure. |

Sixteen becomes about eleven strands with insides, plus tools and places.

## 3. Does the plot grow organically from the setting?

**Yes, which is the treatment's best quality.** A city that sells hope and introductions produces exactly these
plots: a dying rich woman sold both, a peer's son sold to her money, a physician who sells sleep, a man who bought
nine years of pretending his wife is dead. Carth House is the city's thesis turned inside out: everyone else in
Hessel pretends it is the Season; one house pretends it is still the night of the great snow.

The risk is that **Carth House and the comedy do not touch**. They touch through Rob (he loves the window) and through
the chairmen (they carry everyone, and one of them, every night, carries nobody up the hill and waits). That must be
built, not asserted.

## 4. The collection's habits, caught

- **Being shown.** The drops are a bottle that, shown to the right person, ends Hartopp. That is an exhibit. Change the
  shape: **Mrs Cherrett knows the drops are laudanum.** She takes them because her nights are bad. What she does not
  know is that Hartopp has been doubling them since she changed her will. Showing her the bottle does nothing; she
  says "I know what's in it, child." What moves her is a person: Ismay, who has never asked her for anything, asking her
  to stop; or Mopsa, who has nursed her, saying she is afraid; or Ottery, whom she trusts because he told her the truth.
  Hartopp is undone, if at all, socially (the Rooms) or bodily (the Old Town), not by a verdict.
- **The truth always wins.** Ottery's thesis is the collection's ethic. The work must let it lose. Mrs Cherrett, told
  everything, may choose to sleep. Sir Gervase, told his wife lives, may not open the door. Ismay may marry Rupert
  rather than say aloud whom she loves. The protagonist's own position (comfort or truth) must be taken and remembered,
  and must be able to be right.
- **The old woman with the key.** Mrs Cherrett holds no key and knows no secret about the past. She wants things, and
  her wants are wrong. Keep her that way.
- **A misread past.** Lady Carth's survival is a secret found out. Acceptable at this size, because it is a living
  woman's choice in the present (what she wants now), not the meaning of an inheritance.
- **A vote, a hearing.** The College at Orm is a tribunal. **It is never shown.** The walk ends at the College's door.
- **A ledger as exhibit.** The Pump Room book is scenery. Nothing is proved by it.
- **The coda of custom.** Forbidden. Last lines are the morning after, about people.

## 5. Is the romance a game or a passage of dialogue?

As treated, a dialogue. Each courtship must be **acts in places at times**, each a remembered fact a person's words
can read: *carried her letter*, *stood beside him at the church*, *danced the second dance with her*, *lied to her*,
*saw her hit Hartopp with the warming-pan and laughed*. A courtship can fail: Mopsa refuses a protagonist who tries
to rescue her. None of it is a number on the screen.

Six couples is too many to do this well. **Four** carry play: Ismay and Kit; Rob and Celia; Rupert and Hetty (who
need courage, not love); Mopsa and the protagonist (optional). Sir Gervase and Lady Carth are an ending, not a
courtship. Mrs Cherrett's dance is a grace note.

## 6. Is the satire a game?

It is when it is a **tool**:

- **The Rules of the Rooms** can be broken on purpose, in front of Twiss, to disgrace someone (Hartopp dancing three
  dances running with an heiress), or bent by him for someone he decides deserves it (a dying woman's last minuet,
  after eleven).
- **Mrs Scrope** carries whatever she is told. That is a lever on every strand: tell her Ismay has no fortune, and
  Hartopp cools; tell her Rupert has a child, and the banns are a scandal before Sunday; tell her nothing, and she
  invents.
- **Doctors' Walk** can be asked, for a fee, for an opinion; eleven opinions, each the same water.
- **The fare-table** charges by weight: a scene, not a mechanism.

## 7. Is the horror a game?

Carth House needs a **way in** (several), a **night** (the knock at two), a **danger** (Sir Gervase armed and not
sane at that hour; the heat), and a **decision** (open the door, take Celia out, fetch Nan, or leave the winter be).
That is a small adventure with a shape. Fear that lasts it is not; it is a night's dread inside a comedy, which is what
*The Winter's Tale* does with its storm and its bear.

## 8. The Road to Orm

A day's walk out of a four-day story is a real cost, which is good. But if Orm is a day away and the College sits on
the second afternoon, the walk takes the whole second day, when the comedy is setting up. **Decision:** the walk is
optional, and it is the second morning: leave at first light, over the down, three hours; the College sits at noon;
the carrier comes back down by dusk. The protagonist loses the second day in the city and gains Ottery's friendship
and a position taken on the road. Ottery's errand matters to the city: **he is the one physician Mrs Cherrett will
listen to about the drops**, so if he is struck off at Orm, he can still speak to her; if he is never delivered, he
is a fugitive and cannot come up the hill.

The walk must be **positions taken and remembered**: at the stile, the shepherd's hut, the gibbet, Orm's gate. A
question asked at the start is turned at the end.

## 9. Does it reward different builds?

The treatment did not ask. A comedy of manners will reward charm unless it is built not to. Ways in for each kind:

- **Body**: catching the chair; taking a pole as a chairman when Kit is hurt; climbing into Carth House by the area
  railings and the coal-hole; carrying Mrs Cherrett up the hill; a fight with Hartopp's man, or with Sir Gervase at two
  in the morning; footpads on the down.
- **Craft and stealth**: the coal-hole lock; following the knocker at night unseen; switching the drops for Pump
  water; the apothecary's shop after dark.
- **Learning and faith**: judging the drops; arguing with Ottery; steadying Mr Pyne; what may be said at a reading of
  banns.
- **The wild**: the sheep-path over the down, which saves an hour to Orm.
- **Charm and manners**: Twiss, Lady Wenlock, Mrs Scrope, the dances.

And **two principal approaches** to the work's central problems (the banns, Hartopp, Carth House) that differ in kind:

- **The Rooms** (above): introductions, leave, rumours, dances, a scene in public. Social power; reputations are made
  and broken; it costs face and nerve.
- **The Old Town** (below): the chairmen, the laundresses, the apothecary's boy, the back stairs. Bodies and work;
  doors opened from below; it costs sweat, coin and the risk of being caught where a guest should not be.

Each of the three central problems must be solvable both ways, with different outcomes.

## 10. Exposition

The treatment's districts read as a guidebook. In play: learn the Rules by breaking one; learn the drops from a
yawn; learn the knock by hearing it.

## 11. Are the comic moments earned?

The runaway chair is slapstick, and earns its place by consequence. Rupert's secret pays off at the third reading,
which is the work's best scene. Mr Twiss is funny only if he is also sincere. Mopsa must be the funniest person in
the work and must not be a wisecrack machine: she is funny because she is right.

## 12. Does a character meaningfully change?

| Who | From → to | Moved by |
|---|---|---|
| Mrs Cherrett | arranging → letting them choose | Ismay asking her for something, for the first time; being reminded she married a navvy against her own father |
| Ismay | silence → saying it | being stood beside; being told Kit was hurt for her |
| Kit | pride → speaking | being told she watches for his chair; a fare refused by his mates on her account |
| Rupert | cowardice → telling | someone going with him to his mother; Hetty's ultimatum |
| Lady Wenlock | snobbery → a grandmother | the baby put in her arms |
| Sir Gervase | the stopped clock → the door | Celia; the protagonist sitting up with him; Nan's voice |
| Dr Ottery | contempt for hope → mercy | the protagonist's position held against him on the road |
| Mr Twiss | the Rules → one exception | a dying woman's request, put to him rightly |
| The protagonist | meddler → somebody's | Mopsa refusing to be arranged |

## 13. Doing nothing

The treatment does not say. It must:

- Sunday, the third reading: no one speaks; Monday, Rupert marries Ismay; Hetty at the church door with the baby.
- Hartopp's doubled drops: Mrs Cherrett does not wake on the morning after the Ball.
- Kit gives up the poles at the Season's end and goes to the canals.
- Carth House keeps its winter; Nan knocks.
- Ottery, undelivered, is struck off in his absence.
