# The Banns at Hessel: synopsis, acts, characters and conflicts

## Synopsis

A stranger comes over Lim Bridge into Hessel in the last four days of its Season. On the Parade a bath-chair is
running away downhill with a vast old woman in purple in it. However the stranger meets the chair, Mrs Abigail
Cherrett hires them to be her legs: she can no longer get up the hill. She has forty thousand pounds made in canals,
a heart that is giving out, and three young people to settle before it does. Her granddaughter Ismay is to marry the
Honourable Rupert Wenlock on Monday; the banns have been read twice. Her great-nephew Rob is to be engaged to a
sensible girl. Her companion, Mopsa Gedge, is to be married off before she becomes a poor relation. And she has been
barred from the Assembly Rooms for laughing at a minuet, so none of it can be done at the Closing Ball.

Everything she wants is wrong, and the stranger finds out how by carrying her messages. Ismay loves the chairman who
let go of the chair. Rupert has a wife and a baby in Quay Lane. Rob is in love with a girl he has only seen through
a crack in a shutter. Mopsa does not want a husband; she wants a house. And Mrs Cherrett is not just sleeping a great
deal because she is dying. Her physician, who is in her will and means to marry Ismay, has doubled her drops. At the
current dose she will not wake on the morning the Season ends.

Hessel is built on one hill, and the stranger can work at either end of it. The Rooms, at the top, are a world of
Rules, apologies, introductions, gossip and the Master of the Ceremonies. The Old Town, at the bottom, is the
chairmen, the laundresses, the milliner, the apothecary's boy and the Two Poles. Each way can untangle every strand,
and the two leave different towns behind. The book closes at noon on the fifth day, when the coaches go, and the
closing page reports what became of each person the stranger touched.

## Acts

Hessel is a clock, not a corridor; these are the shapes a Season takes, not gates.

**I. Arrival (day 1).** The chair on the Parade; hired as legs; the household in the parlour. Mopsa teaches the form of
an apology; Lanyon says the chairmen will strike; the shuttered house at the end of the Crescent smokes in the warm
afternoon. At dusk a girl with a candle at a crack in a shutter; at two in the morning, a knock.

**II. The Round (day 2).** Ottery's walk to Orm at dawn, for whoever promised to go. Noon on the Beacon and behind the
Sham Castle. Hetty's ultimatum to Rupert by evening. Brocca drunk at the Two Poles. Mrs Scrope makes public whatever
she was told that morning. Each doubled night of drops greys Mrs Cherrett further.

**III. Sunday (day 3).** The chairmen lay their poles crossways, unless the fares were restored. At eleven Mr Pyne
reads the banns for the third time and pauses, as he always does, and the church waits for someone to speak.

**IV. The Closing Ball (day 4).** The wedding at ten, if the banns stood; Hetty is on the steps. From seven, the Ball:
Twiss's minuet; who is admitted and who is turned back; Hartopp's dances with Ismay against the Rule; Kit on the lawn;
Rob with Celia, if she is out; eleven o'clock; and the last minuet, if Twiss will bend. At two, the knock again.

**V. The Morning After (day 5, until noon).** Mrs Cherrett wakes or does not. Her thanks. The coaches go at noon, and
the closing page reads the town strand by strand. The valley stays open afterwards: the company can walk on, or back
over Lim Bridge.

## Conflicts

- **Mrs Cherrett against her own plans.** Every match she has made is the wrong one, and she made each of them out
  of love and fear. The kindest resolution makes her change her mind, not fail.
- **The Rooms against the Old Town.** The Rules are kindnesses made into law (Twiss says so, and the game agrees),
  and the Old Town carries those laws uphill on its shoulders for ninepence. The fares dispute, the boots, the
  companion who may not dance and the chairman who may not come in are all one conflict.
- **Public against private.** Mrs Scrope can end any secret by three in the afternoon. The private way, through
  drawing rooms, notes and doors opened by the right hand, is slower and kinder. It leaves the Rooms not knowing.
- **Truth against comfort.** Ottery's questions on the Orm road are the moral spine of the walk, and they decide one
  practical thing: whether he stops the drops or halves them.
- **Sir Gervase against the morning after.** Carth House is a man who has stopped every clock at the hour of his worst
  act. The house is the one place in Hessel where the comedy stops, and the one place the company can be thrown out
  by force.
- **Hartopp against everyone.** The only villain, and a small one: a pleasant man who doubled a dose. He is
  defeated by procedure (his boots, his third dance), by the chairmen (he walks), or by Ismay's asking. Never by a
  sword.
