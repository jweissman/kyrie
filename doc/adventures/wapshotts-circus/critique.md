# Wapshott's Peregrine Circus — the second-pass critique

*Read against the Phase I build (commit fa63775) as if someone else had written it. What I am not happy with, and
what the revision does about it. The revision is described in [README.md](README.md); the frictions met on the way
are in [friction.md](friction.md).*

## 1. Is there a story, or a list?

**A list, mostly.** Phase I is a good inventory (fifteen people, six beasts, four lessons, a Fair) laid side by side.
Nothing has acts. The note arrives on the third day out of a clear sky, the Fair is announced in a ledger, and the
reckoning is a threshold sum read at ten. There is no point at which the player feels the floor tilt. A circus story
has a natural dramatic shape that the build ignored: the wagons come in (wonder), the money runs out behind the
canvas (the backstage truth), the rival's shadow comes over the green (pressure), the canvas is cut in the night (the
worst hour), and the last show is given anyway (the get-up).

*Revision:* four chapters with headings the page shows (**The Wagons**, **The Note**, **The Goose Fair**, **On the
Road Again**), each opening on something seen, not announced. The note is foreshadowed for two days before it is
served (a grey hat at the back of the house, writing; Nance's warning; a portrait turned to the wall in the
Colonel's wagon). The Fair has a worst hour: Slee's hired men come in the dark after the first night to cut the
guy-ropes.

## 2. What is it about?

I did not know when I wrote it, and it shows: the comedy is a series of turns. Reading it back, three themes are
already there, half-buried, and the revision digs them out and makes the side stories rhyme with them:

- **The roof and the road.** Everyone in the circus wants a roof or ran from one: the Colonel's amphitheatre in
  stone, Honoria's tea-shop, Lusk's Nell who wants the wagons and her gran who says *a circus is a thing that leaves*,
  Susan Pruett who left a tripe-lane for the wire, Ninepence who must leave a mill after four hundred years,
  Methuselah who has been going round the country for three centuries with one travelling household or another. The
  story's answer is not given; each thread answers it differently. The revision has people notice the rhyme:
  Mumchance hears that Mrs Pruett sits at the back and says his father sat at the back every year and never once came
  round; Ninepence and the tortoise are both, in their way, older than any house.
- **Humbug and the true thing.** The bills, the basilisk "theory", the Sylph's East, the Colonel's commission, Slee's
  "I make things easy", Sallow's "I have never cheated". The priest of the fair town says the hinge sentence: *I don't
  mind it being impossible. I mind it being false.* The revision puts that sentence in the jury's verdict, makes an
  honest bill and the Sylph's real name count for something at the Fair, and gives the Colonel one line in the
  epilogue that is not humbug.
- **The fall and the get-up.** Mumchance's whole art: *a fall happens to you; going down is something you do.* The
  Colonel falls over the ring boards every night and has never once meant it. In Phase I he falls on purpose at the
  reckoning, but nothing had prepared it. The revision has Mumchance say early, plainly, that the day the Colonel means
  that fall he will be a clown; and the paid ending pays it off. Ada's arc is the opposite movement: she has never
  once been allowed to fall (someone must keep the books), and Sallow, who recognises her, offers her a roof.

## 3. Characters: arcs or attributes?

Most of the cast have a want, a grievance and a secret, which is good, and a single scene each, which is not. Only
the Quinceys and the Sylph change. The revision gives arcs to:

- **Colonel Wapshott**: from avoidance to facing it. A relic-wagon that shows his past (the first bill from a field,
  the dead elephant's ankle-chain, a portrait turned to the wall), a scene where he is found counting the box alone at
  night, and the deliberate fall.
- **Mumchance**: the silence. Jos Haverhill has tried nine years to make him speak. At the Flyting, when it matters
  and only if the company has learned the Answer, he says one word aloud in public, the first in forty years. One word
  is the right size.
- **Ada**: Sallow's offer at the Fair (a quarter-share in the Hippodrome, at once, the thing she wanted from
  Wapshott's). The company can ask what she means to do. She stays or goes by the reckoning: if the note is torn she
  stays; if united she keeps the strongbox key for Sallow; if paid she stays and gets her quarter-share from a
  chastened Colonel. (She never leaves the circus in Phase I; the offer is a tension, not a twist.)
- **Jos**: a rival who becomes an ally at the Fair, heckling Bombazine from the crowd for Wapshott's if he was beaten
  fairly, against it if he was not.

## 4. Tone

The register is consistent (dry, warm, specific) to the point of monotony. Tics that a second reading finds: *for a
long time* (fifteen), *very slowly*, *enormous*, *magnificently*, *purring like a millstone* (twice), *like a cow in
love* (twice), and a recurring construction where a small gesture "in him is a speech / a standing ovation / very
nearly a word" (five times). Each is fine once. Repeated, they are a voice listening to itself. The revision prunes
them and lets other registers in: the night the ropes are cut is frightening; the glimmer-fox is not funny at all;
the Sylph's mother is told plainly; Slee is pleasant and therefore chilling; the hob speaks in an older idiom.

**Pulp exuberance.** The capitals are the Colonel's and belong to him; they are kept for him and removed elsewhere.
The worst offender is the show itself: every night is told in the same words, so the third visit reads like a
gramophone. The revision tells each act in full the first time it is seen and briefly after, and gives every night
one *incident* drawn from a small table (a dog in the ring, a drunk joining the Quinceys, the lamps guttering and
Pip holding a lantern up for the wire, rain finding the one hole over the Colonel at the word LADIES, a child asleep
through all of it), so a fourth night is a different night.

## 5. Danger

One dangerous creature (the owlbear), and nothing that threatens the circus physically. OSR play wants a real knife
in the dark with real ways round it. The revision adds the **tober-raiders**: three men from Dunmere hired by Slee
to come at midnight on the Fair's first night and cut the big top's guy-ropes. They are dangerous (cudgels, numbers,
level enough to kill a first-level company), and they can be fought; or the company can raise the old cry of the
circus, **Hey Rube!**, and the whole backyard turns out with tent-stakes behind it; or pay them; or talk them off by
telling them who Honoria is; or lead them a chase through the guy-ropes in the dark (tumbling, or the Feint). Left
alone, the canvas comes down in the night, and the contest is played in a patched tent.

## 6. The illusion of depth

The world beyond the three towns was a handful of names (Dunmere, Marlow, Wakeham, Oster). The revision gives them
texture without building them: the Waggoner at Dunmere's loft where players sleep; the flood year at Marlow when the
elephant Dido died and is buried under the market cross; the Salle Fell; Old Pantaloon; the Household of the
Progress, which Hench describes before the tortoise ever appears; the bone in the third town's shrine, mentioned by
its priest on the first visit, long before Pip names it at the end. And the circus speaks its own cant, a little:
the *tober*, the *flatties*, a *joey*, the *half*, *Hey Rube*.

## 7. Is it playable and compelling as written?

Playable, yes: the journeys pass and the circuit runs. Compelling, partly. The weakest stretch is the middle: after
the first night there is plenty to do and no pressure until the Fair. The revision puts pressure in the middle: the
note is served at the end of Act I; Sallow's bills appear in the next town before the wagons do; the larder and
wages pinch in the second town; and the Fair is visibly coming (the geese are being driven along the roads from the
eighth day, past wherever the company is).

## 8. If the ambition were ten times this

- **The circus as a living institution**, not a module: its acts procedurally assembled from a troupe whose members
  join, quarrel, age, leave and return across seasons; a ring programme generated each night from who is fit and who
  is speaking to whom.
- **Several circuits**, across every country of the world, with rival companies on their own roads; the company
  meeting Gosse's players at a crossroads; a Circus Parliament at Dunmere where the companies trade acts and grudges.
- **The menagerie as an ecology**: beasts with needs, temperaments, breeding and escape; intelligent creatures with
  contracts and unions; the Royal Progress's old inventory as a quest map across countries.
- **The Gentle Art as a full calling**, to the Mirror, with schools and rival schools (the Bombazine school of loud
  clowns), and a duel culture of Flytings in every fair of the world.
- **Seasons**: winter quarters, spring rehearsal, a summer circuit, the autumn Fair; a calendar the whole world shares.
- **The Household of the Progress**: a court that travels like a circus, with the circus as its shadow.

## 9. What I am still not happy with after the revision

- The verdict at the Fair is still a sum against a threshold, however well dressed; a jury that argues aloud would be
  better (Pettigrew, the innkeeper and the priest each giving a reason drawn from what they saw).
- The economy is real but mostly invisible except through Ada. A bill of the week's figures on the ticket wagon would
  make it legible.
- Every first country gets the same circus, with the same names. A second company, or its people drawn by Deem,
  would make it a world rather than a set piece.
- Nothing has been played by anyone but its author.
