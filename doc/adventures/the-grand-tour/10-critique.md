# The Grand Tour: a critique

*(Written after the first build. `12-second-pass.md` reads it again, harder, and records what was then done about
the antagonist offstage, the one voice, the hidden romance and the mystery without a wrong answer.)*

*An honest reading of what was built, as literature and as play, by its author, from the ten transcripts in
`playtest/` and the journeys behind them. The author is the worst-placed reader there is; what follows tries to read
against that.*

## What it is

A comedy of manners in a vertical city, with a murder in the middle that turns out not to be one, and a heist at the
end that can go five ways. Its register is the collection's least usual: sunlit, quick, epigrammatic, closer to Aiken
and Wodehouse than to the winter and grief the other adventures keep. It answers the commission's three seeds as one
place: *The Grand Tour* is the contest and the House; *Whelmed beneath Waves* is the Whelm, the merfolk and the
Aenean engine, and Mercy and Perrin are its romance; *Cloudgate* is the hill in the sky and the fall from its rail.

## What works

**The city is one joke, told at three heights.** The brass plate on the third step, BY ORDER: NO PERSON OF THE SEA
ABOVE THIS STEP; the servants' hall in Cloudgate that has no windows; the winch-gang's pay docked for the family's
mourning; the Halyards who "say that it is very pretty, from up here" of the candles set on the water for the people
their great-great-grandmother drowned. Nobody but the Rector is wicked. The arrangement does the rest, and every
small story is a place where somebody has stopped noticing it: Pumphrey, who sat in his grandmother's lap on the top
step; Jem, who has not been down in eleven years; the Dowager, who has never gone down to the Quay on that night.

**Each court is played by its own rule.** The Hour Court crowns novelty, so a hand-mirror (the court seeing itself
still), a geode (a million years old, therefore unheard of), or a jar of cold wind wins the Fire. The Slow Assembly
decides by procedure, so you either sit for two days or find a pebble who says "it's only a motion". The Whelm keeps,
and gives only for the asking. The Air trades in talk. These are the best things in the work: satires of four ways a
society can be stupid, each of which is also a game a player can solve without a skill.

**The witnesses are people first.** The editorial's hardest finding (E1: three exhibits wearing coats) held in the
build. Kittiwake remembers the eleven o'clock basket for someone who has walked her capstan; Jem tells what he saw at
the rail on the way down to a life he can bear; Tolley tells about the four drafts once he has his own sentences
back. An acolyte with no silver tongue solves the case in a day and a half by doing three kindnesses.

**Two scenes earn the whole.** Mercy in a water-butt at the rim of the Promenade watching the lanterns go up alive,
saying "They go up," and then telling the Dowager, in front of all Cloudgate, "I caught your son"; the ring on her
thumb; Lady Halyard on her knees on the gravel in black lace. And No Admittance, told what her name means in Sennet,
laughing until the fish leave by the windows. Both are the work's argument in a gesture.

**The endings differ in texture, not only in outcome.** A Present from Sennet is a joke that saves a city (Prue
sends the Halyards her account, and they pay it). The Keepsake and the Letter are the same snow on Sennet in June,
reached by an apology and by a theft, and they end differently: one with the Dowager on the Quay with a candle she
does not know how to light, the other with an empty box and a green ribbon in her lap. First Through tells an
accomplice what they have become in its last line. Found Out is the dryest and the most decent.

## What does not

**The mystery is a checklist.** Any two of three witnesses and the inference rules assert `quance_pushed` for you.
The player gathers; the module deduces. There is no moment where the player must put two things together and be
right or wrong. The accusation on the Promenade comes closest (it fails, and Cloudgate laughs, without two witnesses
or Perrin), but it is a gate, not a judgement. A detective work wants a wrong answer that can be given.

**It can be solved too fast.** A knowing acolyte has the Rector in a cell by ten on the second morning, before the
funeral; the Rector's eulogy, her best scene, never happens (the House sends its chaplain, who says he did not know the
boy: a good line, and a smaller scene); the Night of Lanterns has no villain. The
editorial asked that minds change for reasons other than evidence (E8); the Watch's mind changes only for evidence,
and at once. The work is better at a first-time player's pace than a knowing one's, which is true of every mystery and
is still a fault here, because Kyrie's players will replay.

**The antagonist acts offstage.** Left alone (`alone.md`), the week is a series of chapter lines and then a hill
falling. The rivals' crowning, their dig, the divers' raid, the Rector going down the keel ladder: none of it is
seen unless the company stands where it happens. The plot's pressure is real (it decides the ending) and invisible.
A crier, a broadsheet, or the gossip of the Quay telling each turn of the race as it happens would cost a dozen rules
and would make the week a week.

**The Rector's reason is thin on the page.** She pushed a boy she loved because he was going to give the fourth
petal back to the wind and so put the Door beyond her for ever. That is coherent, and the player learns it only by
inference; she never says it, except obliquely at the keel ("Oh, I am sorry it's you"). Her best quality, that she
keeps her word to an accomplice out of vanity, is seen only by the worst player.

**One voice.** The merfolk, the ifrit, the sylphs, the North Wind, a Fish Street footman and a Cloudgate dowager all
speak the narrator's clipped, wry English. It is a good voice, and it flattens the difference between storeys that
the satire depends on. The Whelm should not sound like Cloudgate.

**The Undertow skips the heart of the work.** It is the right second approach mechanically (short, physical, a
moral price) and the worst experientially: a warrior can open the Door of Doors by nightfall on the first day without
ever going up the chain, meeting the Dowager, or learning what happened at the rail. The ending says so ("the Rose was
never made"); it does not say what the player missed, and it cannot.

**Walking.** The House of Doors is four rooms up the Headland Steps from Lamp Street, and the Tour route walks
Quad, gate, Lamp Street, Glass Market a dozen times, each with its paragraph. Cloudgate is a basket ride and four rooms
more. The city's size is right for a city and wrong for a page that tells every room.

**The romance with Fabian is easy to miss.** His fondness is counted and never shown; the dance needs three, the
declaration five, and the Night of Lanterns; the principal Tour route reaches none of it. He is the best-written
companion in the collection and a player can finish the Tour without knowing he could be loved.

**The highest reward is mostly unused.** The Doorward's door is a world-changing power, and in the campaign the book
closes at nine the next morning. Only placed in a world does it matter, and there it learns the world only from Sennet
on (friction 13).

## As play

The work is generous: no wrong turn kills you (the Whelm lifts you out, the Fire carries you out), and nearly every
gate has a way for anyone. That generosity costs tension; the stakes are other people's lives, which is the right
place for them and only works if the player cares. The cares that land are Ha'penny, Mercy and Jem; the ones that
land least are the Halyards, who are seen mostly in their drawing room and their son's empty coffin.

The clocks are mostly fair and mostly hidden: the strike, the funeral and the Lanterns are announced; the divers
and the rivals' dig are learned only by asking the right person at the right time. A player who does not ask
Pargeter how trade is will not know the Whelm is to be raided.

## What would make it better, in order

1. Tell the race: the crier's board changes each morning (who has which petal), the Quay talks.
2. Give the mystery a wrong answer: accusing Aubrey, or Florian, to Bassett should be possible, believed, and ruinous.
3. Hold the Rector's exposure until she has given the eulogy, or let her send it from the cell, to be read.
4. Give the Whelm its own speech (shorter sentences, no subordinate clauses, the merfolk's literalness).
5. Show Fabian's fondness in what he writes, so the player sees it rise.
6. Let the Doorward's door outlive the closing morning in the campaign.
