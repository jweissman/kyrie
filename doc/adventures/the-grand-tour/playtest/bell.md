# The Bell

Played by Tamsin, a bard; seed `sennet`; 15 steps given, 189 taken; ended at watch 1014, at the ending "The Second Whelming". Written by `bun scripts/the-grand-tour-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The coast road comes over the last of the downs, and there below you, all at once, is the sea, and a city going down to it in glass. Above the city, over the bay, the cloud lies on the water like a fleece; and standing up out of the cloud, in sunlight, is a hill. It has houses on it, and gardens hanging over its edges, and a chain coming down out of the bottom of it to the city like a plumb-line, as if somebody had lifted the hill up out of the bay by it and forgotten to put it back. Under the bay, where the hill came out, there is nothing to see at all. A milestone at the roadside says SENNET 1, and under it someone has scratched: AND THEN UP, OR DOWN.

**>sennet__landward_gate**

You set out from the top of the coast road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. The bells of the House of Doors ring eight across the bay, and the gulls go up off the Quay all at once, and over all of it, over the cloud, a hill of houses catches the sun. The Landward Gate is a gate in no wall: the wall came down a century ago and the gate was kept because the city liked it. Beyond it Sennet goes down to the water in roofs of green copper and glass, glass everywhere, glass-house chimneys smoking, and the bay beyond, very blue. Under everything, so low you feel it in your feet before you hear it, the city hums: that is the chain.

**>sennet__glass_market**

The Glass Market: a square of stalls under awnings, and everything on them glass. Glass floats for nets, glass eyes for dolls, glass bottles with the light of the Fire Door still moving in them, sold as night-lights. A crier walks the square with a board on a pole. The crier’s board says, in red: THE GRAND TOUR IS PROCLAIMED. Underneath, smaller, in black: PERRIN HALYARD, DOORWARD, FELL 12 DAYS SINCE. LET DOWN ON THE 3RD DAY AT NOON. Someone has chalked beneath both of them: MIND YOUR HEADS.

**>sennet__quay**

The Quay: granite, wet, smelling of weed and tar and frying. Boats tied two deep. Eel barrows, lobster pots, a gull standing on a bollard with the air of having been appointed to it. Looking up from here, Cloudgate is directly overhead, and its underside is rock, brown and hanging, like the bottom of a turnip pulled out of the ground. No ferry runs between the Quay and Halyard Hill while the hill is in the sky. Old Bethan’s eyes are red at the rims, and twice while you watch she starts to nod over the eels and jerks awake.

**Ask Old Bethan about the bell under the water**

‘The bell.’ She kills an eel, neatly, with the back of a knife. ‘They’ll tell you it’s the drowned. Lir’s church went down with the lower town the night the hill went up, and the sexton with it, and he’s been at his rope two hundred years.’ Another eel. ‘My Ned went out after mackerel in the January blow and the sea kept him. Never come ashore. Not a button.’ She wipes the knife. ‘And twice a day, at the turn, that bell. And every time I think it’s for him. And it isn’t. It’s for nobody. And I’ve to go on thinking it, twice a day, morning and night, and I haven’t slept since Candlemas.’ She looks at you. ‘Eel pie?’

**>sennet__fish_stairs**

The Fish Stairs go down off the end of the Quay into the bay: green granite, worn hollow in the middle, going down and down under the water until the light gives up on them. A brass plate is let into the third step from the bottom of the dry ones: BY ORDER: NO PERSON OF THE SEA ABOVE THIS STEP. MCCXI. On the third step from the bottom, exactly on the brass plate, as if to make a point, a merfolk child sits with a basket of cockles and her tail in the water. The plate is brass, green at the screws; its letters have been polished smooth by eighty years of people sitting on them. Ha’penny, a merfolk child with a basket of cockles, is here.

**Talk to Ha'penny**

A merfolk child is sitting on the third step from the bottom of the dry ones, with her tail in the sea and a basket of cockles in her lap: green-skinned, freckled with silver, with gills under her ears like the gills of a fish, which open and shut when she is interested, which is always. ‘Cockles,’ she says. ‘Ha’penny a scoop. That’s my name too. Ha’penny. What’s yours?’

**Ask Ha'penny about the bell in the drowned church**

‘The bell!’ Ha’penny’s whole face lights up, gills and all. ‘We ring it. Me and Shutters and Cash Only and all of us. You swim up the tower and you get hold of the rope and you hang off it, and it goes BONG and you go up with it, and it goes BONG again and you come down.’ She demonstrates, with her whole body, on the step. ‘At the turn, when the water swings. It’s the best noise in the Whelm.’ She stops. ‘Can you hear it up here?’

**Ask Ha'penny why the children ring the bell at the turn, and never between**

She frowns, as if you had asked why the sea is wet. ‘Because you do. At the turn.’ She thinks. ‘Gran says if you don’t, the Sexton comes down.’ She says it in a voice that does not believe in the Sexton, and then she looks down the stairs into the green and her gills shut. ‘Shutters’s grandad has one arm,’ she says. ‘He says it was a shark.’

**Tell Ha'penny about Old Bethan, who cannot sleep for the bell**

Ha’penny goes very still on the step, and then she puts her cockles down. ‘We didn’t know,’ she says. ‘Nobody told us anybody heard.’ Her gills open and shut. ‘Is she the little one with the eels? She gives me the heads.’ She looks at the water, as if the bell were looking back. ‘We could stop.’ A pause. ‘Or we could ring it for him. Properly. Once. So he’d know.’ She looks up at you. ‘Which would she want?’

**Ask Ha'penny to stop ringing the bell**

‘All right.’ She says it sadly, the way a child gives up a stick. ‘I’ll tell the others. Cash Only will cry. He always cries.’ She slides off the step into the water up to her chin. ‘Tell her sorry. From the bell.’

**>sennet__taproom**

The Sounding Line is low, and dark, and full of fishermen. A sounding-lead the size of a child’s head hangs over the bar on its line, and every so often somebody pats it for luck without looking. The window looks straight out across the bay at nothing but water and, above the water, the underside of Cloudgate. Through the window, out of the bay, a bell rings under the water. The fishermen at the bar stop talking until it has done. The Courant on the bar. THRESHOLDS, Chapter the Thirtieth: Second. ‘I went through my first door at fourteen, as a scullion, with a bucket of the Hall’s ashes, because the Doorward’s boy was sick. I have been second through every door since. The second Doorward of my year, after a Mottram. The second woman to be Rector, after a Halyard. I shall be second through the Door of Doors, after whoever wins the Tour, which is only right: it will be the first door anyone ever asked me to go through.’ Old Bethan’s eyes are red at the rims, and twice while you watch she starts to nod over the eels and jerks awake. Here are Peg Arkle, the landlady of the Sounding Line; and Mr. Josiah Pumphrey, the Harbourmaster of Sennet, with a brass telescope and the Bylaws under his arm.

**Tell Old Bethan why the merfolk ring the bell at the turn of the tide**

She listens with the knife in her hand and an eel in the other, and does not kill it. ‘A thing in the tower,’ she says. ‘And their bairns ringing it down, twice a day, so it won’t come out and take the rest of them.’ She puts the eel back in the barrow, alive, which you have not seen her do. ‘Then it was never ringing for Ned.’ She is quiet. ‘It’s ringing so nobody else’s goes.’ She covers the barrow with the wet sack. ‘He’d want it rung. He threw them his crusts.’ And at the next turn of the tide, they tell you on the Quay, Old Bethan slept through it. Tamsin gains 50 experience: gave Old Bethan a night’s sleep. Tamsin gains 75 experience: let Old Bethan sleep, and the bell ring.

Of the Rector: she went through the Door of Doors with the four petals of the Rose set in it, alone, and has not come back. Mr. Tench has written in his ledger: MAGISTRA I. QUANCE. OUT. Of the Halyards: the Dowager was carried off the hill on a door by four of her own footmen, upright, saying nothing; Aubrey Halyard was found in the wine cellar of Halyard House, up to his knees in the sea, holding a bottle, laughing. Of Thursday, the House’s cat: Mr. Tench saw him go through the cat-flap of the tower door yesterday, as he always does, and come out an hour later with sand in his fur from a beach nobody in Sennet has ever seen. The first through the Door of Doors in two hundred years, Mr. Tench says, may not have been a person. Of the Air petal: it is in the Rose, in the boss of the Door of Doors, with the other three, and the North Wind has not been told.

**Wait › An hour**

In the morning Halyard Hill is standing in the bay where it stood two hundred years ago, wet to its first floors, its gardens hanging off it in ropes of weed, a hundred yards off the end of the Fish Stairs; and under it is the Whelm. Chandlers’ Row, and Lir’s church with its bell, and the Guildhall with the arms of the Glovers’ Company, and the merfolk who lived in them, who had never in their lives been told that anything was coming. Some got through the Water Door. Not many. All morning things come up on the tide at the Fish Stairs: a teapot, a drowned armchair, a basket of cockles. The harbour stands on the Quay and watches the water and says nothing, not even the thing it says when the sea is up. Cloudgate’s people come across on the fishermen’s boats, in their pale clothes, wet, and stand on the Quay among them, and for once nobody in Sennet can tell which is which.
