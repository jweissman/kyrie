# The Wrong Answer

Played by Tamsin, a bard; seed `sennet`; 100 steps given, 296 taken; ended at watch 1014, at the ending "The Tour Unfinished". Written by `bun scripts/the-grand-tour-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The coast road comes over the last of the downs, and there below you, all at once, is the sea, and a city going down to it in glass. Above the city, over the bay, the cloud lies on the water like a fleece; and standing up out of the cloud, in sunlight, is a hill. It has houses on it, and gardens hanging over its edges, and a chain coming down out of the bottom of it to the city like a plumb-line, as if somebody had lifted the hill up out of the bay by it and forgotten to put it back. Under the bay, where the hill came out, there is nothing to see at all. A milestone at the roadside says SENNET 1, and under it someone has scratched: AND THEN UP, OR DOWN.

**>sennet__landward_gate**

You set out from the top of the coast road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. The bells of the House of Doors ring eight across the bay, and the gulls go up off the Quay all at once, and over all of it, over the cloud, a hill of houses catches the sun. The Landward Gate is a gate in no wall: the wall came down a century ago and the gate was kept because the city liked it. Beyond it Sennet goes down to the water in roofs of green copper and glass, glass everywhere, glass-house chimneys smoking, and the bay beyond, very blue. Under everything, so low you feel it in your feet before you hear it, the city hums: that is the chain.

The crier’s board says, in red: THE GRAND TOUR IS PROCLAIMED. Underneath, smaller, in black: PERRIN HALYARD, DOORWARD, FELL 12 DAYS SINCE. LET DOWN ON THE 3RD DAY AT NOON. Someone has chalked beneath both of them: MIND YOUR HEADS.

**Wait › A while**

Whenever the Rector moves, the keys on her chain of office chime, a small bright sound, like a purse shaken. Time passes.

**Talk to Magistra Isadora Quance**

A tall woman of sixty in a gown sewn in four colours gets up from behind a desk the size of a boat, scattering books, and comes round it with both hands out. On her chain of office a ring of old keys clinks. ‘A Tourist! A real one, from over the hill. Do sit. Throw those on the floor, they’ve been there for years.’ She looks at you with enormous interest, as if you were the only interesting thing to have happened to her all week. ‘Isadora Quance. I run this place, or it runs me. You’ve come about the Grand Tour. Everybody has. Most of them are idiots. Are you?’

**Ask the Rector about the Rose of the Winds**

‘The key of the Door of Doors.’ She picks up a book, looks at it, and drops it. ‘Broken in four and given to the four courts of the planes, the Fire, the Earth, the Water and the Air, so that nobody could ever open the Door alone. Very sensible. Very dull.’ She lights a cigarillo off a candle. ‘The Door opens on anywhere, you know. Anywhere at all. You think of a place and there it is, on the other side. Nobody has been through it in two hundred years.’ She blows smoke at the ceiling. ‘The courts still have their petals. They have to be asked for them, each in its own fashion, which is the Tour. Bring me all four and you’ll be first through. That’s the prize.’ She smiles. ‘And I’ll be second. That’s mine.’

**Ask the Rector about Perrin Halyard**

For a moment the Rector does not say anything. She puts down the cigarillo. ‘My best,’ she says. ‘The best student I have had in thirty years. He could read Aenean like a menu, and he was kind, which they never are at that age.’ She looks out of the window at Cloudgate. ‘He’d been drinking. He and his brother. They always fought. He went to the Balustrade to cool off, and the fog came up.’ She picks the cigarillo up again; her hand is perfectly steady. ‘I gave the eulogy at the lying-in-state. I’ll give it again on the third day, when they let him down. I would give the whole Tour to have him back.’ She smiles at you, a little crookedly. ‘Don’t tell anyone I said that. It’s very bad for discipline.’

**Ask the Rector to enter you for the Grand Tour**

‘Of course.’ She is already writing on a card in violet ink. ‘The fee’s ten coin, I’m afraid. The Bursar insists. It keeps out the riff-raff and lets in the rich, which is the opposite of what one wants, but there.’ She looks up at you over the pen. ‘Unless.’ She taps her teeth with it. ‘There is a thing you could do for me, instead. If you go down into the Whelm, for the Water petal. Perrin’s things went into the bay with him. His notebook. It was the House’s, and it was the last thing he wrote, and I’d like it back.’ Her voice is quite steady. ‘Find me that, and you’re entered for nothing. I’ll lend you a helm.’

**Say you will look in the Whelm for Perrin Halyard's notebook**

‘Bless you.’ She says it simply, and for a moment she looks her age. Then she is writing on the card again, and ringing a bell, and a timid young man comes in with a glass helm in his arms like a baby. ‘Tolley, the House’s helm for our Tourist. Mind the bladder, it squeaks.’ To you: ‘His notebook is red, with the House’s key on it. If you find it, bring it straight to me. Nobody else.’ She signs the card. ‘Not his mother, poor woman. It would only distress her.’ You take a Tourist’s licence. You take the House’s brine-glass helm. Tamsin gains 25 experience: entered for the Grand Tour.

**>sennet__murrey_shop**

Murrey’s: a narrow shop with a gilt key over the door, every wall shelved to the ceiling with the same red book, MURREY’S HANDBOOK FOR TRAVELLERS THROUGH THE DOORS, and over the counter a portrait of a formidable old woman in a fire-cloak, with one foot on a stone that is plainly from somewhere else. **Talk to Fabian Murrey**

A long thin man unfolds himself from behind a counter stacked with the same red book, knocks a pile of them over, catches two, and says, ‘Murrey’s. Handbook for Travellers through the Doors. Sixth edition. Three coin.’ He looks at the pile on the floor. ‘It’s a bit out of date. Forty years. But the planes don’t change much, do they?’ A pause. ‘I wouldn’t know.’

**Ask Fabian Murrey about the Handbook**

‘My grandmother’s.’ He glances up at the portrait. ‘Dame Philippa Murrey. She did the Tour nine times, when it was the fashion, before the fashion went to the spas. She wrote the sixth edition in a single winter, after her ninth, and it’s never been out of print.’ He touches a copy. ‘I’m doing the seventh. I’ve done four hundred pages.’ He clears his throat. ‘Out of the sixth. Correcting the commas. I’ve not been through a door myself, as such.’

**Ask Fabian Murrey why he has never been through a door**

He takes off his spectacles and cleans them on his tie, which makes them worse. ‘When I was twelve she took me to the Fire Door, to start me. It was open. I could see the Hour Court through it, all red. And the heat came out of it like a hand.’ He puts them back on. ‘I fainted. Into a Doorward. She picked me up off the floor by my collar and said, in front of the whole Hall, Fabian, you have the soul of an index.’ He smiles, very slightly. ‘She wasn’t wrong. I’m a very good index. Guidebook men don’t go. They write down where other people went.’

**Ask Fabian Murrey to come with you on the Tour**

He drops the book he is holding, and picks it up, and sees that you mean it. ‘Me? I’d faint. I’d faint and you’d have to carry me, and I’m all elbows.’ He picks up a book and puts it down. ‘Have you been through? Any of them?’ He looks at your face. ‘No. Then you don’t know either.’ He shakes his head. ‘Go through one. Come back and tell me what it was really like, and not what she says it’s like.’ He taps the red book. ‘Then ask me again.’

**>sennet__hall_of_doors**

Thursday comes to wind once round your ankles, and goes back to his place. The Hall of Doors: long, dim, cold, with a floor of black and white stone worn into dips, and at the far end two doors side by side in the wall. **Talk to Mr. Tench**

The little man at the desk looks up over half-spectacles. ‘Tench. Doorwarden. Name, licence, and are you sure.’ He dips his pen. ‘Everybody’s sure. Then they come back through the Fire one with their eyebrows off.’

**Ask the Doorwarden for a fire-cloak out of the House's press**

He gets down off his stool, and unlocks a press, and gives you a grey cloak that stands up by itself. ‘The House’s. Bring it back. If you come back without it, I’ll know what happened.’ He climbs back on the stool. ‘Cooked.’ You take the House’s fire-cloak.

**>sennet__hour_court**

The Hour Court is a great hall without walls, held up by pillars of fire that are never the same pillars twice. The court of the ifrit moves through it, tall and burning, in clothes of smoke, talking all at once and never about the same thing for more than a moment. In the middle, on a dais of embers, a brazier of white fire, and in the white fire, lying on it as if on a cushion, a petal of red glass. Beside the brazier, a throne, and on the throne, this hour’s Monarch. This hour’s Monarch is a song: a tune of three notes that somebody made up a little while ago, sitting on the throne as a wavering in the air. The court bows to it.

**>sennet__ash_market**

The Ash Market: stalls of flame selling flame. Everything on them is brand-new and burning, and will be ash within the hour, and the ifrit buying and selling it do not seem to mind; they mind only if it is not new. At the back of the market, where the stalls were an hour ago, lie drifts of grey ash, and a breeze is taking them. A hawker of the Ash Market, an ifrit selling flames off a tray of flame, is here.

**Talk to a hawker of the Ash Market**

An ifrit with a tray of fire hung round their neck turns to you and looks you over, flickering. ‘New!’ they say. ‘You’re new. I’ve never seen you.’ They look again, and their face falls in on itself in smoke. ‘Oh. I have now.’

**Ask the hawker how a Monarch is made in the Hour Court**

‘Newness!’ They spread their hands, and flames come off the fingers. ‘Every hour the court crowns the newest thing in it. A song somebody’s just made up. A colour. A baby. A fight, if it’s a new fight; never the same hold twice.’ They lean close, hot as an oven. ‘Show the court something it has never seen, and if it’s newer than the Monarch, you’re the Monarch till the bell. And the Monarch has the brazier, and the petal on it.’ They shrug, in sparks. ‘Only nothing’s new to us for long. A thing seen twice is ash. And we’ve seen everything that burns.’ They look at you with sudden cunning. ‘What have you got that doesn’t?’

**Ask the hawker what is the newest thing anyone ever showed the court**

‘Oh.’ The hawker goes quite still, which for an ifrit is a kind of trance. ‘There was a woman, once, before I was lit. I was told. In a grey cloak, with a book. She showed them a snowball.’ They shiver, in sparks. ‘Cold. A thing that was cold. And then she was rude to them, which nobody had ever been.’ They lean in. ‘The oldest thing in the Fire is about an hour old. Anything older than that,’ they whisper, as if it were indecent, ‘nobody here has ever seen. We wouldn’t know what to do with ourselves.’

**>sennet__assembly**

A pebble the size of a cat rolls down off the bottom tier and stops against your boot. ‘You’re quick,’ it says, with deep approval, in a voice like gravel in a bucket. ‘Everything up there’s quick, isn’t it. I’ve heard. Birds. Weather. Weeks.’ It looks up the tunnel toward the door. ‘I’m six hundred and forty,’ it says, ‘and I have never seen a week.’ It is pitch dark. Without a light you can see nothing here, and do nothing that needs seeing. Fabian writes in his notebook by your light, and reads it you in a whisper: ‘The Assembly is slow because it is careful. The youngest of them is six hundred and forty, and I liked him best.’ In the dark you can hear the Speaker of the Slow Assembly, the Slow Assembly and Chert close by.

**Ask Fabian how he is bearing up**

‘I haven’t fainted.’ He sounds astonished. ‘I keep waiting to. I’ve been holding the notebook ready, in case.’ He looks round him at the place, at all of it, with his mouth a little open. ‘She said vulgar,’ he says. ‘It’s not vulgar. It’s the least vulgar thing I’ve ever seen.’ And he writes that down.

**Talk to Chert**

‘Still quick,’ says Chert approvingly, and rolls a little way toward you, which for him is running.

**Ask the Speaker of the Slow Assembly what it is saying**

The Speaker does not stop. Nothing stops in the Assembly. But after a while, a long while, a small pebble on the bottom tier rolls down to your feet and says, in a voice like gravel in a bucket, very fast: ‘It’s reading the motion. The second reading. That the Assembly consider whether to consider the return of the petal to the House of Doors. It’s got to the petal to the House.’ The pebble sighs. ‘It started the second reading in your year Ten-Ninety. It’ll say of tomorrow, by your sun. Doors the day after. Then it puts the question, and they’ll say yes, they always say yes, they’re only careful.’ It looks up at you. ‘If you sat through it, you’d be the petitioner. They’d give it you. Two days.’ It looks round at the tiers. ‘Two days! I can’t bear it. I’m Chert.’

**Ask Chert what he would do, if he could do anything**

‘See the sky.’ He says it at once. ‘I’ve heard about it. It’s blue, they say, and very big, and nothing holds it up. And birds. And a week.’ He rocks on his round bottom. ‘I’d go through the door. I’d roll all the way to wherever the sky is and look at it until I was bored, which would be about fifty years.’ He looks round at the Assembly, which has not moved. ‘They’d say wait. They’d say there’s a motion before the Assembly.’ He makes a noise like a small landslide. ‘There’s always a motion before the Assembly.’

**Ask Chert to join the company**

Chert rolls up onto your boot and clings there with both stone arms. ‘Don’t tell them,’ he says, nodding at the Assembly. ‘They’ll find out in about a hundred years. By then I’ll have seen a bird.’

**Pack › A candle › Light**

Tamsin lights a candle. Murrey’s Handbook, sixth edition, which you read by lamplight: ‘THE PLANE OF EARTH. The Slow Assembly may be observed from the gallery. A visit of less than a week is pointless, and of more than a week, dull.’ The chamber of the Slow Assembly is a cave as big as the inside of a hill, and the Assembly sits round it in tiers: shapes of stone, some as big as houses, some as small as a dog, perfectly still, every one of them facing the middle. In the middle, set into the floor, a petal of dark stone, and over it, on a pillar of basalt, the Speaker, who is speaking. A word comes out of the Speaker, very slowly, like a boulder coming down a slope in a dream. It takes a long time to arrive. The Speaker is in the middle of a word. You listen for a long time before you understand that the word, when it is finished, will be OF.

**Ask Chert whether he might fetch the petal out of the chamber floor, motion or no motion**

‘It’s only a motion.’ He rolls off your boot and across the chamber floor, quite slowly, while the Assembly listens to the Speaker and notices nothing, and puts both his stone arms round the petal in the floor and pulls, with a face of enormous effort, and it comes out of the rock with a sound like a cork. He rolls back with it held over his head. ‘There,’ says Chert. ‘They’ll find out in about a hundred years. They’ll say, Chert. In that voice.’ He grins with all his cracks. ‘I’ll be a hundred and forty by then. I’ll be old enough to argue.’ Tamsin takes the Earth petal.

**Ask the footman why he keeps away from the windows**

He goes red. ‘Heights, sir. Madam.’ He swallows. ‘I come up at fourteen from Fish Street. Her Ladyship took me on for my legs. I’ve not been down since. She doesn’t let us down, the servants: they don’t come back, she says.’ He looks at the window, and away. ‘Eleven years I’ve been up here, and every morning I wake up and think about the edge. There’s nothing on the other side of those windows, and everyone up here walks about as if there was.’ He straightens his coat. ‘I’d go down tomorrow if I’d a place to go to. I’d go down on my hands and knees.’

**Talk to Aubrey Halyard**

A man of thirty in a shirt he has slept in is lying on a sofa with his boots on the silk and a bottle on his chest, and does not get up. ‘Another Tourist,’ he says, to the ceiling. ‘Mother sends them in to be cheered up by. Go away and look at the view.’

**Sit down and drink with Aubrey Halyard**

You sit on the floor, since he is on the sofa, and he passes you the bottle without looking, and for an hour you drink his family’s brandy, which is very good, and he talks about nothing: horses, a girl in Sennet who married a ship’s chandler, the view, which he hates. Toward the end of the bottle he stops talking, and looks at the ceiling, and you understand that if you ask him now, he will tell you.

**Ask Aubrey Halyard what happened on the night his brother fell**

He doesn’t answer. He turns the bottle until its label faces him, as if it might help. ‘We quarrelled. On the Promenade. Perrin had found something out, family business, and he was going to give it away. Give it away, to strangers, to the wind.’ He laughs. ‘I said if he wanted to go down so badly he could jump. I said that.’ He puts the bottle down on the floor, carefully. ‘He went off to the Balustrade. I went after him. I was going to say I was sorry. And then I don’t remember. The fog came up. I remember the rail under my hands, wet. And then bells, and people shouting, and I was sitting on the gravel.’ He looks at his hands. ‘I think I put my hands on him. I think I did. I’d had a great deal to drink and I was angry, and he was my brother, and I think I put my hands on him.’

**>sennet__promenade**

The Promenade runs round the rim of Cloudgate between the great houses and the edge: gravel raked into patterns, orange trees in tubs, benches with nobody on them, and people walking slowly in pale clothes under parasols, looking at the view, which is cloud. The houses of Cloudgate are the houses of old Sennet, very old, with their ground floors gone into the rock, so that the front doors are on the first floor and the steps go up to them out of the gravel. All the windows face outward. A nursemaid from the Sennet side, in Halyard green, wheels a Cloudgate baby along the rim of the Promenade. She keeps to the inside of the path, against the house-fronts, and does not look at the view; and when the baby throws its rattle at the Balustrade she lets it lie. The Landing Stage, white and gilt, in the sun, with the cloud lying below the railings like snow.

**Go down in the basket**

Down through the cloud, and out of the bottom of the cloud into Sennet’s weather, whatever it is, with the bay coming up at you and the capstans’ thunder coming up through the floor of the basket, until you are set down on the winch-house floor and somebody unhooks you. The winch-house: four capstans as big as merry-go-rounds, each with fifteen bars and fifteen people on them, walking round and round in a thunder of boots and chain, singing. Mother Kittiwake, the master of the winch-gang, with a voice like a foghorn, is here.

**>sennet__ascent_yard**

The anchor chain comes down out of the cloud into the middle of the yard, each link the size of a door, and goes into the ground through an iron collar on a granite block the size of a house, and the whole yard hums with it, very low, the way a church does after the organ has stopped.

The Glass Market: a square of stalls under awnings, and everything on them glass.

**>sennet__lamp_street**

Lamp Street climbs toward the headland between glass-houses, every one of them with its doors open and its furnace roaring, so that walking up it is like walking past a row of opened ovens.

**>sennet__glasshouse**

Prue Quickly’s glass-house: one long hot room, a furnace at the end of it like an open mouth, and on every shelf and hook and windowsill glass: floats, bottles, rods, hand-mirrors, a chandelier half-made hanging from a beam, and a tray of red glass petals, each the length of your hand, under a card: GENUINE PETALS OF THE ROSE. Here are Prue Quickly, a glass-blower, with forearms like a stevedore’s and no eyebrows; and Sergeant Bassett, a sergeant of the Watch, in a leather hat. Sergeant Bassett of the Watch stands just inside the door with his leather hat in his hands and a notebook open on it, and Prue Quickly stands at the furnace with her arms folded, and neither of them is enjoying it.

**Tell Sergeant Bassett that it was Aubrey Halyard who pushed his brother off the Balustrade**

The sergeant writes it down, every word, on the crown of his hat, and reads it back to you, and you hear how it sounds: two brothers, a quarrel, the fog, the rail. ‘Heir to Cloudgate,’ he says. ‘That’ll be a long day.’ He puts his hat on. ‘I’ll go up and ask him. Mostly they tell you.’

The Guildhall of old Sennet: a great room with a hammer-beam roof, where the merchants met, full now of the merfolk. Two merfolk with eel-spears keep the Guildhall steps, floating, watching who comes. No Admittance, the merfolk’s matriarch, in a crown of shells with something green in it, is here.

**>sennet__air_bell**

You come up out of the water through a round hole in a floor, into air. Here are Mercy, a merfolk girl with silver in her hair; and Perrin Halyard, a young man with his leg in splints made of an oar.

**Tell Perrin Halyard that his brother is in the Watch-house, for pushing him**

He does not say anything. He puts the drowned book down on the weed, very carefully, open, so as not to lose his place, and then he looks at the oar splinted to his leg as if it were somebody else’s. ‘He’ll say yes,’ he says at last. ‘He’ll say yes to all of it, because he thinks it’s true. The idiot. The great kind idiot.’ He reaches for the oar. ‘Get me up those stairs.’

**Ask Perrin Halyard to come up out of the Whelm with you**

‘Yes.’ He is already pulling himself toward the hole in the floor. ‘Mercy will bring me, she’ll have to, I’m a stone in the water with this leg.’ He looks at you over his shoulder. ‘Where? Not Mother’s. Not yet. Somewhere she’d come to.’ He thinks. ‘Somewhere very public, Isadora would say. She always said, if you’ve something to say, say it where it can’t be unsaid.’ Perrin Halyard gets the oar under his arm and stands, white to the lips, and grins at you. ‘Lead on. Slowly. I’m a dead man; we don’t hurry.’

The eighth morning. The Door of Doors is shut at the top of the tower, and the Rose is in pieces where it was, and Cloudgate is in the sky, and the Whelm is under the bay, and the gulls stand on the bollards of the Quay as if they had been appointed to them.
