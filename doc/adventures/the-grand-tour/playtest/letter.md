# The Letter

Played by Wren, a thief (a burglar); seed `sennet`; 22 steps given, 199 taken; ended at watch 1014, at the ending "The Letter". Written by `bun scripts/the-grand-tour-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The coast road comes over the last of the downs, and there below you, all at once, is the sea, and a city going down to it in glass. Above the city, over the bay, the cloud lies on the water like a fleece; and standing up out of the cloud, in sunlight, is a hill. It has houses on it, and gardens hanging over its edges, and a chain coming down out of the bottom of it to the city like a plumb-line, as if somebody had lifted the hill up out of the bay by it and forgotten to put it back. Under the bay, where the hill came out, there is nothing to see at all. A milestone at the roadside says SENNET 1, and under it someone has scratched: AND THEN UP, OR DOWN.

**>sennet__landward_gate**

You set out from the top of the coast road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. The bells of the House of Doors ring eight across the bay, and the gulls go up off the Quay all at once, and over all of it, over the cloud, a hill of houses catches the sun. The Landward Gate is a gate in no wall: the wall came down a century ago and the gate was kept because the city liked it. Beyond it Sennet goes down to the water in roofs of green copper and glass, glass everywhere, glass-house chimneys smoking, and the bay beyond, very blue. Under everything, so low you feel it in your feet before you hear it, the city hums: that is the chain.

**>sennet__glass_market**

The Glass Market: a square of stalls under awnings, and everything on them glass. Glass floats for nets, glass eyes for dolls, glass bottles with the light of the Fire Door still moving in them, sold as night-lights. A crier walks the square with a board on a pole. The crier’s board says, in red: THE GRAND TOUR IS PROCLAIMED. Underneath, smaller, in black: PERRIN HALYARD, DOORWARD, FELL 12 DAYS SINCE. LET DOWN ON THE 3RD DAY AT NOON. Someone has chalked beneath both of them: MIND YOUR HEADS.

**>sennet__quay**

The Quay: granite, wet, smelling of weed and tar and frying. Boats tied two deep. Eel barrows, lobster pots, a gull standing on a bollard with the air of having been appointed to it. Looking up from here, Cloudgate is directly overhead, and its underside is rock, brown and hanging, like the bottom of a turnip pulled out of the ground. No ferry runs between the Quay and Halyard Hill while the hill is in the sky. Old Bethan’s eyes are red at the rims, and twice while you watch she starts to nod over the eels and jerks awake.

**>sennet__taproom**

The Sounding Line is low, and dark, and full of fishermen. A sounding-lead the size of a child’s head hangs over the bar on its line, and every so often somebody pats it for luck without looking. The window looks straight out across the bay at nothing but water and, above the water, the underside of Cloudgate. On the bar, the Sennet Courant, with this week’s part of the Rector’s memoirs. THRESHOLDS, by Magistra I. Quance. Chapter the Twenty-Ninth: Under the Plate. ‘I was born in a room on Fish Street that the spring tides came into, and my mother sold eels on the Quay from the barrow beside the steps. On Ascension night I sat on the third step from the bottom, on the brass plate that says how high the sea may come, and watched the lanterns go up from a hill I would never be let up; and it seemed to me that the plate had been put there for me as much as for the sea.’ Peg Arkle, the landlady of the Sounding Line, is here.

**>sennet__ascent_yard**

The anchor chain comes down out of the cloud into the middle of the yard, each link the size of a door, and goes into the ground through an iron collar on a granite block the size of a house, and the whole yard hums with it, very low, the way a church does after the organ has stopped. Beside it the winch-house, all doors and chimneys, smoking. Looking straight up the chain you lose it in the cloud a long way up, and above the cloud, very small, very sunlit, the underside of Cloudgate.

**>sennet__winch_floor**

The winch-house: four capstans as big as merry-go-rounds, each with fifteen bars and fifteen people on them, walking round and round in a thunder of boots and chain, singing. In the middle of the floor the basket waits on its hook, a wicker thing the size of a cart with a bench in it and a rail round it, and a slate hanging off the rail: UP 5 COIN. DOWN FREE. NO DOGS. Mother Kittiwake, the master of the winch-gang, with a voice like a foghorn, is here.

**Pay five coin and go up in the basket**

You sit on the bench. Mother Kittiwake blows her whistle and the capstans start to walk, and the floor drops away, and the roof, and the winch-house is a box of noise under you, and the city a map, and the bay a plate, and then the cloud comes down over all of it, white, cold, wet, for a long time, nothing but the chain going up beside you link by link like a ladder in a dream. And then you come up out of it, and Cloudgate is over your head with its gardens hanging over the edge, and the basket comes up through a hole in the Landing Stage like a bucket out of milk. Even at night it is not quite dark in Cloudgate. Above the cloud the stars are enormous, and the cloud below gives back a light of its own, like snow under a moon.

**>sennet__promenade**

The Promenade at night: the gravel grey under enormous stars, the orange trees black in their tubs, the great houses shuttered, and all round the rim the cloud lying pale below the Balustrade like a field of snow under a moon. Somebody in one of the houses is playing a piano, badly, with the window open. Here are Aubrey Halyard, the Halyards’ heir, unshaven, in last night’s shirt; the Honourable Florian Mottram, a Tourist from Cloudgate, in a coat the colour of a sunset; and Miss Agatha Sone, a bear-leader, square and grey, with a sergeant’s moustache of eyebrow.

The drawing room of Halyard House runs the width of the house with windows all down the outer side, so that the cloud fills them like a white wall. Every chair is gilt. Down the inner wall, Halyards in oils look down their noses at the windows, beginning with a lady in green with a hill on her hand. An escritoire of black lacquer stands under the portrait of the lady in green, locked.

**Pick the lock of the escritoire, while the drawing room is empty · likely**

The drawing room is dark but for the cloud in the windows, which gives back a light of its own. The lock is old and good and French, and takes you a quarter of an hour, with the Halyards in oils watching you down their noses the whole time; and then the lid comes down into your hands without a sound. **>sennet__wind_court**

The Wind Court is a round garden in the middle of Cloudgate, walled, with no plants in it but grass, and the grass all lying flat in one direction. In the wall on the far side is an arch of white stone, very old, with nothing in it but sky; and out of the sky in it comes a wind, steady, cold, smelling of snow, that never stops. A bar of silver lies across the arch of the Air Door, with the Halyards’ seal on it in green wax: whoever has the Halyards’ leave lifts it, and sets it back.

**Through to the far side of the Air Door — break the Halyards' seal, and lift the bar off the Air Door**

Somewhere behind you on the Promenade a footman shouts. The bar of the Air Door lies on the grass, and the Halyards’ seal is broken.

**>sennet__air_threshold**

Through the arch, and there is nothing under your feet, and you do not fall. You stand on air, as on a floor of glass that is not there, and below you, a very long way below, cloud, and below the cloud, more sky. Wind goes past you in every direction at once, warm and cold, carrying voices. All the voices are talking about something. The bar of the Air Door lies on the grass, and the Halyards’ seal is broken.

**>sennet__gallery**

The Gallery of Winds is a long hall made of nothing, a hall you know is there by the way the wind goes round its pillars. The sylphs of the Air are in it, a great many of them, slight and quick and nearly transparent, talking, all of them, all the time, in voices that go past you and come back from behind. Every word said anywhere in the hall is said everywhere in it a moment later. The north wind will not let you up: it puts you back down in the Gallery like a leaf. The sylphs say he receives only whoever is most talked of. In the voices going past, you hear, over and over, the same few things: who the Rector of the House of Doors is dining with, what the Mottram boy said about his tailor, and your own name, which nobody here has been told. The sylphs of the Gallery, the sylphs of the Air, a great many, all talking, are here.

**Talk to the sylphs of the Gallery**

A sylph comes past you, turns round in the air, comes back, and looks at you very close, as a bird does. ‘Oh, it’s you!’ they say. ‘We’ve heard all about you. Have you heard what they’re saying about you?’ They do not wait for an answer. ‘Neither have we. Tell us something.’

**Ask the sylphs about the Air petal of the Rose**

‘Oh, that!’ They are delighted. It is the oldest thing in the Air to talk about and they talk about it. ‘Tramontane gave it away! The North Wind! To a girl! A mortal girl from the Sennet side, Ysabeau, with red hair, two hundred years ago, they talked all one summer up in his eyrie and he gave her the petal for a keepsake, the court’s own petal, imagine, and she took it home and set it in the bottom of her hill and lifted her whole family into the sky with it.’ They go round you, faster. ‘And the sea came in. And she never came back. And he’s been blowing cold ever since, haven’t you noticed, the north wind, it’s cold, it’s because of her.’ They come close. ‘It’s still there! In the hill! Holding them up! Everyone knows! Nobody ever says!’

**Tell the sylphs one true thing about yourself**

You tell them. You do not know why you choose the thing you choose; it is the first true thing that comes. They listen, all of them, perfectly still for once, and then they take it away with them in every direction at once, and a moment later it comes back to you from behind, said in a hundred voices, and it sounds, said by them, like something worth having said.

**Tell the sylphs, out loud, that the Air petal is in the keel of Cloudgate**

‘We know!’ they cry, all of them, enchanted. ‘But nobody’s ever said it! Out loud! In the Gallery!’ And they are off, saying it, out loud, in every direction, all the way down.

**>sennet__eyrie**

The eyrie of the North Wind is the top of the sky: blue going to black, stars in the day, cold enough to hurt. There is nothing here but the wind, and the wind is a person: a tall pale shape with hair of snow streaming from it, who has been looking out over the edge of the world at nothing for a very long time. Tramontane, the North Wind, a prince of the Air, with hair of snow, is here.

**Talk to Tramontane**

The tall pale shape turns from the edge of the world, slowly, and looks at you, and you are cold to the bone. ‘They talk of you,’ says the North Wind. His voice is the sound of a wind round a house at night. ‘Down there, and up here. So you are let up.’ He looks at you longer. ‘You have come from Sennet. You smell of the sea.’ And then, after a long time: ‘Is it still there? The hill?’

**Tell the North Wind the hill is still there, and ask him about Ysabeau**

He is silent so long the stars move. ‘She came up through the arch at seventeen, on a dare, with her skirts tied up, and stood on nothing, and was not afraid. Nobody had come up for a hundred years.’ The wind goes round you, softer. ‘We talked a summer. I was four hundred; I was young. At the end of it she said, give me something to remember you by, and I gave her the petal. The court’s petal. I had no right.’ His face does not change. ‘She went home and set it in the bottom of her family’s hill and lifted them into the sky above the smell of fish. And the sea came in under them, and her people’s people drowned in their beds. And she never came back up the arch. Not once.’ He looks at the edge of the world. ‘She used it as a lever. I gave her my heart and she used it as a lever.’ A pause. ‘I have blown cold since. You will have noticed.’ Wren gains 75 experience: was let up to the North Wind.

**Give Ysabeau's letter**

You give Ysabeau’s letter to Tramontane. The North Wind reads it. It is two lines; he reads it for a long time. Then his face, which has not changed since you came up, changes, and the cold goes out of the eyrie all at once like a held breath let go, and somewhere far below, over Sennet, in June, it begins to snow: big soft flakes out of a clear sky, falling on the Quay and the glass-houses and the bay and on Cloudgate, settling on the orange trees. ‘Remember me,’ says Tramontane. ‘She said that.’ He folds the letter very small. ‘Then let her house keep what I gave her. Tell them it is a keepsake. Tell them it is not theirs.’ He looks at you. ‘And tell them the North Wind is sorry about the sea.’ Wren gains 150 experience: had the North Wind’s gift renewed.

**Ask the North Wind what he will give whoever brings his gift home**

He takes a feather out of the air, or out of himself; it is hard to tell. ‘Carry this,’ he says. ‘Whoever carries it does not fall.’ He gives it you. ‘It seemed to me a thing somebody in your city should have.’ You take a feather of the North Wind.

It snowed on Sennet in June: big soft flakes out of a clear sky, on the Quay and the glass-houses and the bay, on Cloudgate’s orange trees, on the brass plate on the third step of the Fish Stairs. By morning it has gone, and Cloudgate is still in the sky, held up by a keepsake that is not its own, by leave of a wind who was given a letter two hundred years late, by a hand that had no business with it. Nobody at Halyard House knows who opened the escritoire. Lady Halyard has had the lock changed, and sat a long time in the drawing room with an empty box and a green ribbon in her lap, and has not sent for the Watch. The sylphs know, and are telling everyone. Nobody believes them; it is too good.
