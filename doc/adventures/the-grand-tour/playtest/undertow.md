# The Undertow

Played by Brann, a warrior (a labourer); seed `sennet`; 29 steps given, 64 taken; ended at watch 150, at the ending "The Undertow". Written by `bun scripts/the-grand-tour-transcripts.ts` from the journey as the page told it.

*(The first page)*

The coast road comes over the last of the downs, and there below you, all at once, is the sea, and a city going down to it in glass. Above the city, over the bay, the cloud lies on the water like a fleece; and standing up out of the cloud, in sunlight, is a hill. It has houses on it, and gardens hanging over its edges, and a chain coming down out of the bottom of it to the city like a plumb-line, as if somebody had lifted the hill up out of the bay by it and forgotten to put it back. Under the bay, where the hill came out, there is nothing to see at all. A milestone at the roadside says SENNET 1, and under it someone has scratched: AND THEN UP, OR DOWN.

**>sennet__landward_gate**

You set out from the top of the coast road early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. The bells of the House of Doors ring eight across the bay, and the gulls go up off the Quay all at once, and over all of it, over the cloud, a hill of houses catches the sun. The Landward Gate is a gate in no wall: the wall came down a century ago and the gate was kept because the city liked it. Beyond it Sennet goes down to the water in roofs of green copper and glass, glass everywhere, glass-house chimneys smoking, and the bay beyond, very blue. Under everything, so low you feel it in your feet before you hear it, the city hums: that is the chain.

**>sennet__glass_market**

The Glass Market: a square of stalls under awnings, and everything on them glass. Glass floats for nets, glass eyes for dolls, glass bottles with the light of the Fire Door still moving in them, sold as night-lights. A crier walks the square with a board on a pole. The crier’s board says, in red: THE GRAND TOUR IS PROCLAIMED. Underneath, smaller, in black: PERRIN HALYARD, DOORWARD, FELL 12 DAYS SINCE. LET DOWN ON THE 3RD DAY AT NOON. Someone has chalked beneath both of them: MIND YOUR HEADS.

**>sennet__quay**

The Quay: granite, wet, smelling of weed and tar and frying. Boats tied two deep. Eel barrows, lobster pots, a gull standing on a bollard with the air of having been appointed to it. Looking up from here, Cloudgate is directly overhead, and its underside is rock, brown and hanging, like the bottom of a turnip pulled out of the ground. No ferry runs between the Quay and Halyard Hill while the hill is in the sky. Old Bethan’s eyes are red at the rims, and twice while you watch she starts to nod over the eels and jerks awake.

**>sennet__fish_stairs**

The Fish Stairs go down off the end of the Quay into the bay: green granite, worn hollow in the middle, going down and down under the water until the light gives up on them. A brass plate is let into the third step from the bottom of the dry ones: BY ORDER: NO PERSON OF THE SEA ABOVE THIS STEP. MDCIX. On the third step from the bottom, exactly on the brass plate, as if to make a point, a merfolk child sits with a basket of cockles and her tail in the water. Ha’penny, a merfolk child with a basket of cockles, is here.

**Talk to Ha'penny**

A merfolk child is sitting on the third step from the bottom of the dry ones, with her tail in the sea and a basket of cockles in her lap: green-skinned, freckled with silver, with gills under her ears like the gills of a fish, which open and shut when she is interested, which is always. ‘Cockles,’ she says. ‘Ha’penny a scoop. That’s my name too. Ha’penny. What’s yours?’

**Ask Ha'penny what is up the stairs that she wants**

‘The market.’ She says it the way other children say Midsummer. ‘The glass one. Where everything’s glass and the light’s in the bottles.’ She looks up the stairs, all the way up, to where they turn the corner onto the Quay. ‘I want to buy a present. With my own pearl.’ She shows you: a pearl the size of a pea, in her fist. ‘For Mercy. She’s gone funny. She smiles at nothing all day and won’t come to the shoals.’ Her gills shut, offended. ‘But it’s above the plate. The fat man with the telescope says five coin to anybody who brings me.’ She considers you. ‘Have you got five coin?’

**Lift Ha'penny into a fish-basket and carry her up the Fish Stairs to the Glass Market**

She weighs about as much as a wet dog, and she is in the fish-basket before you have finished lifting it, with her tail curled round under her and her arms over the rim, and her gills going like bellows. Up the stairs, past the brass plate (she leans out to spit on it), along the Quay, where every head turns, up the Haul, and into the Glass Market, where she sees the bottles with the light moving in them and says nothing at all for the first time since you met her. Ha’penny grips the rim of the basket with both hands, like a queen in a carriage. ‘Go on, then,’ she says. ‘Before the fat man looks.’ The Glass Market: a square of stalls under awnings, and everything on them glass. In her fish-basket Ha’penny is looking at everything at once: the bottles with the light in them, the glass floats, a stall of hand-mirrors, the crier’s board, a dog. The stallholders have stopped crying their wares to look at her, and she at them.

**>sennet__lamp_street**

Lamp Street climbs toward the headland between glass-houses, every one of them with its doors open and its furnace roaring, so that walking up it is like walking past a row of opened ovens. Over each furnace a copper pipe comes down out of the street’s great main, which runs up the hill on iron legs to the House of Doors, and hums. Between the glass-houses, two shops: MURREY’S, under a sign of a gilt key, with a window full of the same red book; and PARGETER’S, OUTFITTERS TO THE GRAND TOUR, with a dummy in the window wearing a fire-cloak, a brine-glass helm and an expression of great confidence. Sergeant Bassett, a sergeant of the Watch, in a leather hat, is here.

**>sennet__glasshouse**

Prue Quickly’s glass-house: one long hot room, a furnace at the end of it like an open mouth, and on every shelf and hook and windowsill glass: floats, bottles, rods, hand-mirrors, a chandelier half-made hanging from a beam, and a tray of red glass petals, each the length of your hand, under a card: GENUINE PETALS OF THE ROSE. ONE COIN. Prue Quickly, a glass-blower, with forearms like a stevedore’s and no eyebrows, is here.

**Let Ha'penny choose her present**

Ha’penny goes along Prue’s shelves in her fish-basket like a duchess at a sale, touching nothing, looking at everything, while Prue Quickly watches her with her arms folded and something happening to her face. In the end Ha’penny chooses a hand-mirror in a copper frame, the plainest, and holds out her pearl for it on her flat palm. Prue looks at the pearl, which would buy the shop, and closes Ha’penny’s fingers back over it. ‘Keep that,’ she says. ‘Mirrors are a ha’penny today. For people called Ha’penny.’ And she gives her a little blue glass fish as well, for change, and Ha’penny holds it up to the furnace light and sees the bubble in it and makes a sound like a kettle. Brann gains 50 experience: brought Ha’penny above the third step to buy a present.

**Ask Ha'penny why Mercy needs a present**

‘Because she’s gone soft.’ Ha’penny holds the mirror up and looks at herself in it and makes a face. ‘She caught a man. Out of the sky. He came down through the cloud in the fog like a gannet, twelve nights ago, and she caught him before the bottom got him, and took him to the air-bell, and she keeps him there, and feeds him, and he’s broken his leg, and he reads books, and she won’t come to the shoals.’ She puts the mirror against her chest. ‘Gran says he’s a dry man and no good. I’ve seen him. He’s all right. He talks to me.’ She looks at you sideways. ‘Don’t tell. It’s a secret. Everybody knows.’

**Take Ha'penny back down to the Fish Stairs**

She is quiet on the way back, holding the mirror. At the third step she slides out of the basket and into the water to the shoulders and turns round. ‘You can come to supper,’ she says. ‘In the Guildhall. Gran won’t mind. She will, but she won’t.’ She looks at the stairs going down into the dark. ‘You need breath to come. I can give you some. Mine doesn’t last long. I’m little.’ And she ducks, and is gone, and the mirror flashes once, going down. Ha’penny flicks her tail once and is gone into the green, the way the merfolk go: not leaving, just not there. The Fish Stairs go down off the end of the Quay into the bay: green granite, worn hollow in the middle, going down and down under the water until the light gives up on them. Ha’penny, a merfolk child with a basket of cockles, is here.

*(10 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › A while**

On the third step from the bottom, exactly on the brass plate, as if to make a point, a merfolk child sits with a basket of cockles and her tail in the water. Time passes.

**Ask Ha'penny for some of her breath**

She puts her arms round your neck and kisses you on the nose, very loud. ‘There. An hour. I’m little.’ She considers. ‘If it runs out, come up. Don’t be brave. The brave ones are the ones on the bottom.’

**>sennet__drowned_stair**

Green. Everything is green, and slow, and the light comes down from above in long wavering ropes. The Fish Stairs go down out of the sky into a street: a real street, cobbled, with houses on both sides of it with their doors open and their windows open and weed growing out of their chimneys, and lamps lit in some of the windows, white lamps, burning steady under the sea. A shoal of little silver fish goes up the street like a crowd going home.

**>sennet__drowned_street**

Chandlers’ Row, under the water: shopfronts with their glass long gone and their signs still up, the gilt letters furred with weed, CHANDLER, TALLOW AND WAX, ROOMS TO LET, CASH ONLY, SHUTTERS, HA’PENNY A SCOOP. The merfolk live in the shops. Through the open doors you see them at their lives: a woman combing a child’s hair, an old man asleep in a drowned armchair, two boys fighting over a teapot. Two merfolk with eel-spears keep the Guildhall steps, floating, watching who comes.

**Tell the guards Ha'penny asked you to supper**

The two guards look at each other. One of them says something to the other in bubbles, and the other laughs, also in bubbles. ‘Ha’penny,’ the first says. ‘She brings us everything. Last week a cat.’ He moves his spear aside. ‘Supper is at the turn of the evening.’ He leans close and breathes on you, which is how the Whelm says come in; the water in your chest goes soft and easy. ‘Go up. Eat what you are given.’

**>sennet__guildhall**

The Guildhall of old Sennet: a great room with a hammer-beam roof, where the merchants met, full now of the merfolk. White Aenean lamps hang from the beams on chains. At the far end, in the drowned mayor’s chair, sits an old woman with a crown of shells. Over the small door at the back of the Guildhall, in old gilt letters furred with weed, a sign: NO ADMITTANCE. Two merfolk with eel-spears keep the Guildhall steps, floating, watching who comes. No Admittance, the merfolk’s matriarch, in a crown of shells with something green in it, is here.

*(5 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Sit down at No Admittance's table, and eat what you are given**

Supper in the Whelm is laid on the old merchants’ table, under the white lamps: raw things, in brine, on drowned plates with the arms of the Glovers’ Company on them. Eels. Something with too many legs that is still deciding whether to be dead. Weed in long green ribbons. A soup of nothing you can name that tastes of the inside of a shell. The merfolk watch you eat it, every mouthful, in a silence like a theatre. No Admittance watches longest. When you have eaten it all she picks something off her own plate, a little white thing like a pearl, and holds it out to you on her palm. You eat that too. The Guildhall breathes out. ‘Well,’ says No Admittance. ‘Well. That is how it is done.’

**Ask No Admittance what she thinks of the dry folk of Sennet**

‘They let the sea into their own houses.’ She sweeps a hand at the Guildhall, the street outside, the church. ‘Their great ones went up into the sky and the sea came into the hole, and their little ones drowned in their beds, and we came through two lifetimes after and found the lamps still lit and the tables laid.’ She leans forward. ‘And then the dry men set a brass plate on the third step and told the sea how far it may come up their stairs.’ She sits back. ‘No admittance. Above the third step, no admittance.’ She does not seem to hear what she has said.

**Tell No Admittance what the sign over the back door of her Guildhall says, and what it means in Sennet**

You tell her. In Sennet, over a door, NO ADMITTANCE means keep out. It is what they put up on the door to the cellar. The Guildhall goes so still you can hear the lamps hum. No Admittance looks at you. She looks over her shoulder at the little door at the back of the hall, and the gilt letters over it, for a long time. Then her shoulders begin to shake, and her crown slips, and she puts up a hand to it, and the sound that comes out of her is so loud and so long that the fish go out of the windows. She laughs until she weeps, and the merfolk laugh with her because she is laughing, and then because they understand. ‘Keep out!’ she says, wiping her eyes. ‘Sixty years. Sixty years I have been keep out, and so proud of it.’ She looks at you with her wet-stone eyes and they are not stones at all. ‘Well. We are the same, then, the plate and I. The plate and I should meet.’ Brann gains 50 experience: made No Admittance laugh.

**Ask Mercy how she caught the man who fell out of the sky**

‘In the fog.’ She says it with her whole face, the way Ha’penny talks about the bell. ‘I was at the top, at the cloud, where the light is, the night of the first fog. And something came down through it. A man, all arms, with his coat open like wings, falling.’ She holds out her arms. ‘I went up under him and caught him in the water before the bottom got him, and his leg broke on me, and he was drowning, so I gave him breath and took him down to the air-bell, the one the guests sleep in, and he woke up and saw me and said, I’m dead, aren’t I.’ She laughs. ‘And I said no, you’re in the Whelm. And he said, that’s worse, my mother would die.’ She looks at you. ‘His name is Perrin. He’s mine. Do you want to see him?’ Brann gains 50 experience: found the dead man alive.

**>sennet__air_bell**

You come up out of the water through a round hole in a floor, into air. Real air, cool and stale and smelling of copper. You are inside a dome of something like glass, as big as a room, set on the bottom of the sea, with the green light outside it on every side and fish looking in. An Aenean lamp burns on a hook. On the floor: a mattress of weed, a heap of books drying in a row, a broken chair, and a young man with his leg in splints made of an oar. Perrin Halyard, a young man with his leg in splints made of an oar, is here.

**Ask Perrin Halyard about the Aenean books he is drying**

‘Oh!’ His whole face changes, the way Mercy’s does about the lanterns. ‘You won’t believe it. The House of Doors, the Collegium, was built on top of the Aeneans’ own. Ours is a copy. Theirs is down there, under the Guildhall.’ He points at the floor of the bell. ‘The tide-engine. It drives a shaft that goes up through the headland to the Nexus, and if you start it, it opens the Door of Doors from below. No Rose. No petals. The Aeneans never needed a key; they had the sea.’ He laughs. ‘Isadora would sell her soul for it. I haven’t told her. I’ve been rather busy being dead.’ He sobers. ‘It runs on the whole tide of the Whelm, though. Start it, and every lamp down here goes out for a day, and the air-bells empty.’ He looks round at the bell he is lying in. ‘Including this one.’

**>sennet__guildhall**

The Guildhall of old Sennet: a great room with a hammer-beam roof, where the merchants met, full now of the merfolk. Two merfolk with eel-spears keep the Guildhall steps, floating, watching who comes. Here are No Admittance, the merfolk’s matriarch, in a crown of shells with something green in it; and Mercy, a merfolk girl with silver in her hair.

**Ask No Admittance for leave to start the tide-engine**

She is quiet a long time. ‘A day without lamps,’ she says. ‘A day without the bells. The children will be frightened. The old ones will not come out of their houses.’ She looks at you. ‘We have never started it. Our grandmothers read what it does, and decided they had no door they wanted that much.’ She turns her crown in her hands. ‘You want a door that much?’ And then, before you can answer: ‘If you do it, do it with us. With every hand of the Whelm on the wheel, so it is ours that did it, not yours. And not with anyone left in a bell. Move the dry man first.’

**Ask Mercy to take Perrin out of the air-bell for the day, while the engine runs**

‘Out of the bell?’ She looks at you, and at the floor, through which, somewhere, the great wheel is waiting. Then she nods. ‘To the Guildhall. With me. I’ll breathe for him all day; he won’t like it, he says I breathe like a seal.’ She goes down through the floor of the water like a dropped stone, and you hear, faintly, up through the green, Perrin Halyard objecting.

**>sennet__undercroft**

Below the Guildhall the merfolk’s town stops and something much older begins: walls of black stone fitted without mortar, tall arches, everything square and plain and cut with a precision that hurts the eye. The Aenean Undercroft. The lamps here are the same white lamps, but there are hundreds of them, in rows, and they hum.

**>sennet__engine_room**

The tide-engine fills a hall the size of a church: a great wheel of black metal, standing on its edge, half in a pit, with paddles on it the size of doors, and round it a confusion of shafts and chains and copper, all green, all still. A plaque in the wall is cut in letters you half know. The merfolk are here, all of them, the whole Whelm, holding the paddles of the great wheel in silence, waiting for you.

**Put your hands on the great wheel beside the merfolk, and turn the tide-engine over**

No Admittance says a word in bubbles, and the whole Whelm leans on the wheel, and so do you, and nothing happens, and nothing happens, and then the sea moves. You feel it: the whole weight of the bay, swinging, the way the tide swings at the turn, and the wheel goes over with it, slowly, enormously, and the paddles come up out of the pit streaming, and somewhere far above in the rock a chain begins to run. All down the Undercroft the white lamps go out, row by row, as if somebody were walking along them with a snuffer. In the dark, under your hands, the wheel turns. There is a tightness coming in your chest, very gentle, like a hand. What breath you were given is running out.

**>sennet__shaft_foot**

The passage ends at the foot of a shaft, square, black, going straight up into the rock, with a cage of black metal standing at the bottom of it on rails, like a lift in a mine, waiting. The cage at the foot of the shaft is humming, and its door stands open.

**Step into the Aenean cage, and ride it up the shaft**

The cage goes up. It goes up through black rock for a long time, out of the water and into air, cold, then dry, rattling, with the chain running somewhere beside you and the tide working the whole of it from below like a heart; and stops; and the roof of the cage opens like a hatch, and you climb out of the floor of a round room at the top of a tower, beside a door standing by itself with nothing behind it. As you watch, the Door of Doors swings open on its own, pushed from below by the whole weight of the sea, and on the other side of it there is everywhere. Feet on the tower stair, running, and the tower door bangs back: the Rector of the House of Doors, with her gown of four colours flying and her chain of keys in her fist, stops in the doorway of the Nexus and sees the Door of Doors standing open on everywhere, and you beside it. She does not say anything at all. She looks past you, at it, the way a woman looks at a sea she has walked a long way to see. Brann gains 150 experience: opened the Door of Doors from below, by the Whelm’s leave. The Nexus is a round room at the top of the tower with no windows and no furniture. In the middle of it a door stands by itself in a frame, with nothing behind it: you can walk round it. On this side it is a plain door of pale wood with a round boss in the middle, carved into a rose of four petals, and four hollows in the rose where petals should be. On the far side, when you walk round, it is the same door. A cat’s dish stands by it, empty. The Door of Doors stands open in the middle of the round room, and through it, everywhere: the coast road, the Hour Court, a beach, the moon, a kitchen with a woman singing, all of them at once and each the only one. A wind comes through it that smells of every weather. The tower door is locked. The lock is the size of a book. Magistra Isadora Quance, the Rector of the House of Doors, in a gown of four colours and a chain of keys, is here.

**Step through the Door of Doors, first**

You step through, and for a moment you are everywhere, and it is not like anything; it is like being a door. Then you think of the Nexus, because it is the last place you were, and you are standing in it again with the wind of everywhere at your back, and you are the first person in two hundred years to have done that, and you know, without anybody telling you, that the Door knows you now. That it will open for you on anywhere you have stood. And that any door in the world, knocked on three times by you, will open on this room. Brann gains 100 experience: went first through the Door of Doors in two hundred years.

**Stand in the doorway of the Door of Doors, and bar the Rector's way**

You step into the doorway, with everywhere at your back, and she stops a yard from you. For a moment you think she will try; she is taller than you, and has her keys in her fist, and you have seen what is in her face. Then she laughs, a little, and sits down on the floor of the Nexus in her gown of four colours, with her back against the wall, and looks at the Door over your shoulder. ‘I shall sit here, then,’ says Isadora Quance, ‘and look at it. Nobody can stop me looking.’

**Wait › An hour**

Of Perrin Halyard: he is dead, officially, and was let down on the third day, and is in an air-bell under the bay with his leg mending, reading drowned books by an Aenean lamp, and is, the merfolk say, the happiest dead man in the Whelm. Of Ha’penny: she sits on the third step every morning with her cockles and a glass fish, which she shows to the harbour children, who have never seen a merfolk child so pleased with a thing she cannot eat. Of Thursday, the House’s cat: Mr. Tench saw him go through the cat-flap of the tower door yesterday, as he always does, and come out an hour later with sand in his fur from a beach nobody in Sennet has ever seen. The first through the Door of Doors in two hundred years, Mr. Tench says, may not have been a person. Of the Rector: she sat all night on the floor of the Nexus looking at the Door of Doors, and in the morning went down the tower stair and gave out that the Grand Tour was won, and that she would not go through herself, having seen it; which the Courant prints, and believes.

**Wait › An hour**

The Door of Doors was opened from below, by the whole of the Whelm with its hands on an Aenean wheel, and by you; and the Rose was never made. The four petals of it are where they are: in their courts, in your packs, in the keel of a hill in the sky. The Grand Tour is declared, by a House that does not know what else to declare, won. The lamps of the Whelm came back on at the turn of the next tide, row by row along the Undercroft, as if somebody were walking along them with a taper; and the merfolk came out of their houses into the green and looked at one another, and No Admittance said, in bubbles, that it had been a long time since anybody in the Whelm had done anything, and that she had liked it.
