# Half the Hive — Stage 2: severe editorial criticism

*An attack on [01-treatment.md](01-treatment.md), written as an editor who has read the rest of the collection and is
tired of being charmed. The design that answers it is [03-revised-design.md](03-revised-design.md).*

## The verdict

A setting with a genuinely original material law (belonging by smell) and a dramatic occasion (a town that must split)
that is not one more misread inheritance. But the treatment is in love with its premise and has not yet made it hurt.
Its principal choice has a right answer. Its romance is stock. Its antagonist is furniture. Its fourth act happens after
everyone who matters has walked off the page. And it is sweet: bees, honey, gold light, cello voices. The collection's
house style has a weakness for the warm and the consoling; this treatment would indulge it.

## 1. Is the setting truly distinctive?

Yes, at the level of the law, and nearly at the level of the image. The danger is whimsy. Bees as big as foxhounds are
either a children's book or a horror, and the treatment has not chosen. **Choose horror's honesty without its
genre**: a Sister's sting is a crossbow bolt and a lethal dose of venom; a Sister that stings dies, tearing her own
gut out; the Humefolk know this and grieve each sting. The Comb is beautiful *and* a place where a stranger whose
scent has worn off at the wrong moment dies screaming on the stair. Put the cost in every image.

## 2. Does it feel inhabited apart from the plot?

Not yet. Every named person is defined by whether they go or stay. That is thematically tidy and dramatically thin;
real people in a week of crisis go on caring about the price of tallow. The treatment needs people with no stake in
the Leaving, and it has one ready-made: **the lowland traders who come up for it**. Leavers can carry nothing; their
wax, honey, furniture and tools must be sold or abandoned. The Chandlers' factor is not background: he is running a
**Leavers' Market** at a fifth of value, and Smoke End is having its best week in thirty years. That is the satire the
setting naturally contains (every emigration has its fire-sale and its vultures) and it gives the protagonist a trade
to take part in, honestly or not.

Also missing: ordinary pleasures. What do they eat? (Bee-bread; honey-cakes; mead; and **drone brood fried in its
comb**, the Brow's delicacy, which the human Drones are served at their feasts and nobody finds funny but outsiders.)
What do they do at night? (They visit the dead. They sing in the hum's key. They listen to the walls.)

## 3. Are the small adventures substantial and varied?

Thirteen are named; five are a sentence. *Duke the pet drone* is a fetch quest. *Honey-hunting* is a job, not a story.
*The mead* is a texture. *The Teague brothers* are a plot convenience. Cut or join:

- **Duke** becomes the way into the Marls' secret: Tibby is the only person who knows where his grandmother goes at
  night, because he follows her to look for Duke. A child-witness, not a fetch.
- **Honey-hunting** stays as livelihood, not as an "adventure".
- **The mead** becomes a short comedy of making with a judgement at the Calling feast, joined to Huw's vanity and to
  the moor (heather honey); otherwise cut.
- **The Teagues** are cut. Corve does not need bribed dancers; he has the flask.

## 4. Is the principal story dramatically compelling?

No, because **it has a right answer**. Read the table of sites: three are traps and one, the Far Brow, is perfect. A
player who scouts all four dances for the Far Brow and wins. That is a puzzle with a solution, not a choice.

**Make every site cost someone.** The Far Brow is a day over the moor: the old, the lame and Agnes with her dead cannot
walk it. Choosing it is choosing that Hannet Coke stays behind. The Gallowtree is close enough for everyone and will kill
them by spring. The Dell is good if the brocks die or the old bargain with them is remembered. The Chantry is close,
safe, and unfree. The best outcomes are made, not found: a road round the moor that the old can walk, carried on a
litter by people who were not going to carry anyone; a truce with the brocks found in an old woman's memory; a squire
persuaded to make a gift instead of a claim.

And **the bees have opinions**. The treatment makes the Humefolk dancers the only voters. Real swarms decide by their
own scouts. Let the Sisters' own dancing favour the Far Brow (they choose for bees: a dry cavity, the right size, the
right aspect), and let the human dancers lean against or with it. The Humefolk believe they guide the swarm; they are
partly right. This turns the "vote" into something stranger: persuading a non-human mind that does not care about your
grandmother's legs.

## 5. Does the plot grow from the setting?

Mostly. The scent law produces Nell's crime, Corve's lure, the moth's horror, and the gate. The swarm produces the
Leaving, the Daughters, the Escort. This is the treatment's real strength. Protect it: nothing should arrive from
outside the law. In particular, **the moth must not be part of anybody's plan**. It is an animal doing what its kind
does. Not every mystery is the conspiracy.

## 6. Do characters have conflicting motives and credible inner lives?

- **Linnet and Eben** are a duty-versus-art romance out of stock. Make it a comedy of errors in a tragic key: each has
  secretly decided to give up their calling for the other. **Eben has decided to go** and abandon the Stair; **Linnet
  has decided to stay** and give her keeping to a seventeen-year-old. Neither has told the other. Left alone, they
  cross on the Apron on the morning of the Leaving, each sacrificing for nobody. The protagonist can tell one, both,
  or neither; can finish the Stair so that Eben's sacrifice is unnecessary; can talk Linnet out of a choice that
  would leave Pim alone with three princesses.
- **Corve** is the genial improving squire in debt, which is a costume. Make him a **lover of bees**: a younger son,
  an amateur naturalist who has corresponded with the Hum-keepers for twelve years, sent them drawings, and been
  refused the scent three times. He wants a hive the way Nell wanted her husband: to belong to something that will not
  have him. The debt is his father's. The flask is not greed; it is a theft committed for love, which is worse to
  oppose and better to write.
- **Kestrel** is good but static: give her a reversal. She has never been wrong in public; she is not wrong about the
  Far Brow; she is wrong about who can walk it. Her change comes from people (Hannet, Ivo), not a fact.
- **Ivo** is the treatment's best invention. Do not make him a mascot. His fear must be real and his charm a defence:
  he has never been asked for anything because nobody thinks he can give it.
- **Ursel Marl** is a cheat with one note. Give her a reason: the Marls' princess lost two generations running, and the
  Marls have been *the household that loses* for sixty years; Ursel's own mother died in the losing cell's wax. She is
  cheating to end a humiliation, and the price of her cheating is two princesses' lives.

## 7. Does anything feel like generic fantasy furniture?

The inn ("the Smoker's Rest") and its ex-soldier keeper. The smith. The "two fingers short" Wardmistress (the
collection already has many who are missing something). The "old woman who holds the old bargain" (Hannet and the
brocks) is exactly the device the survey asked to retire. **Fix the last**: the brock-bargain is not in an old woman's
memory; it is cut into the Dell itself, in the Brow's dance-marks, on the chalk, where the old Searchers left a
standing message for one another. It is read by anyone who has learned to read a dance.

## 8. Alternatives and consequences?

Many on paper. Ensure each difficult thing has at least three ways: the brocks (fight, smoke and flame, the bargain on
the chalk, or let Moss go first); the moth (fight in the dark, break custom with a light, set the Sisters on it with a
sting's alarm-scent, or walk away and let it drain the dead); the flask (steal, persuade Corve, persuade Nell to have
spoiled it, or let it work).

## 9. Tonal contrast?

There is warmth and there is dread. Missing is **joy**: a scene where things go right and people are glad. The founding
night must be it, whatever the site: the first comb drawn on a new wall, white as milk, and two hundred people too
tired to sing, singing.

## 10. Themes through action?

The treatment says "belonging" in its notes and nowhere in its scenes, which is right. But it risks the opposite
problem: every strand rhymes too neatly (Nell, Corve, the moth all counterfeit the scent). Rhyme is good; a
crossword is not. Let **one** of the counterfeits be uncomplicatedly monstrous (the moth) and leave the human ones
unresolved in feeling.

## 11. Structure: the fourth act walks off the page

The treatment's worst fault. Once the Mother leaves, everyone with a reason to be loved walks after her, and Act IV
happens in an emptied town with secondary characters. **Bees solve this.** A swarm leaves *just before* the first
princess emerges. So the Daughters wake **the night of the Leaving**. The protagonist cannot be in both places. Half
the hive goes; the other half wakes; the protagonist chooses which half to belong to that night, and the other half's
night happens without them, to be learned on return. The title means the protagonist too.

The Turning-out of the Drones is then the coda: the new queen flies the second day after; the Escort walks behind
her in yellow; and the evening after, by custom, they are turned out. Short, comic, and very sad.

## 12. Would I explore this even knowing nothing of the plot?

I would explore the Comb. I am not yet sure about the Wold; four sites each with one secret is a hub of dungeons. Give
the Wold ordinary country between the sites: a linden wood with wild comb high in the trees (honey-hunting), a moor
with a shepherd's bothy, the Toot with its standing stone, and the dance-marks on the chalk that turn up everywhere for
anyone who can read them.

## Summary of required revisions

1. Every site costs someone; the best outcome is made, not found.
2. The bees dance too, for bees' reasons.
3. Linnet and Eben cross: each secretly sacrificing for the other.
4. Corve is a lover of bees, not a squire in debt.
5. The Leavers' Market: the lowland's vultures, and a trade for the protagonist.
6. The brock-bargain is cut in the chalk in dance-marks, not held in an old woman's memory.
7. The moth is nobody's plan. Light is forbidden among the dead.
8. The Daughters wake the night of the Leaving; the protagonist chooses which half.
9. Cut the Teagues; join Duke to the Marls; mead becomes small comedy or nothing.
10. Joy at the founding.
11. The protagonist wants something: a founder's share, a cell in a new town, a scent for life.
