> >hume__smoke_street
You set out from the drove road at the Wold’s edge early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. In the Wold of Hume the limes are in flower, the queen-cells in the Comb were capped six days ago, and every household under the Brow is deciding who goes. The hum has dropped a note. Anyone in the Wold can hear it, if they know what it sounded like before. Smoke End is one street of turf-roofed houses and timber yards strung along a stream at the mouth of the gorge, and all of it smokes. Smudge-fires of damp bracken and dung burn in iron baskets at every door and at the street’s ends, and the smoke goes up the gorge on the draught, grey and sweetish, and makes your eyes run. People here walk with a hand over their mouths, out of habit. Somewhere a hammer. Up the gorge, through the smoke, a glow like a lamp behind a curtain. On the post of the Smoker’s Rest a notice is nailed up, in a square hand, in lowland letters: SEARCHERS WANTED. ANY WHO CAN WALK AND LOOK. PAID IN WAX. ASK FOR K. DUNN, THE DANCING HALL, UP THE COMB. And under it, smaller: GET SCENTED FIRST. Handcarts go up the gorge empty and come down loaded: chests, chairs, a cradle, a loom in pieces, all with that faint golden tackiness of things that have lived a long time in the Comb.

> >hume__apron
The gorge opens into a bowl under a lip of grey rock a quarter of a mile long, and hanging from the underside of the rock is a honeycomb the size of a town, with people going in and out of it, and bees as big as hounds. It is gold and amber and brown and in places nearly black; it hangs in great curved sheets, like curtains frozen in a wind, the oldest of them four hundred years old; and it is cut through with doors, and stairs, and windows. The light through the wax is the colour of beer. The hum is everywhere: in the walls of the gorge, in the ground, in your breastbone. Overhead the Sisters come and go in brown streams the width of a road, gold-and-black, furred like a good coat, with eyes like wet coal. They land on the comb’s lip and walk in. When one passes low the wind of her wings lifts your hair. The people on the Apron do not look up, the way people in a town do not look up at the weather. Stalls along the troughs sell honey-cake, comb in squares on vine leaves, bee-bread in brown loaves, mead by the horn, and a fried thing in paper cones that smells of nuts and butter, which the stallholder says is brood: the drone brood, a delicacy, fried in its own wax. Doll Rowntree, a stallholder by the troughs, with a griddle, a fly-whisk, and forearms like hams, is here.

> >hume__dancing_hall
The Dancing Hall is the strangest room you have ever stood in. Its floor is tilted like a roof, steep enough that you would slide if it were not tacky; and its walls are written all over with marks scored into the wax: lines and loops and figure-eights, thousands of them, overlapping, generations deep. Up at the top of the slope a round window lets in the sun, and the Searchers dance on the slope below it: a straight run, waggling, then a loop back round, then the run again. The angle of the run from straight up is the angle from the sun. How long the waggle lasts is how far. How hard is how good. The Searchers do not dance alone. On the upper slope, where the wax is darkest, Sisters dance too, the bees’ own scouts, huge and fast and silent, running their lines and turning; and where a Sister dances, the people watching go quiet and lean to read her. For the Far Brow, east over the moor, a long waggling run straight toward the window and a little left, a day’s distance: three dancing it. For the Chalk Dell, south-east: two. For the Gallowtree, a short run west, two hours: one. Here are Kestrel Dunn, the Wardmistress of the Searchers, lean and brown, two fingers short on her left hand; Hannet Coke, the oldest of the Searchers, small and brown and bent, sitting on the slope with her stick across her knees; and Moss Ashdown, a Searcher, nineteen, freckled, with a boar-spear he will not put down.

> Talk to Kestrel Dunn
A lean brown woman at the foot of the slope looks you up and down the way a farrier looks at a horse. ‘You came about the notice,’ she says. It is not a question. ‘Good. I’ve two hundred people to house by Midsummer and half my Searchers have decided to stay home and grow old. Can you walk? Can you look? Can you tell a dry hollow from a wet one? Then you’ll do.’

> Ask to be taken on as a Searcher
‘Taken on.’ Kestrel takes a strip of yellow ribbon out of her belt and ties it round your arm, hard, with her teeth and her good hand. ‘Elspet at the Scenting House will scent you for nothing while you wear that. You’re paid in wax, a cake for every place you go and look at and come back and tell me about, honestly. Four places worth the walk, this year.’ She counts on her short hand. ‘The Gallowtree: Hannet’s. Two hours west, on a knoll, big hollow lime, close, which is all Hannet cares about. The Chalk Dell: Moss’s. South-east. Dry, warm, a cave in the chalk, and the brocks live in it, which Moss thinks is a selling point. The Far Brow: mine. A day east, over the moor, and the most beautiful place in the Wold. And Larkhill.’ Her face shuts. ‘A lowland squire’s got a chapel on the Wold’s edge, and for a week the Sisters’ own scouts have been flying out to it and coming back excited, and I don’t know why, and I don’t like not knowing.’ Wren gains 25 experience: was taken on as a Searcher of Hume Brow.

> Ask Kestrel to teach you the dance
She stands you at the bottom of the slope with the sun coming in at the round window. ‘Straight up is the sun. Wherever it is. Run up the slope, that’s toward it; run off to the left of straight up, that’s left of the sun, by as much.’ She runs a line, waggling her hips, comically and then not comically at all: you see the moor in it. ‘Waggle as long as it is far. Waggle as hard as it is good. Loop back, do it again, till they’ve got it.’ She makes you do it, twenty times, thirty, until your legs know it and the Searchers sitting round the walls stop laughing. ‘You dance like a lowlander,’ she says at last. ‘But it reads. Don’t dance for anything you haven’t stood in.’ Wren gains 50 experience: learned the Searchers’ dance.

> Take your leave
‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Kestrel Dunn, who was not trying to.

> >hume__gallow_knoll
The Gallowtree stands alone on its knoll, a linden so old it has outlived its own name: nobody was ever hanged on it; the name is older than hanging. Its trunk is as big round as a cottage, grey and ridged and hollow, and its crown is still full and green and loud with wild bees. In the trunk, at the height of a man, someone long ago cut a door, and shaped its sill in wax, and the wax has gone grey.

> >hume__gallow_hollow
Inside the tree is a room, round, tall as a chapel, lit greenly from cracks high up. The walls are comb: old grey comb, built up the inside of the trunk in sheets, abandoned, dry; and there are people in it. > >hume__wax_gallery
The Waxwrights’ gallery is cooler, and smells of warm candles and of something sharp they use to keep the wax workable. Benches, moulds, knives with long thin blades, cakes of wax from almost white to almost black. And going up out of the middle of it, through a hole in the ceiling, round and round, the Lantern Stair: a spiral of carved wax so fine the light comes through every tread, each step carved with flowers, bees, faces, hands, the whole of the Brow’s life climbing up into the dark. It stops, three turns up, at nothing. The Lantern Stair climbs round and round toward the Crown and stops, three turns up, at nothing. Eben Candlish, a Waxwright: a long-faced man of forty with burnt fingertips and wax in his beard, who keeps looking up at the Stair, is here.

> Talk to Eben Candlish
A long-faced man at the foot of the Stair is warming a blade in a little brazier, and does not look round. ‘Don’t touch it,’ he says. ‘Everyone touches it.’ Then he looks round, and sees you are a stranger, and something in his face lets go. ‘Sorry. Nineteen years. You’d think they’d stop touching it.’ He looks up the spiral to where it ends in air. ‘One course,’ he says. ‘One turn. > Ask Eben about the Lantern Stair
‘Every Waxwright makes one thing that’s theirs. That’s mine.’ He follows it up with his eyes, turn by turn: flowers, Sisters, the faces of the Brow, children, hands. ‘My master started it. > >hume__mothers_chamber
The Crown of the Comb is a long dim hall under the very rock of the Brow, where the comb is oldest and nearly black. In the middle of it, on a wide shallow cell like a bed, is the Mother: as long as a pony, slender, the gold of her body gone dark with age, her wings short on her long back. The Sisters stand round her in a ring facing in, touching her, stroking her, feeding her from their mouths. She is quite still. The hum here is so deep it is not a sound at all but a pressure, like being underwater. Every little while the ring round the Mother breaks: a Sister pushes at her, butts her, runs up her back, and the Mother walks three steps, and the ring closes again. They are making her thin enough to fly. Here are Linnet Amory, the Hum-keeper: a young woman with a shaved head and bare arms crawling with Sisters, who does not seem to notice them; and Pim Garnock, the keeper’s novice: a gangling boy of seventeen with a newly shaved head, sunburnt where the hair was.

> Talk to Linnet Amory
A young woman sitting cross-legged beside the Mother looks up. Her head is shaved and her arms are bare and there are Sisters walking on them, on her shoulders, on her scalp, and she moves them aside with a finger as you might move a cat off a chair. ‘Shh,’ she says, not to you. Then, to you, low: ‘She’s resting. She has a long way to go and she’s never flown in her life, not since she was a girl.’ She looks at the Mother with an expression you have seen on people at a bedside. ‘I’m Linnet. I keep her. > Ask Linnet whether she is ready to go

> Tell Linnet that Eben means to go with the Leaving, and leave the Stair

The Dancing Hall is empty. The marks of this week’s dances are the newest on the walls, pale and sharp among the old ones. Time passes. It begins as a change in the hum: up, and up, a whole octave, until the gorge rings with it like a struck bell. Then out of every door and crack and window of the Comb at once, the Sisters pour. Not in streams: in a flood, a brown roaring flood, filling the air of the gorge from wall to wall, so many and so loud that the sun goes dim and you cannot hear yourself shout. They boil up over the lip of the Brow in a column a quarter of a mile wide. Somewhere in the middle of it, invisible, is the Mother. And down the foot stair, out of the doors, with bundles on their backs and veils on their heads and little bells tied on their wrists so as not to lose each other, come the people who are going: two hundred of them, walking fast, not looking back, while the people who are staying stand at the troughs and on the Apron and in the doorways and watch them go.

The gorge opens into a bowl under a lip of grey rock a quarter of a mile long, and hanging from the underside of the rock is a honeycomb the size of a town, with people going in and out of it, and bees as big as hounds. Doll Rowntree, a stallholder by the troughs, with a griddle, a fly-whisk, and forearms like hams, is here. The Apron is too quiet. The Sisters still come and go overhead, but thinly, a stream where there was a river; half the stalls are bare boards; and the people who are left stand about in twos and threes, not talking much, looking up at the Comb as if it were somebody’s house after a funeral.

> Walk with the Leaving, east over the moor (the hollow under the Far Brow)
You walk with them, all the long afternoon and into the evening, east over the moor behind a roaring cloud. The heather is waist-high and the bees in it are wild and small and indifferent. The line stretches and stretches; the old fall behind and the young go back for them; the little bells on everyone’s wrists go on, all down the moor, a mile of bells. Near sunset, ahead, you see the Far Brow catch the light; and the swarm, which has been going in long low loops all day, goes straight at it, like water finding a drain, and pours in under the rock. By the time the last of the Leaving comes in under the Far Brow it is nearly dark, and the swarm has already hung itself from the rock, a great brown beard of bees as long as a barn, roaring softly; and at its top, where it touches the stone, the Sisters have begun to build. You can see it if you stand close: a first finger of new comb, white as milk, the whitest thing in the world, growing while you watch. They are all too tired to stand. Someone lights a fire, which they never do at home. Somebody passes round a crock of honey and a knife. And then, for no reason, very badly, too tired to sing, two hundred people sing: the hum, the old note under everything, sitting on the warm dust under the Far Brow with the white comb growing over their heads; and Kestrel Dunn sits by the child-handprints at the back with her knees up and her face hidden. The old came by the shepherds’ drove, slow, with the bothy to rest in. Hannet Coke was carried in on the Searchers’ litter at dusk, by four of them and a man in a yellow coat, telling them all the way how to do it. Later, when the singing has worn itself out, a man in a yellow coat gone brown with walking goes to the back of the hollow and sits down by the handprints, a little way from Kestrel Dunn; and after a while she shifts over, without looking at him, and makes room. Late, by the fire, the keeper of the Leaving comes and kneels in front of you with the jar of the Mother’s own scent, and draws two fingers down your forehead, your throat, the insides of your wrists, behind your ears, saying the words under the breath that have the hum’s tune; not the guest’s anointing, but the other one, the one for a child. ‘You walked with us,’ is all that is said. A Sister on the rock above you turns her head toward you, and away, as if you had always been here. And they tell you, as if it were a small thing, what the Leaving has decided to call the new comb, in the Wold way, after the first Searcher who danced for it who was not born to it. It is your name. They say it oddly, with the hum in it. It will be on the maps of the Wold for four hundred years, said wrong. Wren and Ivo Gildersleeve each gain 200 experience: walked with the Leaving. Wren and Ivo Gildersleeve each gain 250 experience: was named to the hive. Under the Far Brow it is dry and still and warm. Here are Kestrel Dunn, the Wardmistress of the Searchers, a lean brown woman with cropped grey hair, walking at the head of the Leaving; Hannet Coke, the oldest of the Searchers, small and brown and bent, sitting on a fleece with her stick across her knees and two wax bees on a cord round her neck; the Drewes, a family of chandlers of the Comb going with the Mother: a mother, a father, two girls, standing by a table of fine candles; Linnet Amory, the Hum-keeper, with a shaved head, and grass-stains on her gown from the long walk; Eben Candlish, a Waxwright, with wax in his beard; Agnes Mellor, the Capper’s widow; and Huw Penry, the Comb’s mead-maker: a big man with a magnificent beard and a voice he uses to its full extent, among his barrels. Under the Far Brow a new comb hangs from the rock, a yard long already and growing, white at the edges and going gold at the heart, roaring softly. Along the back wall the Leaving has put up shelters of wattle and sailcloth, and somebody has scratched FAR BROW on a post, and somebody else has added NEW in front of it.

You are of the Comb now. The scent will not come off: you will smell, a little, of the Mother for the rest of your life, in the lowland, in the rain, in your grave; and any Sister anywhere in the Wold will turn her head toward you, and away, as if you were family. Linnet Amory keeps the old Mother in the new comb, and Eben Candlish is carving the doorframe of the first cell. In the Brow, the Lantern Stair goes up three turns and stops at nothing, and the children of the Waxwrights dare each other to climb to the last step and stand on it. Hannet Coke went with the Mother at last, carried, complaining the whole way, and sits now in the sun at the mouth of the new comb on a stool somebody made her, telling the children how it was done in the old Brow, mostly wrongly, with two wax bees on a cord round her neck. Agnes Mellor went with the Mother, and left Tobit capped in the Gallery with the others, safe, and has a view. Nothing comes into the Capped Gallery in the small hours now. The Cappers have taken down the board that says no flame among the Capped, and put it back up again, because it is still true. Once a month Nell Quarrie goes up the stair in the dark smelling of her own oil, and sits an hour with Aldo, and comes down, and nobody notices. At Larkhill the squire’s tower stands empty and dry and pointed, with an empty green flask in a crack at the top of it; and the squire sits in his study among twelve years of drawings and does not draw. Moss Ashdown can walk again, with a stick, and will be a Searcher still, they say, in a year. There is a song about him in the Brow after all; the children made it up. It is not the song he wanted. It is quite a good song. The Breck princess woke first and stung her sisters in their cells, and reigns, and the Brecks are the Mother’s household at last after a hundred years, and everyone says what a strong queen she is, and how lucky. Ivo Gildersleeve is with you, in a yellow coat gone brown with walking, and knows the way. Under the Far Brow the new comb hangs a yard long already, white going gold, and the old came by the shepherds’ drove and are there to see it. It is the best place in the Wold, as Kestrel always said; and this time everyone could walk it. There are child-handprints at the back in the old wax, and new ones beside them now, and one that a grown man says is his from when he was nine, and nobody knows whose the oldest are, or why the first comb here was ever left. Half the hive went. Half stayed. Both halves are alive.
