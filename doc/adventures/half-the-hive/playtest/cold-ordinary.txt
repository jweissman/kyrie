[To Smoke End] You set out from the drove road at the Wold’s edge early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. In the Wold of Hume the limes are in flower, the queen-cells in the Comb were capped six days ago, and every household under the Brow is deciding who goes. The hum has dropped a note. Anyone in the Wold can hear it, if they know what it sounded like before. Smoke End is one street of turf-roofed houses and timber yards strung along a stream at the mouth of the gorge, and all of it smokes. Smudge-fires of damp bracken and dung burn in iron baskets at every door and at the street’s ends, and the smoke goes up the gorge on the draught, grey and sweetish, and makes your eyes run. People here walk with a hand over their mouths, out of habit. Somewhere a hammer. Up the gorge, through the smoke, a glow like a lamp behind a curtain. On the post of the Smoker’s Rest a notice is nailed up, in a square hand, in lowland letters: SEARCHERS WANTED. ANY WHO CAN WALK AND LOOK. PAID IN WAX. ASK FOR K. DUNN, THE DANCING HALL, UP THE COMB. And under it, smaller: GET SCENTED FIRST. Handcarts go up the gorge empty and come down loaded: chests, chairs, a cradle, a loom in pieces, all with that faint golden tackiness of things that have lived a long time in the Comb.
[Into the Smoker's Rest] Martin Sayle, the keeper of the Smoker’s Rest: a grey, square, careful man who turns his head to listen with his left ear, is here.
[Talk to Martin Sayle] The man behind the board turns his head to bring his left ear round to you. ‘Speak up on that side. The right’s been no use since a Sister put a sting in it, twenty years back, the first week I came up here.’ He pours you something without asking. ‘Mead. Mine. It isn’t as good as Huw Penry’s up the Comb, and I’ll tell you why before you ask: he’s got the heather honey and I’ve got what he sells me. Bed’s two coin. Welcome to the Wold.’
[Ask Martin about the Wold] ‘Up the gorge is the Comb, and in the Comb are the Humefolk, and the Sisters, and the honey.’ He wipes the board. ‘They’re good people. Odd. They’ll sell to you all day at the foot of the stair, but they won’t have you up it unless you smell right; Elspet at the Scenting House’ll do you for two coin, a day’s worth. Don’t let it run out up there.’ He touches his deaf ear. ‘And this week they’re splitting the town in half and walking off with the old queen, so mind where you stand.’
[Take your leave] Martin Sayle lifts a hand, once.
[Out to Smoke End] Smoke End is one street of turf-roofed houses and timber yards strung along a stream at the mouth of the gorge, and all of it smokes.
[To the gorge path] The gorge closes in: grey limestone walls going up and up, ferns in every crack, a stream loud beside the path. The smoke thins behind you and something else takes its place: warmth, out of all proportion to the day, and a smell like a summer field baked and then poured into a jar, and a sound you feel first in your teeth.
[To the Apron] The gorge opens into a bowl under a lip of grey rock a quarter of a mile long, and hanging from the underside of the rock is a honeycomb the size of a town, with people going in and out of it, and bees as big as hounds. It is gold and amber and brown and in places nearly black; it hangs in great curved sheets, like curtains frozen in a wind, the oldest of them four hundred years old; and it is cut through with doors, and stairs, and windows. The light through the wax is the colour of beer. The hum is everywhere: in the walls of the gorge, in the ground, in your breastbone. Overhead the Sisters come and go in brown streams the width of a road, gold-and-black, furred like a good coat, with eyes like wet coal. They land on the comb’s lip and walk in. When one passes low the wind of her wings lifts your hair. The people on the Apron do not look up, the way people in a town do not look up at the weather. Stalls along the troughs sell honey-cake, comb in squares on vine leaves, bee-bread in brown loaves, mead by the horn, and a fried thing in paper cones that smells of nuts and butter, which the stallholder says is brood: the drone brood, a delicacy, fried in its own wax. Doll Rowntree, a stallholder by the troughs, with a griddle, a fly-whisk, and forearms like hams, is here.
[Talk to Doll Rowntree] ‘Honey-cake, bee-bread, comb on a leaf, mead by the horn,’ says the woman at the griddle, ‘and brood, fried, hot, the best in the Brow, a coin the cone. Go on. The Escort eat it every night and they’re the handsomest men in the Wold.’ She laughs until she has to hold on to the trough.
[Ask Doll Rowntree who is going] ‘Me? Staying. I’ve a griddle that weighs more than me and the Mother’s got no use for brood fried or otherwise.’ She fans the coals. ‘My sister’s going. My boy’s going. Searcher, he is, like his dad was.’ She fans harder. ‘He’s twenty-two. You can’t keep them.’ She does not look up for a while.
[Take your leave] ‘Off you go, then,’ says Doll Rowntree, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’
[Into the Scenting House] A low room cut into the rock beside the foot stair, very clean. Shelves of stoppered jars, a stone basin, a brazier with something resinous on it. A slate on the wall says in chalk: GUESTS SCENTED, ONE DAY. TWO COIN. SEARCHERS NOTHING. Elspet Garnock, the Scent-warden: a small exact woman with a stained apron and very clean hands, who smells overwhelmingly of the Comb, is here.
[Talk to Elspet Garnock] A small woman at the stone basin looks at you over her spectacles, and then, deliberately, leans close and smells you: hair, throat, hands. ‘Lowland,’ she says. ‘Woodsmoke, sheep, somebody else’s soap. Nothing of ours.’ She wipes her hands. ‘Two coin and I’ll make you smell like a cousin for a day. The Sisters won’t know you from a Hume child. They’ll know you the day after, so come back.’
[Ask Elspet what the scent is] ‘The Mother.’ She holds up the jar. ‘Every queen has her own smell, and every Sister born to her knows it, and knows that whatever smells of it is the hive, and whatever does not is a robber. So we take a little of what the Mother leaves on the comb where she walks, and we cut it with wax and oil, and we put it on our children the day they are born, and on ourselves at every feast. That is what it is to be of the Comb.’ She stoppers the jar. ‘On you it lasts a day. On us it never quite comes off.’
[Ask Elspet to scent you for a day, two coin] She dips two fingers in a jar of something gold and thick, and draws them down your forehead, your throat, the insides of your wrists, behind your ears, saying something under her breath that has the hum’s tune and no words. It is warm, and then it is nothing; and then, as you go out, a Sister on the doorframe turns her head toward you, and away, uninterested, as if you were family.
[Take your leave] Elspet Garnock lifts a hand, once.
[Out to the Apron] The gorge opens into a bowl under a lip of grey rock a quarter of a mile long, and hanging from the underside of the rock is a honeycomb the size of a town, with people going in and out of it, and bees as big as hounds. Doll Rowntree, a stallholder by the troughs, with a griddle, a fly-whisk, and forearms like hams, is here.
[Into the Comb] Inside, the Comb is warm as a kitchen and gold as the inside of an egg. The gallery is a corridor of six-sided rooms, each as big as a cottage, their walls thin enough to see shapes through: bees, people, a cradle. The floor gives very slightly underfoot and is tacky, like a well-polished table on a hot day, and everyone here goes barefoot or in felt. The hum is so close now that you hear words in it if you let yourself.
[To the Dancing Hall] The Dancing Hall is the strangest room you have ever stood in. Its floor is tilted like a roof, steep enough that you would slide if it were not tacky; and its walls are written all over with marks scored into the wax: lines and loops and figure-eights, thousands of them, overlapping, generations deep. Up at the top of the slope a round window lets in the sun, and the Searchers dance on the slope below it: a straight run, waggling, then a loop back round, then the run again. The angle of the run from straight up is the angle from the sun. How long the waggle lasts is how far. How hard is how good. The Searchers do not dance alone. On the upper slope, where the wax is darkest, Sisters dance too, the bees’ own scouts, huge and fast and silent, running their lines and turning; and where a Sister dances, the people watching go quiet and lean to read her. For the Far Brow, east over the moor, a long waggling run straight toward the window and a little left, a day’s distance: three dancing it. For the Chalk Dell, south-east: two. For the Gallowtree, a short run west, two hours: one. Here are Kestrel Dunn, the Wardmistress of the Searchers, lean and brown, two fingers short on her left hand; Hannet Coke, the oldest of the Searchers, small and brown and bent, sitting on the slope with her stick across her knees; and Moss Ashdown, a Searcher, nineteen, freckled, with a boar-spear he will not put down.
[Talk to Kestrel Dunn] A lean brown woman at the foot of the slope looks you up and down the way a farrier looks at a horse. ‘You came about the notice,’ she says. It is not a question. ‘Good. I’ve two hundred people to house by Midsummer and half my Searchers have decided to stay home and grow old. Can you walk? Can you look? Can you tell a dry hollow from a wet one? Then you’ll do.’
[Ask to be taken on as a Searcher] ‘Taken on.’ Kestrel takes a strip of yellow ribbon out of her belt and ties it round your arm, hard, with her teeth and her good hand. ‘Elspet at the Scenting House will scent you for nothing while you wear that. You’re paid in wax, a cake for every place you go and look at and come back and tell me about, honestly. Four places worth the walk, this year.’ She counts on her short hand. ‘The Gallowtree: Hannet’s. Two hours west, on a knoll, big hollow lime, close, which is all Hannet cares about. The Chalk Dell: Moss’s. South-east. Dry, warm, a cave in the chalk, and the brocks live in it, which Moss thinks is a selling point. The Far Brow: mine. A day east, over the moor, and the most beautiful place in the Wold. And Larkhill.’ Her face shuts. ‘A lowland squire’s got a chapel on the Wold’s edge, and for a week the Sisters’ own scouts have been flying out to it and coming back excited, and I don’t know why, and I don’t like not knowing.’ Wren gains 25 experience: was taken on as a Searcher of Hume Brow.
[Ask Kestrel why the Far Brow] ‘Because it’s the best place in the Wold, and because the Sisters think so too: look at them.’ She nods up the slope at the bees’ dancers. ‘My gran said the Brow’s founders came from there. I’ve danced for it at every Leaving since I was fourteen, and every time the Mother’s gone somewhere nearer, and every time I’ve been right and they’ve been wrong. Ketter’s damp. The Nine Holes have the moth. Sallowcomb starves every third winter.’ She folds her arms. ‘This time the bees are with me.’ Then, quieter, almost to herself: ‘It’s a long walk. I know it’s a long walk.’
[Take your leave] ‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Kestrel Dunn, who was not trying to.
[Talk to Hannet Coke] A tiny old woman sitting on the slope pats the wax beside her. ‘Sit, sit, you’ll slide. You’re Kestrel’s new legs? Good. Mine are seventy and they’ve struck. Two hours and then they sit down wherever they are and won’t get up for anybody.’ She laughs, a startling young laugh.
[Ask Hannet why she dances for the Gallowtree] ‘Because I can walk there.’ She says it simply. ‘I’ve stayed six Leavings. Six. My mother stayed, and I stayed with her, and then my man stayed, and I stayed with him, and then I was too old for anyone to take me, and now they’re all capped upstairs and I can go where I please, and I please to go with the Mother.’ She taps her knee. ‘Two hours. The Gallowtree’s two hours. It’s a big old hollow lime and the Sisters could fill it in a summer. Kestrel’s Far Brow, now, that’s a day across the moor and I’d be capped in the heather by teatime.’
[Take your leave] ‘Come back and tell me how it goes!’ calls Hannet Coke after you.
[To the Brood gallery] Inside, the Comb is warm as a kitchen and gold as the inside of an egg.
[To the Waxwrights' gallery] The Waxwrights’ gallery is cooler, and smells of warm candles and of something sharp they use to keep the wax workable. Benches, moulds, knives with long thin blades, cakes of wax from almost white to almost black. And going up out of the middle of it, through a hole in the ceiling, round and round, the Lantern Stair: a spiral of carved wax so fine the light comes through every tread, each step carved with flowers, bees, faces, hands, the whole of the Brow’s life climbing up into the dark. It stops, three turns up, at nothing. The Lantern Stair climbs round and round toward the Crown and stops, three turns up, at nothing. Eben Candlish, a Waxwright: a long-faced man of forty with burnt fingertips and wax in his beard, who keeps looking up at the Stair, is here.
[Talk to Eben Candlish] A long-faced man at the foot of the Stair is warming a blade in a little brazier, and does not look round. ‘Don’t touch it,’ he says. ‘Everyone touches it.’ Then he looks round, and sees you are a stranger, and something in his face lets go. ‘Sorry. Nineteen years. You’d think they’d stop touching it.’ He looks up the spiral to where it ends in air. ‘One course,’ he says. ‘One turn. And then it goes all the way to the Crown and the sun comes down it at midsummer like water down a drain.’
[Ask Eben about the Lantern Stair] ‘Every Waxwright makes one thing that’s theirs. That’s mine.’ He follows it up with his eyes, turn by turn: flowers, Sisters, the faces of the Brow, children, hands. ‘My master started it. I took it on at twenty-one. Every tread is somebody: that’s Kestrel at fourteen, that’s old Penry’s first barrel, that’s Linnet the day she was made keeper.’ He stops. ‘The last course wants white wax. New comb, never brooded. The Sisters don’t draw it white any more, not in a hive this old; the only white comb left in the Wold is in places nobody goes.’ He shrugs, too hard. ‘It doesn’t matter now.’
[Take your leave] Eben Candlish has gone back to work before you have turned round.
[To the Brood gallery] Inside, the Comb is warm as a kitchen and gold as the inside of an egg.
[Out to the Apron] The gorge opens into a bowl under a lip of grey rock a quarter of a mile long, and hanging from the underside of the rock is a honeycomb the size of a town, with people going in and out of it, and bees as big as hounds. Doll Rowntree, a stallholder by the troughs, with a griddle, a fly-whisk, and forearms like hams, is here.
[To the gorge path] The gorge closes in: grey limestone walls going up and up, ferns in every crack, a stream loud beside the path.
[To the linden wood] You leave Hume Brow in the full of the morning, with the sun climbing and the path dry underfoot. The linden wood is tall and cool and full of the noise of bees: the limes are in flower, and the Sisters work the high branches, huge shapes in the green, and the scent of the flowers comes down so thick it is almost a taste. Paths go off every way: north to the moor, west to a knoll with one great tree on it, south-east downhill toward chalk, west along the edge toward Larkhill.
[To the Gallowtree's knoll] The Gallowtree stands alone on its knoll, a linden so old it has outlived its own name: nobody was ever hanged on it; the name is older than hanging. Its trunk is as big round as a cottage, grey and ridged and hollow, and its crown is still full and green and loud with wild bees. In the trunk, at the height of a man, someone long ago cut a door, and shaped its sill in wax, and the wax has gone grey.
[Into the hollow of the Gallowtree] Inside the tree is a room, round, tall as a chapel, lit greenly from cracks high up. The walls are comb: old grey comb, built up the inside of the trunk in sheets, abandoned, dry; and there are people in it. In the grey comb of the walls, upright, capped in wax gone the colour of old bone: men, women, a few children, in clothes eighty years out of fashion, eyes shut. Some have their hands folded. Some do not. One woman holds a baby. By the door, on the floor, against the wall, sits the one who capped the others: uncapped, because there was nobody left to do it. Bones in a brown Capper’s apron, a sealing-iron by one hand. In the lap, something small. At the back of the hollow the inner wood of the tree goes up into the dark, ridged and grey, wet-looking. High up in the hollow, above the capped, where the dry top of the trunk catches the light, a few sheets of comb hang that are not grey but white, white as milk: the first comb the seventh Leaving’s bees drew, before anything was born in it.
[Examine — Look closely] You look closely at the capped of the seventh Leaving. You go round the walls slowly. Forty-one of them. The capping gets worse as you go: the first are smooth and clear and careful, the work of a Capper with time; then rougher; then thin, hurried, cracked, as if done by someone who was cold, or ill, or alone. The last few are hardly capped at all.
[Examine — Look closely] You look closely at the one by the door. Wren gains 100 experience: found what became of the seventh Leaving. In the lap of the bones, where the hands were, is a little bee carved in wax, no bigger than a thumb, very carefully done, every leg; and cut into the iron by the hand, small and square: M. COKE.
[Take the little wax bee from the lap of the one by the door] The bones do not hold it. It comes away into your hand, warm from the green light, lighter than it looks. You take a little bee carved in wax.
[Out to the Gallowtree's knoll] The Gallowtree stands alone on its knoll, a linden so old it has outlived its own name: nobody was ever hanged on it; the name is older than hanging.
[To the linden wood] You set out from the Gallowtree’s knoll. The linden wood is tall and cool and full of the noise of bees: the limes are in flower, and the Sisters work the high branches, huge shapes in the green, and the scent of the flowers comes down so thick it is almost a taste.

Doll Rowntree, a stallholder by the troughs, with a griddle, a fly-whisk, and forearms like hams, is here.
[Into the Comb] Inside, the Comb is warm as a kitchen and gold as the inside of an egg.
[To the Dancing Hall] The Dancing Hall is the strangest room you have ever stood in. Here are Kestrel Dunn, the Wardmistress of the Searchers, lean and brown, two fingers short on her left hand; Hannet Coke, the oldest of the Searchers, small and brown and bent, sitting on the slope with her stick across her knees; and Moss Ashdown, a Searcher, nineteen, freckled, with a boar-spear he will not put down.
[Talk to Hannet Coke] Hannet pats the wax beside her. ‘Sit.’
[Give Hannet the little wax bee from the Gallowtree] Hannet takes it in her palm and does not say anything. Then she reaches inside her collar and pulls out a cord, and on the cord, worn smooth and dark with seventy years of being held, is another bee, its twin, every leg. She holds them side by side. ‘Merrit,’ she says. ‘My aunt Merrit. She went with the seventh. She gave me this one when I was four and said she’d send the other when they’d built, and that would be the sign.’ She looks at you. ‘Where was it?’ You tell her: the tree, the grey comb, the people standing in it; the one by the door, uncapped, who capped all the others and had nobody to do it for her. Hannet closes her hand on both bees. ‘Well,’ she says. ‘She sent it.’ After a while she gets up, very slowly, with her stick, and goes and sits by the wall, and does not dance any more. Wren gains 150 experience: brought Merrit Coke’s wax bee home to her niece.
[Take your leave] ‘Come back and tell me how it goes!’ calls Hannet Coke after you.
