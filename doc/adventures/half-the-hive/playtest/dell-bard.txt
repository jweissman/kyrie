You set out from the drove road at the Wold’s edge early, with your shadow long in front of you on the road. In the Wold of Hume the limes are in flower, the queen-cells in the Comb were capped six days ago, and every household under the Brow is deciding who goes. The hum has dropped a note. Anyone in the Wold can hear it, if they know what it sounded like before. Smoke End is one street of turf-roofed houses and timber yards strung along a stream at the mouth of the gorge, and all of it smokes. Smudge-fires of damp bracken and dung burn in iron baskets at every door and at the street’s ends, and the smoke goes up the gorge on the draught, grey and sweetish, and makes your eyes run. People here walk with a hand over their mouths, out of habit. Somewhere a hammer. Up the gorge, through the smoke, a glow like a lamp behind a curtain. On the post of the Smoker’s Rest a notice is nailed up, in a square hand, in lowland letters: SEARCHERS WANTED. ANY WHO CAN WALK AND LOOK. PAID IN WAX. ASK FOR K. DUNN, THE DANCING HALL, UP THE COMB. And under it, smaller: GET SCENTED FIRST. Handcarts go up the gorge empty and come down loaded: chests, chairs, a cradle, a loom in pieces, all with that faint golden tackiness of things that have lived a long time in the Comb.

> >hume__dancing_hall
The Dancing Hall is the strangest room you have ever stood in. Its floor is tilted like a roof, steep enough that you would slide if it were not tacky; and its walls are written all over with marks scored into the wax: lines and loops and figure-eights, thousands of them, overlapping, generations deep. Up at the top of the slope a round window lets in the sun, and the Searchers dance on the slope below it: a straight run, waggling, then a loop back round, then the run again. The angle of the run from straight up is the angle from the sun. How long the waggle lasts is how far. How hard is how good. The Searchers do not dance alone. On the upper slope, where the wax is darkest, Sisters dance too, the bees’ own scouts, huge and fast and silent, running their lines and turning; and where a Sister dances, the people watching go quiet and lean to read her. For the Far Brow, east over the moor, a long waggling run straight toward the window and a little left, a day’s distance: three dancing it. For the Chalk Dell, south-east: two. At two the new queen comes along the ceiling of the royal gallery: long, gold, new, quick as a cat. She goes to each cell in turn, and at each she walks round and round the cage, piping, frantic, and cannot get in; and inside, the quacking goes on, frightened and alive. The two watchers sit under their cages with their hands clenched in their laps and do not look at Dame Brenna. In the grey of the morning the keeper who stayed comes along the gallery with the jar, and stops in front of you, and dips two fingers in it, and draws them down your forehead, your throat, your wrists, behind your ears, saying the words. ‘You stayed with us,’ is all that is said. ‘Half of us went. You stayed.’ Tamsin gains 250 experience: was named to the hive.

You are of the Comb now. The scent will not come off: you will smell, a little, of the Mother for the rest of your life, in the lowland, in the rain, in your grave; and any Sister anywhere in the Wold will turn her head toward you, and away, as if you were family. Linnet Amory and Eben Candlish stayed, both of them, and are to be married at the Feast of the Lime, and Eben is finishing the Stair, slowly, now there is no hurry; and somewhere across the Wold a boy of seventeen is keeping the old Mother in a new comb, and writing back every week to ask how to do it. Hannet Coke went with the Mother at last, carried, complaining the whole way, and sits now in the sun at the mouth of the new comb on a stool somebody made her, telling the children how it was done in the old Brow, mostly wrongly, with two wax bees on a cord round her neck. Agnes Mellor stayed. She sits in the Capped Gallery every night in the dark with Tobit, on her stool, talking about the goat. Something still comes into the Capped Gallery in the small hours, soft and dry, smelling of the Mother, and drinks from the seals of the dead; and the children of the Brow have a name for it, and a rhyme, and will not go up the honey stair after dark. Once a month Nell Quarrie goes up the stair in the dark smelling of her own oil, and sits an hour with Aldo, and comes down, and nobody notices. At Larkhill the squire’s tower stands empty and dry and pointed, with an empty green flask in a crack at the top of it; and the squire sits in his study among twelve years of drawings and does not draw. The Breck princess reigns in the Brow; and the princess who was caged went out with a cast the next noon, south, with forty people behind her, toward somewhere nobody danced for; and no word has come back yet. The Comb has never had two Leavings in one summer. Some say it is against nature. Some say Garnock would have been proud. Ivo Gildersleeve was turned out with the Escort, with a cloak and a loaf, and is sitting on a milestone at the edge of the Wold looking down the drove road at the lowland, which he has never seen, deciding. In the Chalk Dell the new comb fills the cave in the chalk, and the brocks sleep in a smaller hole at the back and are given the old comb at the turn of the year, as the marks say, and keep the peace. Moss Ashdown is cutting new marks under the old ones, for whoever comes next, so that this time somebody reads them.
