# A Voice for Hexworth: the story, before the Kyr

*Pass 1. Scenes written as fiction first. Dialogue here is draft; the playable lines in `data/adventures/hexworth` are
the later, tighter versions.*

## The premise

Hexworth stands on the lip of the Sour Edge, a peat moor that swallows a cart a year in fog. For forty years its bell,
Long Margery, has been rung at dusk so that the cutters coming off the moor can walk toward it. On Tuesday night she
cracked from lip to waist with nobody near her. The cutters' last haul comes in on the fourth evening, and the fog
comes with it.

A bell is made from what the town gives it. Gundred Ludlam, who cast Margery, is seventy-one, hears nothing above a
hum, and has begun to shake. Her apprentice Piers is fifteen and has never been allowed to pour. Everybody in town
wants to put something in the pot, and some of what they want to put in will ruin it.

Nobody is the villain. There is no theft, no conspiracy, and no ledger. There is a pot, a deadline of cooling metal,
and a bell that may be remembering the wrong thing.

## Scene: the heap

The square, first morning. Under the empty frame somebody has made a heap: horseshoes, a ploughshare, three iron
pots, a gate hinge. Dilly Drave, eight, is sitting on it eating a turnip.

"It's for the bell," she says. "Everybody brought. Mam brought the big pot and Dad said not that one, and Mam said
it's only a pot, and Dad said it's only a bell."

Gundred, at the foundry gate, does not look at the heap. "Nothing that rusts," she says. "I have said it in the
square, and at the chapel, and to Ebba Orlop twice, and the iron comes anyway. They want to give. It does not occur to
them that giving is a skill."

## Scene: what the founder hears

Gundred cannot hear a spoon on a plate. She puts her forehead against the cold bronze and listens with her skull, the
hum, the low note under the note. "The hum is the bell's whole character," she says. "The rest is manners. I can tell
you what a bell will be by what it hums, and I cannot tell you if the tin is any good, because tin cries high, and I
have not heard a cry in nine years. So you will bend it by my ear, and you will tell me."

She holds out a tin spoon, and you bend it, and it crackles in the ear like a small frost. "Again," she says. "I like
to watch your face while it does it."

## Scene: the seam in Margery

Margery lies on her side in the foundry yard like a drunk. Broken up with a sledge she shows her fracture, and along the
crack's whole length, at the start of it, one bright knuckle of something that is not bronze. It is the size of a
thumb. It is the shape, if you turn it, of a key.

Piers, behind you: "She comes down at night and puts her hand on the crack. I thought she was sorry for the bell."

Orlop, over the counter, delighted to be asked: "Edric Ludlam went out after his cows one October dusk when there was
no bell, only a horn, and the Edge had him. They found him at Easter. She cast Margery that winter. Nobody saw her do
it. I will say she did it very fast."

## Scene: what Gundred says when she is told

"It was his key," says Gundred. She says it to the pit, not to you. "The key to the byre. It was in his coat. I thought
it should be in her, to call him. You will say a bell does not work like that." She turns the thumb-sized knuckle over
and over. "It has called thirty-eight winters of cutters home and not him. I do not know what I expected."

She does not weep. She holds out the key and asks you to take it away, and then, when you reach, closes her hand.

## Scene: the pour

Midnight, the third night. The crucible is the colour of an October moon. Whoever pours has nine breaths between the
colour being right and the colour being too late. Gundred, who has poured four hundred and six bells, cannot keep
the ladle level for more than three.

"Piers," she says, and stops. "Put your hands on the shank. Not there. There. Now I am only the one who says when."

## Scene: the ringing

The fog came in at the fourth hour after noon, as it does, flat and white at the height of a wall. At the Stile,
six miles out, eleven men and a boy stand with their lanterns shut, because in the fog a lantern is only a bigger
fog. They are waiting for the thing they have always waited for, and it does not come.

Then it does: a note that is not Margery's. It is lower. It is as sweet as a kettle on a hearth, it has a little bright
cry high up in it that was a child's tin soldier, and under everything, so faint that only a bell-founder could say it
was there, the breath of a boy's whistle.

"That is not her," says Hasket. "No," says Gundred, who cannot hear it, and has her forehead on the frame. "That is
better. Tell me what it is saying."
