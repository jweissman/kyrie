*(The first page)*

The road comes up out of the march between snow-fields and stops being a road and starts being a fell road at a cross of grey stone, man-high, with a ledge round its foot worn hollow by knees. Someone has carved on its shaft, very neatly: FOR THE FAULTS OF ANOTHER. A low stone house stands beside it with smoke coming out of it, and on the step of the Cross a barefoot old man is mending a sandal.

**Talk to Beadle Clem Sutcliffe**

The man in the beadle’s gown does not get up. ‘You’ll be going north,’ he says. ‘Everybody’s going north. It’s the Fast.’ He looks at his foot, which is the colour of a candle. ‘I’m not. I’ve a commission for whoever is.’

**Ask the beadle what the commission is**

‘Him,’ says the beadle, and nods at the old man on the step. ‘Ninian Hern. Barefoot Hern, they call him in the Row. The Consistory sits on him on Ash Day, for heresy, and I was sent to fetch him in, and I’ve fetched him this far, and here’s my foot.’ He takes it out of the hat and looks at it and puts it back. ‘He’s not chained. He’d go by himself, he says. But the law says he’s to be brought, and brought means somebody brings him, and the somebody’s paid. Two shillings now. Eight at the Fell Gate, from the Assessor, when you hand him over.’ He sniffs. ‘It’s three days’ walk for a beadle. It’s one for anybody with two feet.’

**Take Ninian Hern into your custody, for the beadle's two shillings now and eight at the Fell Gate**

The beadle counts two shillings into your palm out of a purse that is mostly string. ‘He’s yours,’ he says. ‘Eight more at the Fell Gate. Don’t lose him. And don’t listen to him: he’s very persuasive and he’s always wrong.’ Hern has finished not-mending his sandal. He throws it into the snow, stands up on his bare feet, and shakes your hand, solemnly, as if a bargain had been struck. ‘My custodian,’ he says. ‘I’ve never had one. I’ve been one, often. It’s very restful, being had.’ Hern picks up his stick and falls in at your shoulder, barefoot, as if he had been waiting all morning to be asked. **Into the vigil-house at the Cross**

The vigil-house: one room, a good fire, a pair of snowshoes on the wall, a long pole shod with iron, a lantern with a horn shutter, and a book open on the table with a column of farm-names down one side and against each, for each night, a small neat C. Here are Ninian Hern, a barefoot old man in a frieze coat, ignoring a cough; and Raistrick, the Ash-reeve, a long quiet man in a sheepskin, the Penitentiary’s warden of vigils.

**Talk to Raistrick**

The man at the table looks up from his book, and closes it on his finger, and waits. He is perfectly polite. ‘Raistrick,’ he says. ‘I keep the vigils on this side of the Levels.’

**Ask Raistrick what he keeps**

‘Vigils,’ says Raistrick. ‘Cold vigils. A house that takes one keeps no fire, for so many nights; I see that it does.’ He holds up his right hand, and turns it over, as if it were a tool he were showing you. ‘Once a night, at an hour I choose, I go in, and I put my hand in the ash of the hearth. Cold, it is kept. Warm, it is broken.’ He lowers the hand. ‘I don’t knock. A vigil isn’t a thing you’re told about before.’

**Ask Raistrick whose vigils they are**

‘The Penitentiary’s.’ He opens his book again, and finds his place. ‘Bought, of the Penitentiary, by those who owe; kept, for wages, by those who need them. I keep the count.’ He makes a small neat C against a name. ‘I report each morning at ten to the one who pays, at his own yard, so he knows his nights are being kept. That’s his right.’ He looks up. ‘I’m not to say who. You’ll know the yard by the billet over the gate.’

**To the Proxy's Stone**

**Tell him: yes. That is what love is**

**Into the house at Carth**

THE FIRST NIGHT. The door shuts behind you, and the dark is complete. Not the dark of a night outdoors, which has a sky in it; the dark of a shut room with no fire, which has nothing. Somebody takes your arm, a hard small hand, and puts you down on the floor against a wall, which is stone, and cold through your clothes in a moment. You hear: a child coughing, close; someone breathing slow by where the hearth must be; a clock; the cow, through the wall; a dog, which comes and presses itself against your legs. And the cold. **Talk to Annis Scaife**

‘You’ll be the ones the snow brought,’ says a woman’s voice. ‘There’s oatmeal in water and there’s a turnip, and you’re welcome to your share of both, and of the dark. Sit on the floor by the wall. Don’t touch the hearth.’

**Ask Annis Scaife why there is no fire**

‘Because we’re keeping a vigil,’ says Annis. ‘A cold one. Forty nights. For somebody in Gaunt that’s done something, and is sorry, and paid the Penitentiary to have it kept.’ A pause. ‘Two shillings a night. Half in advance. It’s the rent. We’re two years behind with the Fabric and they’d have had us out at Lady Day.’ Another pause, longer. ‘It’s the twenty-third. Seventeen to go. Ralph died on the fourteenth. He’s in the byre. The ground won’t take a spade.’ She says it all in the same voice, the voice of someone reading out a list.

**Ask Annis who paid them**

‘The Penitentiary,’ says Annis. ‘That’s who pays. That’s who always pays. Whoever owes pays the Penitentiary, and it pays you.’ A box is opened, and something put into your hand: a shilling, cold, with a mark struck on its face that you can feel with your thumb: a little raised oblong, like a cut log. ‘It never came out of the Penitentiary’s chest,’ she says. ‘All twenty of them have that mark. Somebody’s own shillings, marked for their own counting-house.’ She takes the coin back. ‘I’m not a fool. I know whose mark that is. **Ask Annis whether she knows whose sorrow she is keeping**

Annis is quiet so long you think she has gone to sleep. Then: ‘My sister Ellen went down to the Row at Martinmas twelvemonth, to proxy for the winter. She was good at it. She could kneel all night on the Steps and get up laughing.’ Somewhere the rocking-chair creaks. ‘She was in the frost. They’ve a peg for her in the Barefoot Hall. The thirtieth. I walked down and looked at it.’ Another silence. ‘So when the Penitentiary’s clerk came up the fell with forty nights for sale, and a bag of shillings with that mark on them, I knew whose they were, and what they were for.’ Her voice does not change at all. ‘And I took them. Two shillings a night to sit in the cold for the man that froze her. Will said I was mad. It’s the rent, I said.’ You hear her turn over. ‘It’s not the rent. I want him forgiven by somebody who knows what it cost. A night at a time. I’ve not decided yet whether I’ll finish.’

Through the door behind the scales you see the Ash-reeve, Raistrick, snow on his sheepskin, standing in front of Alderman Thackray’s desk, saying one word, which you do not hear; and the big man nodding, and nodding, and putting his face in his hands. An hour passes.

**Wait › An hour**

At noon the Consistory sits in the Chapter House on the matter of Ninian Hern, deceased, who is present in a deal coffin on trestles on his square of stone, because the canons are clear that death does not discharge a penance, only interrupts it. The charge is read. The Determination is read. The Lady Penitentiary asks the coffin whether it recants, and waits the proper time, and pronounces it put out, in a voice like a door closing. At dusk four serjeants carry it through the Gate of Ash and set it down in the snow outside, on the Levels, and come back in, and the gate is shut and sealed.

**Wait › An hour**

Your tally is still notched. In the Register of Gaunt you owe what you owe, and it will be there when you come back, doubled. The Carth vigil goes on: night twenty-six tonight, fourteen to go. Raistrick will come at three and kneel and put his hand in the ash, and say ‘Kept’, and go. At the thaw the Scaifes will be paid, and pay the Fabric, and be square, and it will be spring. Dowsie Scaife is up and about in her mother’s shawl, with a cough she is proud of, telling the hens about the stone man in the Minster, who she has never seen. On the twenty-sixth morning of the vigil Old Mother Scaife did not come in from the byre. She had gone through to Ralph in the night, as she did every night, and lain down under the sheet with him to keep him warm; and this time nobody brought her back. There are two hurdles in the byre at Carth now. Alderman Thackray has his name re-gilded at the top of the Ladder, and rides up the fell road some mornings on a grey cob to look at a chimney, and weeps, and rides back, and the price of oak is thirty-one shillings the cord. Abel Lumb fasts for Mistress Pickersgill’s thoughts, eleven a week, and she goes down on Mondays to watch him not eat; and neither of them has said anything to anyone. Under the top of the Ladder, DINSDALE is gilded second and SOWERBY third, or the other way about; the city has stopped being able to tell. On the north tower Joby Thwaite’s masons set a course of white stone today, and tonight the frost will get into it. In the Row the Barefoot sit in their doorways with their feet in bowls, and the stove in the Hall is not lit. Hal Metcalfe never came to Gaol Lane with his ladder; and Prudence Garth never had to tell.

**Wait › An hour**

Ninian Hern was put out of Gaunt at dusk on Ash Day in a deal coffin, through the Gate of Ash, and left on the Levels in the snow, struck from the Register. In the night someone came out from the Row with a sledge and took him away, and nobody in Gaunt will say where he is buried, though the children on the ditch know, and sing about it, and get it wrong.
