The Barefoot Hall: a long room with benches, a board on the wall of the day’s rates, a stove that is not lit, and along one wall a row of pegs, each with a name over it and nothing on it. The pegs of those who died last winter. There are forty-one. Bess Ackroyd, the Prior of the Barefoot, a square grey woman with her feet in a bowl and a ledger on her knee, who looks at everyone’s feet first, is here.

**Talk to Bess Ackroyd**

The woman with her feet in the bowl looks at your feet, and then at your face. ‘Not a proxy,’ she says. ‘Not a burgess. A stranger with boots.’ She turns a page of her ledger. ‘If you’ve come to look at the poor, the pegs are along that wall. Forty-one. Everybody looks at the pegs.’

**Ask Bess Ackroyd about the Barefoot**

‘We’re the backs,’ says Bess. ‘Every fast in Gaunt, every vigil, every mile on the setts, every stripe at the post that some burgess earned, one of us does it, and gets paid, and eats.’ She nods at the pegs. ‘Last winter the Wood-reeve held the oak back for a fortnight in the frost, and shut the dead-wood right, which was always ours, in the Bishop’s Wood. Forty-one.’ She turns a page. ‘I’ve said I’ll call the Row out on Ash Day if he doesn’t open the right. Ash Day, when every burgess wants to be clean for the gilding. Best wage of the year.’ She looks at her ledger. ‘They’ll not come out. They can’t afford to. I can’t afford to. But I’ve said it.’

**Tell Bess Ackroyd that Mistress Dinsdale has hired every back in the Stand for Ash Day**

Bess Ackroyd sits quite still. Then she laughs, which you have not heard her do; it is a short dry sound like a stick breaking. ‘Every back,’ she says. ‘Paid in advance, I’ll be bound; Grizel Dinsdale always pays in advance.’ She shuts the ledger. ‘So we’re paid for Ash Day already, every one of us. And if we stay at home, there’s not a proxy to be had in Gaunt for love nor money, and every burgess that wants to be clean for the gilding will have to—‘ She stops, and looks at the pegs. ‘Will have to do it themselves,’ she says. ‘Barefoot. In the snow. In front of the Row.’ She stands up. ‘I’ll call them out. They’ll come, for that. They’d pay to see that.’

FASTENS’ EVE. The last day before the Fast. At two the Doctors dispute in the Schools; at seven the masks come out, and the Fat King is carried round the Middle Town on a door, to be burnt at midnight outside the Wood Exchange, where wood is dearest; and at midnight the tenor bell of the Minster rings the Fast in, and whatever was said under a mask was never said.

**Wait › An hour**

On Ash Day morning the Stand is empty. Every pen, every board rubbed out, and the clerk sitting at his desk at the gate with his ledger open and his pen up and nobody to write. In the Row, every doorstep has someone sitting on it with their feet in a bowl, watching the Middle Town, as if waiting for a procession. At half past eight it begins: the burgesses of Gaunt, who must be clean for the gilding, coming down the Shambles barefoot in the snow to do their own penance, one by one, white-faced, in their shirts, with their boards round their necks in their own hands. The Row watches. Nobody laughs at first. Then a child does, and then everybody does, and then, quite soon, nobody does again. ASH DAY. At nine every forehead in Gaunt is marked with ash at the Minster door, and the Ladder of Merit is gilded for the year. At noon the Consistory sits in the Chapter House on the matter of Ninian Hern. At dusk, for the first time in nineteen years, the Gate of Ash may be opened. Nell gains 300 experience: brought the Barefoot out on Ash Day.

**Out to the Row**

The Row: lodgings on the old wall, feet in bowls in the doorways, Bow Gate, the Barefoot Hall. Prudence Garth, a pale serious girl of seventeen with a student’s satchel and darned gloves, who does not meet anyone’s eye, is here.

**To the Long Shambles**

The Shambles: the penthouse, the butchers, the chestnut man, the Steps up to the Close and Bow Gate down to the Row.

**To the Bearer's Steps, before the Close Gate**

The Bearer’s Steps: thirty broad stone steps up to the Close Gate, every one worn into a dip in the middle by knees, and the Close Gate at the top with a porter behind a grille.

**To the Close of Saint Oswy**

Canon Hamo Laverock, the Master of the Fabric, in a fur tippet, a pleasant round man with very clean hands, is here. The Close: swept snow, smoking chimneys, the Penitentiary, the Schools, the Minster and its one and a half towers. The gaol door is shut. The Consistory’s gaol admits visitors between ten and four, because the Consistory is merciful.

**Into the Minster**

The Minster porch, and in it the Ladder of Merit: a board of black oak twice the height of a man, on which the most penitent men and women of Gaunt are listed in gold, in order of how much they have paid other people to suffer. At the top: HUGH THACKRAY, WOOD-REEVE. Second, by a hair’s breadth and a fresh coat of gold that was not there yesterday: GRIZEL DINSDALE and P. SOWERBY, alternately. Master Sowerby sits on the step of the porch under the Ladder, barefoot, with his feet in a bowl of warm water, and people keep stopping to look at him as if he were a relic. Mistress Dinsdale, in her velvet bonnet and no shoes, stands in the porch under the Ladder with her chin up, daring anyone to say anything, and nobody does. Here are Mistress Grizel Dinsdale, a brewster in a velvet bonnet at a high desk, doing sums of penance and of beer in two columns; and Master Pentecost Sowerby, a draper with a measuring-rod he holds like a sceptre, and the look of a man who has been second for eleven years.

**Wait › An hour**

At noon the Consistory does not sit. It cannot: of its seven members, five are on the Bearer’s Steps, barefoot, in their shirts, with boards round their necks, doing their own penance for the gilding because there is not a proxy to be had in Gaunt; and the sixth, the Wood-reeve, is sitting on the bottom step with his shoes in his hand, weeping, for reasons nobody can get out of him. The Lady Penitentiary comes out of the Chapter House at a quarter past twelve, looks down the Steps at her Consistory, and says, quite loudly, to the whole of the Close: ‘Adjourned. Until the Commerce of Merit resumes.’ The Row, in the nave, begins to laugh, and then, because it is Ash Day, stops. Nell gains 400 experience: saw the Consistory adjourned for want of proxies.

**Wait › An hour**

Your tally is still notched. In the Register of Gaunt you owe what you owe, and it will be there when you come back, doubled. The Carth vigil was broken, and the Scaifes owe the twenty shillings back, and the rent besides. At Lady Day the Fabric will have them off the fell. Annis Scaife has already been down to the Stand to ask the rates for a fast. Dowsie Scaife is up and about in her mother’s shawl, with a cough she is proud of, telling the hens about the stone man in the Minster, who she has never seen. The Ladder of Merit is gilded for the year with HUGH THACKRAY, WOOD-REEVE at the top of it, and the price of oak is thirty-one shillings the cord. Abel Lumb fasts for Mistress Pickersgill’s thoughts, eleven a week, and she goes down on Mondays to watch him not eat; and neither of them has said anything to anyone. Master Sowerby is not gilded second on the Ladder this year, because he did not hire a proxy for Ash Day and so was not clean by the canons in time for the gilding; and he does not seem to mind. He has given a widow her money back for a bolt of black cloth, seven years late, with interest. On the north tower Joby Thwaite’s masons set a course of white stone today, and tonight the frost will get into it. The Stand reopened on the Thursday, at the old rates. Nobody in the Row has stopped talking about Ash Day, and nobody in the Middle Town has started. Hal Metcalfe is in the Consistory gaol, in the warm room, for Fastens’ Eve and a ladder, and will be heard after the Fast. Prudence Garth’s mother has paid her pains and can buy in the Shambles, and Prudence has not been seen on the Steps since.

**Wait › An hour**

The Consistory of Gaunt never sat on the matter of Ninian Hern. On Ash Day the burgesses of the city walked their own penances barefoot through the snow in front of the Row, and the Lady Penitentiary adjourned until the Commerce of Merit should resume, which it did, on the Thursday, at the old rates. Hern was let out of the gaol on Lady Day with no charge against him that anybody could remember. He went back to his room in the Row and sat on the step of the Barefoot Hall with his feet in a bowl. The next morning was cold and clear, and the Stand was full.
