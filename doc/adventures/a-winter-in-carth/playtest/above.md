*(The first page)*

The road comes up out of the march between snow-fields and stops being a road and starts being a fell road at a cross of grey stone, man-high, with a ledge round its foot worn hollow by knees. Someone has carved on its shaft, very neatly: FOR THE FAULTS OF ANOTHER. A low stone house stands beside it with smoke coming out of it, and on the step of the Cross a barefoot old man is mending a sandal.

**Talk to Beadle Clem Sutcliffe**

The man in the beadle’s gown does not get up. ‘You’ll be going north,’ he says. ‘Everybody’s going north. It’s the Fast.’ He looks at his foot, which is the colour of a candle. ‘I’m not. I’ve a commission for whoever is.’

**Ask the beadle what the commission is**

‘Him,’ says the beadle, and nods at the old man on the step. ‘Ninian Hern. Barefoot Hern, they call him in the Row. The Consistory sits on him on Ash Day, for heresy, and I was sent to fetch him in, and I’ve fetched him this far, and here’s my foot.’ He takes it out of the hat and looks at it and puts it back. ‘He’s not chained. He’d go by himself, he says. But the law says he’s to be brought, and brought means somebody brings him, and the somebody’s paid. Two shillings now. Eight at the Fell Gate, from the Assessor, when you hand him over.’ He sniffs. ‘It’s three days’ walk for a beadle. It’s one for anybody with two feet.’

**Take Ninian Hern into your custody, for the beadle's two shillings now and eight at the Fell Gate**

The beadle counts two shillings into your palm out of a purse that is mostly string. ‘He’s yours,’ he says. ‘Eight more at the Fell Gate. Don’t lose him. And don’t listen to him: he’s very persuasive and he’s always wrong.’ Hern has finished not-mending his sandal. He throws it into the snow, stands up on his bare feet, and shakes your hand, solemnly, as if a bargain had been struck. ‘My custodian,’ he says. ‘I’ve never had one. I’ve been one, often. It’s very restful, being had.’ Hern picks up his stick and falls in at your shoulder, barefoot, as if he had been waiting all morning to be asked. **Into the vigil-house at the Cross**

The vigil-house: one room, a good fire, a pair of snowshoes on the wall, a long pole shod with iron, a lantern with a horn shutter, and a book open on the table with a column of farm-names down one side and against each, for each night, a small neat C. **Talk to Raistrick**

The man at the table looks up from his book, and closes it on his finger, and waits. He is perfectly polite. ‘Raistrick,’ he says. ‘I keep the vigils on this side of the Levels.’

**Ask Raistrick what he keeps**

‘Vigils,’ says Raistrick. ‘Cold vigils. A house that takes one keeps no fire, for so many nights; I see that it does.’ He holds up his right hand, and turns it over, as if it were a tool he were showing you. ‘Once a night, at an hour I choose, I go in, and I put my hand in the ash of the hearth. **Ask Raistrick whose vigils they are**

‘The Penitentiary’s.’ He opens his book again, and finds his place. ‘Bought, of the Penitentiary, by those who owe; kept, for wages, by those who need them. I keep the count.’ He makes a small neat C against a name. ‘I report each morning at ten to the one who pays, at his own yard, so he knows his nights are being kept. That’s his right.’ He looks up. ‘I’m not to say who. You’ll know the yard by the billet over the gate.’

**To the Proxy's Stone**

You walk fast to keep warm, and it does not help much. **Ask Hern what penance it was that he refused**

Hern is quiet for a while. ‘The Cold Vigil,’ he says. ‘Forty nights. No fire in the house, not a spark, from Candlemas on. It’s the penance for blood. Somebody in Gaunt has blood on him, and was sorry, and went to the Penitentiary, and they set it at forty nights cold, and he paid for it, and they offered it to me.’ He spits. ‘I’ve kept cold vigils. Three. I’m the best there is. And I thought, I’ll freeze forty nights so some man in a warm house needn’t, and he’ll be forgiven, and the blood won’t be any less on the floor. And I said no.’ He looks at his feet. ‘So somebody else is keeping it.’

**Ask Hern who will judge him**

‘The Lady Penitentiary,’ says Hern, as if it were a joke he had made long ago. ‘Hawise Strake. We were proxies together in the Row when we were twelve: we fasted for a fishmonger’s wife, the pair of us, a week, and ate her cat’s dinner on the fourth day. She went up. I stayed down. She’ll put me out.’ He looks at the snow. ‘She’ll be very fair about it. She always was. That was the trouble.’

**To the Drove Fork**

It is cold enough to see your breath, and the ruts are hard with frost. He comes back. ‘Not who I was,’ he says. ‘No. I’m a man who’s been called after on a road, which I wasn’t when I set out.’ He falls in beside you. ‘Keep it anyway. That’s not the Flow, and it’s not the One. I don’t know what it is. It’s better than both.’ Wren gains 100 experience: answered Hern’s three questions on the road.

**To the top of Carth Fell**

You set out from the Drove Fork. The snow comes in all at once, the way it does on a fell: one moment the road and the long white country beyond it, and the next nothing, a roaring grey, and your own breath going away sideways. You cannot see the road down. Behind you the road down off the fell is gone under a moving wall of white. Nobody goes back down that in this. **To the yard at Carth**

The yard at Carth: a long low house with the byre under the same roof, its one window black; a peat-stack drifted half over; a dog at the door, which barks once and then whines. **Into the house at Carth**

**Talk to Annis Scaife**

**Ask Annis Scaife why there is no fire**

‘Because we’re keeping a vigil,’ says Annis. ‘A cold one. Forty nights. For somebody in Gaunt that’s done something, and is sorry, and paid the Penitentiary to have it kept.’ A pause. ‘Two shillings a night. Half in advance. It’s the rent. We’re two years behind with the Fabric and they’d have had us out at Lady Day.’ Another pause, longer. ‘It’s the twenty-third. Seventeen to go. Ralph died on the fourteenth. He’s in the byre. The ground won’t take a spade.’ She says it all in the same voice, the voice of someone reading out a list.

**Ask Annis who paid them**

‘The Penitentiary,’ says Annis. ‘That’s who pays. That’s who always pays. Whoever owes pays the Penitentiary, and it pays you.’ A box is opened, and something put into your hand: a shilling, cold, with a nick on its face you can feel with your thumb, a little raised oblong. ‘Except it didn’t come from the Penitentiary’s chest,’ she says. ‘The clerk brought it in a bag with the Wood Exchange’s mark on it, the billet. All twenty of them have it.’ She takes the coin back. ‘I’m not a fool. I know what’s dear this winter, and who’s sorry.’

**Take your leave**

‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Annis Scaife, who was not trying to.

**Talk to Old Mother Scaife**

‘Is that the Guest?’ says an old voice by the hearth, quite calmly. Then: ‘No. The Guest doesn’t talk. Sit down, love. Ralph’s in the byre. He’ll not mind you.’

**Ask Old Mother Scaife who the Guest is**

‘The Cold Guest.’ She is pleased to be asked. ‘When a house lets its fire go out for a debt, the Guest comes and sits at the hearth. You lay him a place, so he knows he’s expected and doesn’t take offence. My grandmother laid one in the bad winter, and he came, and he sat, and in the spring there were three fewer of them.’ The rocking goes on. ‘I’ve laid his place. It’s by the hearth. Don’t sit in it.’ A pause. ‘In my mother’s day, of course, the beasts were in with us all winter, and nobody had to lay anything. You can’t let a fire go out that’s a cow.’

**Take your leave**

‘Look after yourself,’ says Old Mother Scaife. ‘I mean it.’

You stand up behind the rope and give your name and show your clean tally, and the Lady Penitentiary says, ‘Speak,’ and you tell the Consistory of Gaunt about Carth: the house with no fire in it; the dark; the hand in the ash; the child’s cough, and what was done for her, and by whom; the old man in the byre on his hurdle, and the old woman who goes through to him at night. The Chapter House is very quiet. Beside the stone chair the Wood-reeve has gone grey. When you have done, the Lady Penitentiary says, ‘Thank you,’ in a voice you have not heard her use, and writes nothing. Wren gains 200 experience: stood witness for Carth before the Consistory.

**Stand as a witness, and tell the Consistory what Hern said on the road**

You tell them: the fell road, the old man barefoot in the snow asking whether one man can be sorry for another; the kneeling proxy at the stone, and the board, and what is owed the dead; the Drove Fork. The canons write. Hern listens with his head on one side, as if hearing a story about someone he once knew, and when you get to what you yourself answered he grins at you across the floor, the three teeth, and the Lady Penitentiary sees him do it.

**Wait › An hour**

At one the Lady Penitentiary asks him: ‘Ninian Hern, do you recant?’ And Hern says no, and then he says it: what he came forty miles barefoot to say inside the Minster where nobody could walk past. Thirty years of other men’s fasts. The stairs on his knees. Eleven hours of contrition for a draper, thinking about dinner. ‘I was the best there was,’ he says, ‘and I was never sorry once, and nor were they, and the bells counted it.’ He coughs. ‘You can buy anything in Gaunt but that.’ He looks round at the city behind the rope, at the ash on its foreheads. ‘A man asked me on the road—‘ and he turns, and finds you behind the rope. ‘Can one man be sorry for another, I said. And this one said: yes, that’s what love is.’ He lets the Chapter House hear it. ‘Which you can’t hire at a shilling a night. Not even from me. I was the best there was.’ Then he stops, because he has said it, and stands on his square of stone looking pleased and frightened and old.

**Wait › An hour**

Beside the stone chair the Wood-reeve stands up. He is a big soft man in a fur gown and he has to put a hand on the Lady Penitentiary’s chair to do it. ‘I’ll bear it,’ he says. ‘By the canons. Putting-out is a penance; any penance may be borne.’ His voice shakes and then does not. ‘I bought forty nights cold of the Penitentiary last winter for the forty-one I froze in the Row by holding back the oak, and Ninian Hern wouldn’t keep them, and a farm on the fell has kept them for me ever since.’ He takes off the fur gown and lays it on the bench. ‘Now walk,’ he says, to nobody, and then, to the Lady Penitentiary: ‘Let me walk.’

**Wait › An hour**

The Lady Penitentiary stands up out of the stone chair, which nobody has seen a Lady Penitentiary do during a sitting. ‘The canons allow it,’ she says. ‘Nobody has ever asked them. I’ve been asked.’ She looks at Hern on his stone, and at the one who offered, and for a moment you see the girl from the Row who ate the cat’s dinner. ‘The sentence of putting-out on Ninian Hern is borne by another, who has offered it freely, and it is accepted, and it will be carried out at dusk at the Gate of Ash.’ The Chapter House breaks into noise. Under it, if you are near enough, you hear her say to the precentor: ‘And the Commerce of Merit has just put out an innocent person for a guilty one in front of the whole city, and called it law, which is what Ninian has been saying for a year. Write that down too.’ Wren gains 400 experience: saw a sentence borne by another in the Chapter House.

**Wait › An hour**

The Chapter House: a round stone room with a stone bench all round it and a stone chair at the head, and in the middle, on the floor, a little square of different stone, where the accused stands. An hour passes.

**Wait › An hour**

At five the Gate of Ash is opened, and Hugh Thackray, Wood-reeve, who has been top of the Ladder of Merit for eleven years, walks out of it into the Levels in his shirt, without his gown, barefoot, carrying nothing, and the gate is shut behind him. The Row is at the grating. Nobody jeers. Someone pushes a pair of old boots through the bars after him, and he comes back for them, and puts them on, sitting in the snow, and gets up, and goes away south, toward the fell, where there is a farm keeping a vigil he bought.

**Wait › An hour**

Your tally is clean. Gaunt has nothing against you, which in Gaunt is the most anybody ever has. At Carth there is a fire on the hearth and a cart of oak in the yard, and the forty nights are paid in full by the man who bought them, and the rent with them. Ralph Scaife is still in the byre, because the ground is still iron; but there is a lamp by him now, and nobody has to lie down with him to keep him warm. Dowsie Scaife is up and about in her mother’s shawl, with a cough she is proud of, telling the hens about the stone man in the Minster, who she has never seen. Hugh Thackray is struck from the Register of Gaunt and from the Ladder of Merit, where a mason has chiselled his name out of the gold, leaving a long pale scar at the top of the board that nobody will paint over. He walked to Carth in a pair of borrowed boots and knocked, and Annis Scaife let him in, and gave him the axe, and he is splitting the oak he sent up the fell, very badly, in the yard, in the snow, and will not come in to the fire until it is all done. Abel Lumb fasts for Mistress Pickersgill’s thoughts, eleven a week, and she goes down on Mondays to watch him not eat; and neither of them has said anything to anyone. Under the scar at the top of the Ladder, DINSDALE is gilded first this year and SOWERBY second, or the other way about; the city has stopped being able to tell, and the scar is above them both. The north tower of the Minster of Gaunt has a new course of white stone on it, the first in two hundred and eleven years that stayed, and Joby Thwaite goes up the scaffold every morning to look at it, and comes down, and sets another. The dead-wood right is open in the Bishop’s Wood, and every child in the Row is out there with a sledge, and the Barefoot Hall’s stove is lit, and on the wall the forty-one pegs have the forty-one names on them, painted, in white. Hal Metcalfe is in the Consistory gaol, in the warm room, for Fastens’ Eve and a ladder, and will be heard after the Fast. Prudence Garth’s mother has paid her pains and can buy in the Shambles, and Prudence has not been seen on the Steps since.

**Wait › An hour**

On Ash Day the Consistory of Gaunt accepted, for the first time in two hundred and eleven years, that a man’s sentence could be borne by another, and put out an innocent person for a guilty one in front of the whole city, and wrote it down. The Commerce of Merit has not fallen; it has only been seen. The next morning, at the Stand, the clerk could not find anyone who would be hired for anything, all morning; by afternoon it was business as usual, and by evening it was not quite.
