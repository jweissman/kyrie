*(The first page)*

The road comes up out of the march between snow-fields and stops being a road and starts being a fell road at a cross of grey stone, man-high, with a ledge round its foot worn hollow by knees. Someone has carved on its shaft, very neatly: FOR THE FAULTS OF ANOTHER. A low stone house stands beside it with smoke coming out of it, and on the step of the Cross a barefoot old man is mending a sandal.

**Talk to Beadle Clem Sutcliffe**

The man in the beadle’s gown does not get up. ‘You’ll be going north,’ he says. ‘Everybody’s going north. It’s the Fast.’ He looks at his foot, which is the colour of a candle. ‘I’m not. I’ve a commission for whoever is.’

**Ask the beadle what the commission is**

‘Him,’ says the beadle, and nods at the old man on the step. ‘Ninian Hern. Barefoot Hern, they call him in the Row. The Consistory sits on him on Ash Day, for heresy, and I was sent to fetch him in, and I’ve fetched him this far, and here’s my foot.’ He takes it out of the hat and looks at it and puts it back. ‘He’s not chained. He’d go by himself, he says. But the law says he’s to be brought, and brought means somebody brings him, and the somebody’s paid. Two shillings now. Eight at the Fell Gate, from the Assessor, when you hand him over.’ He sniffs. ‘It’s three days’ walk for a beadle. It’s one for anybody with two feet.’

**Bind up the beadle's frozen foot**

You rub the foot with snow until he swears, and then with your hands until he weeps, and then wrap it in a stocking of your own and his hat. ‘I’ll keep it,’ he says, surprised. He watches you a while. Then he says, low, so that Hern will not hear: ‘There’s a thing in the canons nobody reads. Putting-out’s a penance. The greatest there is, but a penance. And any penance may be borne by another.’ He looks at Hern on the step. ‘I’ve always wondered if anybody would. Nobody ever has, because nobody who was put out ever had a friend.’

**Take Ninian Hern into your custody, for the beadle's two shillings now and eight at the Fell Gate**

The beadle counts two shillings into your palm out of a purse that is mostly string. ‘He’s yours,’ he says. ‘Eight more at the Fell Gate. Don’t lose him. And don’t listen to him: he’s very persuasive and he’s always wrong.’ Hern has finished not-mending his sandal. He throws it into the snow, stands up on his bare feet, and shakes your hand, solemnly, as if a bargain had been struck. ‘My custodian,’ he says. ‘I’ve never had one. I’ve been one, often. It’s very restful, being had.’ Hern picks up his stick and falls in at your shoulder, barefoot, as if he had been waiting all morning to be asked. **To the Proxy's Stone**

You walk fast to keep warm, and it does not help much. All the way up the fell road Hern has walked beside you, bare feet in the snow, not hurrying, and you notice he puts each foot down flat and whole, like a man laying something on a table. Just below the stone he says: ‘Thirty years I did other people’s sorrow for them. Fasts, vigils, the stairs on my knees. I was the best in Gaunt.’ He coughs, and spits, and goes on. ‘So here’s a question for you to walk on. Can one man be sorry for another?’ Halfway up the fell the road passes a standing stone, and at the foot of the stone a man is kneeling in the snow with his head bowed and a board hung round his neck on a cord. He has been kneeling there since last winter. The frost has kept him. The board says, in good letters, FOR THE ADULTERY OF ONE OF THE COMMON COUNCIL OF GAUNT, FORTY NIGHTS, and under it, smaller, in a different hand, TOBIAS KELL, PROXY. Ninian Hern, a barefoot old man in a frieze coat, ignoring a cough, is here.

**Tell him: no. Sorrow is your own, or it is nothing**

Hern looks at you with great affection. ‘That’s my heresy,’ he says. ‘You’ve just said my heresy, on the king’s highway, to a man in custody. They’ll have you for that.’ He looks away up the road. ‘It’s true, mind. I’m only not sure it’s all that’s true.’ Hern stops at the stone. He looks at the kneeling man for a long time, and then he goes down on his own knees in the snow beside him, easily, as a man sits in his own chair, and looks into his face. ‘Tobias Kell,’ he says. ‘He was a good proxy. Steady. He’d have finished the forty, too, if it hadn’t been the winter it was.’ He puts out a hand and does not quite touch the board. ‘Here’s your second question, then. What do we owe the dead?’

**Tell him: to finish what they were doing**

Hern laughs, a short bark that turns into a cough. ‘Finish Tobias’s forty nights for Gideon Roper? Kneel here till the thaw?’ Then he stops laughing. ‘No. You don’t mean that. You mean finish what he was doing, which was feeding his sister’s boys.’ He looks at you sideways. ‘That’s a harder thing. That’s a thing a man could spend his life on.’

**Ask Hern what penance it was that he refused**

Hern is quiet for a while. ‘The Cold Vigil,’ he says. ‘Forty nights. No fire in the house, not a spark, from Candlemas on. It’s the penance for blood. Somebody in Gaunt has blood on him, and was sorry, and went to the Penitentiary, and they set it at forty nights cold, and he paid for it, and they offered it to me.’ He spits. ‘I’ve kept cold vigils. Three. I’m the best there is. And I thought, I’ll freeze forty nights so some man in a warm house needn’t, and he’ll be forgiven, and the blood won’t be any less on the floor. And I said no.’ He looks at his feet. ‘So somebody else is keeping it.’

**Into the house at Carth**

THE FIRST NIGHT. The door shuts behind you, and the dark is complete. Not the dark of a night outdoors, which has a sky in it; the dark of a shut room with no fire, which has nothing. Somebody takes your arm, a hard small hand, and puts you down on the floor against a wall, which is stone, and cold through your clothes in a moment. You hear: a child coughing, close; someone breathing slow by where the hearth must be; a clock; the cow, through the wall; a dog, which comes and presses itself against your legs. And the cold. Not the cold outside, which moves. A cold that has been sitting in this room for twenty-three days, and has settled. Fly stays where she is, against your legs, and goes on shivering, which is as much welcome as you are going to get. The hearth is dead: a bed of grey ash swept flat, and over it on the chain an empty pot. Beside it, on a cloth on the floor, a bowl and a spoon laid for somebody. Here are Ninian Hern, a barefoot old man in a frieze coat, ignoring a cough; Will Scaife, a big silent man with a shovel, grey-faced, who does not look at anyone; Annis Scaife, the farmer’s wife, thin as a lath, with her sleeves rolled and her mouth set; Old Mother Scaife, an old woman in a shawl, a shape by the dead hearth that rocks a little; and Dowsie Scaife, a child in the box-bed, under every blanket in the house, coughing.

**Talk to Annis Scaife**

‘You’ll be the ones the snow brought,’ says a woman’s voice. ‘There’s oatmeal in water and there’s a turnip, and you’re welcome to your share of both, and of the dark. Sit on the floor by the wall. Don’t touch the hearth.’

**Ask Annis Scaife why there is no fire**

‘Because we’re keeping a vigil,’ says Annis. ‘A cold one. Forty nights. For somebody in Gaunt that’s done something, and is sorry, and paid the Penitentiary to have it kept.’ A pause. ‘Two shillings a night. Half in advance. It’s the rent. We’re two years behind with the Fabric and they’d have had us out at Lady Day.’ Another pause, longer. ‘It’s the twenty-third. Seventeen to go. Ralph died on the fourteenth. He’s in the byre. The ground won’t take a spade.’ She says it all in the same voice, the voice of someone reading out a list.

**Take your leave**

‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Annis Scaife, who was not trying to.

You tell her: the dark, the hearth, the hand in the ash; the child’s cough, the old man in the byre, the old woman lying down with him. She goes on ladling the whole time, a ladle a bowl, never spilling. When you get to the old woman, she misses a bowl. The man holding it says nothing; he waits. She fills it. ‘Forty nights,’ she says. ‘I set it. It’s the penance for blood. I set it on a farm.’ The next bowl. ‘I’ve never seen a vigil kept. In thirty years. I’ve the Register. I’ve Raistrick’s sticks.’ She ladles. ‘Go on,’ she says. ‘The old woman. Go on.’

**Give the masked woman Hern's word: the soup was always too salt**

The ladle stops. The masked woman stands quite still with it held over the copper, dripping, and the queue waits, and nobody says anything, because it is the Row. Then she puts the ladle down and lifts her mask a little, just to wipe her eyes, and puts it back. ‘We made it,’ she says. ‘When we were fifteen. The Hall’s first soup. I salted it, and he said it was too salt, and I said he could fast, then. Forty-five years.’ She picks up the ladle. ‘It’s still too salt,’ she says. ‘I’ve never changed it. Tell him—‘ She stops. ‘No. I’ll tell him.’ Sefa gains 300 experience: moved the Lady Penitentiary.

**Take your leave**

The masked woman at the copper has gone back to work before you have turned round.

**To the Long Shambles**

The Shambles on Fastens’ Eve: lit end to end with torches, crowded, masked.

**Into the Fat Lamb**

The Fat Lamb on Fastens’ Eve: masks, music, a fiddle and a drum, the fire, the ox, couples at every table who would not be at one together on any other night, and the landlord going round with a jug saying ‘Midnight, mind. Here are Matthias Dent, the landlord of the Fat Lamb, in a leather apron, carving; and Old Joby Thwaite, a master mason of seventy-one with stone-dust in his eyebrows and hands like two mallets, eating a pie with great concentration.

**Take a bed and supper**

You eat what is put in front of you, and sleep long, under a roof. ASH DAY. At nine every forehead in Gaunt is marked with ash at the Minster door, and the Ladder of Merit is gilded for the year. At noon the Consistory sits in the Chapter House on the matter of Ninian Hern. At dusk, for the first time in nineteen years, the Gate of Ash may be opened. You reach level 2 (+10 HP). The Fat Lamb: a big warm inn, the warmest room you have stood in since the march, full of people eating as if somebody were going to take the plates away, which on Wednesday somebody is. A fire with half a sheep turning over it, and a boy turning the sheep.

**Wait › An hour**

At nine the Minster’s west door is opened and the city goes past it in a line that reaches down the Bearer’s Steps and into the Shambles, and a canon with a bowl of ash puts his thumb to every forehead, the brewster’s and the barefoot man’s alike, and says the same four words to each. **Out to the Long Shambles**

The Shambles: the penthouse, the butchers, the chestnut man, the Steps up to the Close and Bow Gate down to the Row.

**To the Bearer's Steps, before the Close Gate**

The Bearer’s Steps: thirty broad stone steps up to the Close Gate, every one worn into a dip in the middle by knees, and the Close Gate at the top with a porter behind a grille.

**To the Close of Saint Oswy**

The Close: swept snow, smoking chimneys, the Penitentiary, the Schools, the Minster and its one and a half towers. **Into the Minster**

The Minster porch, and in it the Ladder of Merit: a board of black oak twice the height of a man, on which the most penitent men and women of Gaunt are listed in gold, in order of how much they have paid other people to suffer. At the top: HUGH THACKRAY, WOOD-REEVE. Second, by a hair’s breadth and a fresh coat of gold that was not there yesterday: GRIZEL DINSDALE and P. SOWERBY, alternately.

**To the nave of the Minster**

The nave of the Minster on Ash Day, full: the whole city, it seems, with a grey thumb-mark on every forehead, standing, waiting, looking at the Chapter House door.

**To the Chapter House**

The Chapter House: a round stone room with a stone bench all round it and a stone chair at the head, and in the middle, on the floor, a little square of different stone, where the accused stands. Round the wall above the bench, cut one under another, the names of every Lord and Lady Penitentiary of Gaunt, thirty-one of them, the oldest worn almost away; the newest, cut sharp and still pale, HAWISE STRAKE, OF THE ROW. **Wait › A while**

The Chapter House on Ash Day: the stone bench full of canons with ash on their foreheads, the Lady Penitentiary in the stone chair, the Wood-reeve beside her for the Guildhall, and every inch of floor behind the rope packed with the city, standing, with ash on its forehead too. In the little square of different stone in the middle of the floor stands a barefoot old man in a new grey coat, coughing into his fist. Time passes. The Consistory sits. The charge is read by Canon Laverock, gently: that Ninian Hern, proxy, of the Row, has taught on the Minster steps that no man is sorry for another, therefore that no penance borne by another is of any effect, therefore that the Commerce of Merit is a fraud upon the faithful and the Fabric. Then the Determination of the Faculty, by Doctor Ploughwright, the Irrefragable, on three grounds. You do not understand the third. Hern, on his square of stone, is listening to it with professional appreciation.

**Stand as a witness, and tell the Consistory what you saw at Carth**

You stand up behind the rope and give your name and show your clean tally, and the Lady Penitentiary says, ‘Speak,’ and you tell the Consistory of Gaunt about Carth: the house with no fire in it; the dark; the hand in the ash; the child’s cough, and what was done for her, and by whom; the old man in the byre on his hurdle, and the old woman who goes through to him at night. The Chapter House is very quiet. Beside the stone chair the Wood-reeve has gone grey. When you have done, the Lady Penitentiary says, ‘Thank you,’ in a voice you have not heard her use, and writes nothing. Sefa gains 200 experience: stood witness for Carth before the Consistory.

*(2 waits in which nothing is told)*

**Wait › A while**

Time passes. At one the Lady Penitentiary asks him: ‘Ninian Hern, do you recant?’ And Hern says no, and then he says it: what he came forty miles barefoot to say inside the Minster where nobody could walk past. Thirty years of other men’s fasts. The stairs on his knees. Eleven hours of contrition for a draper, thinking about dinner. ‘I was the best there was,’ he says, ‘and I was never sorry once, and nor were they, and the bells counted it.’ He coughs. ‘You can buy anything in Gaunt but that.’ He looks round at the city behind the rope, at the ash on its foreheads. ‘A man asked me on the road—‘ and he turns, and finds you behind the rope. ‘Can one man be sorry for another, I said. And this one said: no; sorrow is your own, or it is nothing.’ He grins. ‘My heresy, from a stranger’s mouth, on the king’s highway, for nothing. You’ll want to try them too.’ Then he stops, because he has said it, and stands on his square of stone looking pleased and frightened and old.

**Stand, and offer to bear Ninian Hern's sentence yourself**

You stand up behind the rope with your clean tally in your hand and say it: that putting-out is a penance, by the canons, and any penance may be borne by another; and that you will bear his. Nobody moves. Then everybody does. The canons turn to each other on the bench; the city behind the rope makes a long sound like a wave going back down a beach; Hern, on his square of stone, says ‘No—‘ and starts to cough and cannot finish. In the stone chair the Lady Penitentiary looks at you for a long time without any expression at all. ‘The canons,’ she says at last, ‘allow it.’ She looks at Hern. ‘The canons have never been asked.’

**Wait › An hour**

The Lady Penitentiary stands up out of the stone chair, which nobody has seen a Lady Penitentiary do during a sitting. ‘The canons allow it,’ she says. ‘Nobody has ever asked them. I’ve been asked.’ She looks at Hern on his stone, and at the one who offered, and for a moment you see the girl from the Row who ate the cat’s dinner. ‘The sentence of putting-out on Ninian Hern is borne by another, who has offered it freely, and it is accepted, and it will be carried out at dusk at the Gate of Ash.’ The Chapter House breaks into noise. Under it, if you are near enough, you hear her say to the precentor: ‘And the Commerce of Merit has just put out an innocent person for a guilty one in front of the whole city, and called it law, which is what Ninian has been saying for a year. Write that down too.’ Sefa gains 400 experience: saw a sentence borne by another in the Chapter House.

**Wait › A while**

At five the serjeants come for you wherever you are, politely, and walk you to the Gate of Ash through a Row that is standing in its doorways to see, and through the gate, which is open on the Levels and the dusk, and leave you outside in the snow in what you stand in. The gate is shut. You hear the chain, and the seal. Your name is struck from the Register of Gaunt; no door in it will open to you again. Then, behind you, the wicket in the gate opens, which nobody has seen opened in nineteen years, and a barefoot old man in a new grey coat comes through it and shuts it after him. ‘Free man,’ says Hern. ‘I can go out of any gate I like.’ He looks at you. ‘You fool,’ he says. ‘You lovely fool.’ He looks south, to the fell. ‘Fourteen nights at Carth,’ he says. ‘Shall we? We’re the only people in the world who know how.’ Sefa gains 200 experience: went out of the Gate of Ash with him. Ninian Hern, a barefoot old man in a new grey coat, coughing, and pleased with the world, is here. The road across the Levels, with Gaunt at the end of it in its three walls, and out to the west the sheepfold’s thread of smoke. The Fell Gate is shut against you. **Wait › An hour**

The Gate of Ash is shut, and barred, and the bar is chained, and the chain is sealed with the Penitentiary’s seal. It is opened once a year, if anyone has been put out, at dusk on Ash Day. Your name is struck from the Register of Gaunt. You walked south across the Levels in the dark with a barefoot old man in a new grey coat, and at first light you came up the fell to Carth, where no smoke goes up, and knocked; and Annis Scaife opened the door, and looked at the pair of you, and stood aside. There are fourteen nights of the vigil left. You are keeping them, with the Scaifes, in the dark, the whole house in one bed, and Hern telling the child stories about Gaunt that are mostly true. It is going to be a long winter at Carth. The Carth vigil goes on, a night at a time, with more people in the house to keep it than there were, and so more warmth in the box-bed, which Raistrick cannot put his hand in. Dowsie Scaife is up and about in her mother’s shawl, with a cough she is proud of, telling the hens about the stone man in the Minster, who she has never seen. Old Mother Scaife sits by the hearth at Carth in the rocking-chair and does not go into the byre at night. Ralph, she tells the hens, is going; he always was slow. The Ladder of Merit is gilded for the year with HUGH THACKRAY, WOOD-REEVE at the top of it, and the price of oak is thirty-one shillings the cord. Abel Lumb fasts for Mistress Pickersgill’s thoughts, eleven a week, and she goes down on Mondays to watch him not eat; and neither of them has said anything to anyone. Under the top of the Ladder, DINSDALE is gilded second and SOWERBY third, or the other way about; the city has stopped being able to tell. **Wait › An hour**

On Ash Day the Consistory of Gaunt accepted, for the first time in two hundred and eleven years, that a man’s sentence could be borne by another, and put out an innocent person for a guilty one in front of the whole city, and wrote it down. The Commerce of Merit has not fallen; it has only been seen. The next morning, at the Stand, the clerk could not find anyone who would be hired for anything, all morning; by afternoon it was business as usual, and by evening it was not quite.
