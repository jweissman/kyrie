# The Leaving Supper: the story, before the Kyr

Haddle Cross has fed the Gannick Company for twenty-three days. The Company is sixty soldiers who were hired by no one
and left by no one. Their captain, Hester Gannick, keeps one custom: wherever she winters she is served a supper, and
when she has eaten one that is right she marches at dawn. In eleven towns she has never said it was right. The last
town cooked a goose, and kept her five weeks.

Haddle Cross has four days of grain.

## Scenes written first

**The reeve at the gate.** Godfrey Hurst has a swan. The lord's mere has swans, the swan is large, and the reeve has
decided it is the answer. 'She is a soldier,' he says. 'Soldiers are impressed by size.' Nobody has told him the
Captain was shown a swan in a town called Orrel.

**The sergeant.** Tamsin Ostrey is mending a boot on the tailgate of a cart and does not stop. 'She is not hungry,'
she says. 'People think she is hungry. She is looking for a pot she ate when she was nine, in a country that is a
lake now. Skellet. She cannot tell you what is in it. She can tell you what is not in it, and she does, with her
face, and then we stay.' She bites the thread. 'I want to go home, and I have not got one. I would like her to find
it.'

**The cook.** Dame Meggy Peake sits on a stool in the Bell and Barrow's kitchen with both hands in wet linen and
runs it like a ship. 'I have fed that woman nine times. Do you know what I learned? A soldier does not want the
best thing you can cook. She wants the thing that was cooked at her.' She will not say what that means. 'You are
my hands. Do not be clever with my pot.'

**The eel-smoker.** Josiah Tuck has three hundred eels on his rods and will not sell one to the Company. His son
Nat is a drummer in it. The boy went for the drum and stayed for the pay, and Josiah has not seen him in a month,
and he does not say that is the reason. 'Tell him the weir wants him in October. Tell him nothing else.' Nat, in the
camp, with his drum between his knees: 'Tell Da the Captain feeds us. Tell him I did not want to be fed.'

**The widow.** Imke Salter is eighty-one, behind a wall, among sorrel gone to seed, and will not open to soldiers,
reeves or the weather. She opens to someone who sits down and says nothing for as long as it takes. She came to Haddle
Cross out of Skellet as a girl of forty, with a pot. She ran the ash-kitchen at the relief camp in the hungry year,
and fed two hundred a day with barley, smoked eel, sorrel, fen-salt and a handful of the hearth. 'Grey,' she says.
'It was grey. Nobody has made it grey since. They think the ash is a mistake.'

**The supper.** The reeve's swan comes first, borne by four men and a song. The Captain looks at it as one looks at
a relative. Then the company's silence, and the pot.

If it is right she does not speak. The sergeant, who has stood behind her chair for eleven towns, sits down.

If Imke is at the table: the Captain, who is fifty and has killed for money and wept at none of it, looks at the old
woman's hands. 'You were the one with the ladle.' 'I had two hundred a day,' says Imke. 'I remember the ones who
licked it.'

If it is nearly right: she eats it all, courteously, and says it is very good, which is how Ostrey knows.

## What can be asked

A perfect supper is a gift to the Captain, and she is a woman who pays. At the table she will grant one thing:
the boy, the sergeant's discharge, or the grain the Company ate. Or nothing, and the old woman is allowed to be
asked a question about the sorrel.

## Endings

- **Skellet Grey:** the pot is right and Imke is at the table. The Captain sits in the garden until the bell.
- **That Will Do:** the pot is right. She marches at dawn and takes a jar of Imke's ash.
- **Near Enough:** the pot is close. She is polite, and the Company goes in a week.
- **The Swan:** it was wrong. She stays.
- **No Supper:** the dusk went by. The Captain walks to the granary herself.
