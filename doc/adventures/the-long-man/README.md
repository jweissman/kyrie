# The Long Man

*A design, written before a line of Kyr. Spoilers throughout.*

At the head of a narrow dale lies a long, straight, grassy ridge. The people of Merrow call it the Long Man and say a
giant king is buried under it. They are wrong about almost everything, and their dale owes its life to the mistake.

The ridge is a dam. It was built by a people who are gone, and it holds a cold deep lake that drowned their own low
town on purpose. It is kept, still, by the last thing they made: a tall grey figure of fired clay that walks the
crest at night with a long blade, cutting every sapling before its roots can crack the wall. Merrow calls it the
Mower and is frightened of it. This year the steward of the Hall has been prising the bronze out of the dam's
machinery and selling it downriver. The wall has begun to leak, and the Mower has begun to come down off the hill at
night to take its bronze back. The steward has posted forty crowns for whoever will lay the ghost of the Long Man.

The company arrives on the morning that notice goes up. In three days the autumn storm comes in over the fell.

---

## 1. Deep time, in three layers

**The Hesk.** Long before Merrow, the dale was a bog with a lake above it, and a people lived around the water. They
built in *weirstone*, a pale clay fired so hard that iron skids on it. Where water has run over it for centuries it
is glazed a cloudy blue-green, and when struck it rings like a bell. They wrote in notches cut along an edge. They
measured water as other peoples measured gold.

To dry the bog for fields, they did something no later people would understand: they raised their own lake.
- They built a wall across the dale's throat.
- They let the water climb over the lower half of their town, and moved uphill.
- They cut the reason into the wall of their water-house: WE GAVE THE LOW HOUSES TO THE WATER, SO THAT THE FIELDS
  WOULD BE DRY. WE WILL KEEP THE WALL AS WE KEPT THE HOUSES.

They built the gear that keeps a lake: sluices, a master wheel, gauge stones notched at every depth. Last, they made a
keeper that would not die.

Then they were gone. The record does not say how; the last notches in the keeper's niche are a count that stops.

**Merrow.** Centuries later, settlers came up the beck and found a dale of black soil, perfectly dry, at the foot of a
long green hill. Hills are not straight, so the hill was a barrow; barrows hold kings, so it was a giant king's, the
Long Man's. The broken weirstone they ploughed up was the giant's bones. The notched pillar in the beck became a font
for their chapel, and its notches a tally of the Long Man's sins. The tall figure on the hill at night was his ghost.
They put their chapel and their mill in the low ground by the water, where the Hesk had once had fields. They were
happy there, and dry, for two hundred years.

**The year the Long Man wept.** The parish register remembers one bad year, in a hand two centuries old: *In the
year the Long Man wept, the water came over the low field to the chapel step. The Mower walked by day as well as
night, and in seven days the water went back. The lord's men had dug into the Barrow for its gold.* Nobody since has
put those three sentences together.

**Now.** The Hall belongs to a lord who lives elsewhere and keeps a steward here. Aldous Venn has run the estate into
debt to a barge-master. A year ago his men broke through the back of the Hall's cellar, the old base of a Hesk tower,
into a gallery inside the Long Man. They found great wheels of weirstone set with teeth of bronze. Bronze is money.
They have taken twelve teeth so far, and the leak gate inside the wall can no longer close.

## 2. The land

The dale runs north to south. The beck rises in Hollow Water above the Long Man, comes out through the wall at the
Hollow Door, turns the mill and runs down past the village.

```
                      N
        HOLLOW WATER  (the lake: deep, cold, reeds at the edge)
          .  the drowned town (only when the water is drawn down)
          .        [Water-House]  island tower, the master wheel
        ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
          lakeshore ── boat-stage
   ═══════════════════ THE LONG MAN ═══════════════════  (the dam)
   the Hall (north end)     the crest      the sinkhole     the Hollow Door (south end)
     yard, study, cellar ─────── inside: the galleries ─────── the sluice chamber
                                                             │ the beck
        the Weeping Springs  (a row of seeps at the wall's foot)
        the low field (turning to bog)                     the mill, the mill landing
              ·                                               │
           MERROW: the green, the Drowned Ox, the Chapel of the Ford, Gammer Coller's cottage by the dyke
                                     │
                                  the south road
```

The Long Man in section, from the village to the lake:

```
   Merrow ── low field ── springs ┐                                ┌── lakeshore
                                   \  crest ─ sinkhole ─ gauge-head /     ~~~~ Hollow Water ~~~~
                                    \  galleries   leak    shaft  /       (deep; the drowned town on its floor)
                                     \ sluice chamber ── culvert ─/
                                      ────────── the old valley floor ──────────
```

**Inside the Long Man.**
- The Hall's broken cellar wall opens into the *inspection gallery*, a tunnel lined with weirstone and wide enough
  for one tall figure.
- From the gallery, ways go to:
  - the *sluice chamber*, where the great wheels stand over the culvert;
  - the *Keeper's Rest*, the Mower's niche, where it stands by day;
  - the *leak gallery*, where water comes through a crack in the inner face and the bronze-shuttered *leak gate*
    hangs half open;
  - the foot of the *gauge shaft*, a square well up to the crest with notches cut into it at every depth.
- The *sinkhole* on the crest is a fresh collapse into the leak gallery, a way in for anyone with a rope.
- The *Hollow Door* at the south end is the culvert mouth, closed by an iron grate the Hall put there. Venn has the
  key.

## 3. Who is here, and what they want

**Aldous Venn**, steward of the Hall. He is fifty, thin, ink-stained and charming, and desperate.
- He owes Cass Rowell sixty crowns.
- He has twelve bell-teeth in a cart in his yard waiting for her barge, and a ledger of every sale.
- He wants the Mower laid, so his men can strip the rest of the wheels.
- He carries the grate key on his belt.
- He knows the bronze comes from "the Long Folk's mill" and will not let himself think about the springs.
- If exposed, he tries to leave on Cass's barge.

**Dunnet**, Venn's foreman. He is big, slow and loyal. He is owed three months' wages and is frightened of what lives in
the leak gallery; he has seen it move. For a few coins and a kind word he will leave the cellar door unbarred, or say
where the teeth are. He won't go into the galleries again.

**Mother Ysolt**, priest of the Chapel of the Ford. She is sixty, fierce and kind.
- She believes the Mower is the Long Man's restless dead and should be laid with rites. She will ask the company to
  bring her its mask to bury.
- She keeps the parish register.
- Her font is the notched pillar. Its highest notch is level with the chapel's roof-beam, and she has never wondered
  why.
- Shown what the notches are, she changes her mind entirely. On the night of the storm she rings the chapel bell
  and gets Merrow up the hill.

**Hob Teller**, the miller. In the dry spring his wheel would not turn, so he took a spade to the Weeping Springs and
opened them wider. His race has run full ever since. So have the springs, more every week. He will lie about it. Shown
his own spade-cuts, he will close what he opened, with stakes and clay, if he is made to see that his mill stands
lowest of all.

**Gammer Nell Coller**, the dyke-reeve. She is eighty, small and sharp-eyed. She keeps the ditches and the old rhymes:

> *When the Mower walks the crown, keep the willow-roots cut down.*
> *When the Mower walks the green, the water's coming in between.*
> *When the Mower walks by day, Merrow, take your bed away.*

She carries the dyke-reeve's rod, which has passed from reeve to reeve: a staff of dark wood shod in notched bronze
at both ends. She measures ditches with it. It is a Hesk sluice key, and it fits the master wheel in the Water-House.
She will lend it to a company she trusts.

**Cass Rowell**, master of the barge *Heron*. She is hard, but fair by her own lights.
- She comes up the beck at noon on the second day to load the bronze, and leaves at dusk with two hands who can fight.
- She wants the bronze or her sixty crowns.
- She buys bell-teeth from anyone at eight coin each and sells them back at twelve.
- She fights anyone who takes cargo off her deck.

**The Mower** does not speak and cannot be bargained with.
- By day it stands in the Keeper's Rest.
- At dusk it climbs to the crest and walks it until dawn, cutting saplings.
- When bronze has gone from its wheels it goes after it, by the shortest way, wherever it is: the Hall yard, or
  Cass's deck while the barge is at the landing.
- It fights only what stops it or what harms the wall.
- With all twelve teeth back in their wheel, it closes the leak gate in a night, and the springs begin to fail.

**The Wet** is what the leak has made of the clay core: a slumped, crawling mass in the leak gallery. Like any
elemental it holds its ground. It grows bolder as the leak grows, and it slumps back into mud when the gate shuts.

**The Old Jack**, a pike as long as a boat, keeps the deep water by the Water-House. It takes what falls in.

## 4. Things that still work

- **The leak gate**, a bronze-toothed shutter in the leak gallery. It needs its twelve teeth to close, and a keeper,
  or anyone strong enough, to close it.
- **The sluice wheels** over the culvert. Turned, the bottom sluice opens: the culvert fills, and the beck runs high
  and fast past the mill for as long as it stays open.
- **The master wheel** in the Water-House, worked with the dyke-reeve's rod. It draws Hollow Water down by a
  man's height in a day. The pressure on the wall falls, the drowned town comes up out of the water, and for that day
  the beck floods the low field and the mill.
- **The gauge stones**: the font, the shaft, and a stone on the crest.
  - They show the water's height, and what it has been.
  - Anyone who can read old hands can read the notch-script on them.
  - Anyone who understands water can read their meaning without the script.
- **The Mower** itself.

## 5. Dangers

- The Wet, in the leak gallery and the sinkhole below the crest.
- The culvert when the sluice is open: the water comes fast and fills it to the roof.
- The sinkhole's edge. Without a rope it is a fall into the dark.
- The Old Jack, on the water.
- Cass Rowell's hands, if her cargo is touched.
- The Mower, if it is attacked or its work is undone in front of it.
- The storm, and what it does to a wall that is leaking.

## 6. Three days, if nobody interferes

| When | What happens |
|---|---|
| Day 1, morning | The company comes up the south road. Venn's notice is new on the Drowned Ox's door. |
| Day 1, night | The Mower walks the crest. At midnight it comes down to the Hall yard for the bronze and smashes the cart, but Venn has had the teeth moved to the cellar. Dunnet is hurt. |
| Day 2, noon | Cass Rowell's barge ties up at the mill landing. Venn's men load the teeth. |
| Day 2, dusk | The barge leaves with the bronze. The Mower comes down to the landing too late. |
| Day 2, night | The springs run hard. The low field goes under a hand's depth of water. |
| Day 3, noon | Rain, then the storm. Hollow Water rises. |
| Day 3, two hours after midnight | The Long Man breaks. |

When the wall breaks, the water takes the low ground: the low field, the mill, the chapel, the green. Anyone there
when it comes is swept away. Anyone in the culvert or the galleries is drowned. Merrow survives only if someone has
got it up the hill.

## 7. Discoveries that change what the company understands

| What Merrow believes | What shows otherwise | What is true |
|---|---|---|
| The Long Man is a barrow. | It is dead straight, holds a lake, and has a culvert through it. Inside are wheels, not graves. | It is a wall. |
| The Mower is the Long Man's ghost. | It cuts saplings, goes after bronze, and stands in a workshop by day. Its breastplate reads KEEPER OF THE WALL. | It keeps the dam. Laying it dooms the dale. |
| The springs are a blessing. | The miller's spade-cuts, the growing seeps, the bog in the low field. | They are the dam's leak, and getting worse. |
| The font's notches are a tally of sins. | The same notches in the gauge shaft and on the crest; the register's flood that reached the chapel step. | They are water heights. The highest is the chapel roof. |
| The dyke-reeve's rod measures ditches. | Its notched bronze shoe matches a socket on the Water-House wheel. | It is a sluice key. |
| The Hesk were giants who vanished. | Their own words in the Water-House: they drowned their town to dry these fields. | Merrow farms a lake-bed by a bargain it never knew it had made. |
| Venn is hunting a monster for the village's sake. | His ledger, the empty sockets, Cass's barge. | He is selling the wall. |

## 8. How it can go

There is no plot, only what each party will do. Some of the ways it can end:
- **Laid.** The company destroys the Mower and takes Venn's forty crowns. Unless the company itself fits the teeth and
  closes the gate (it can do the keeper's work), the wall breaks on the third night.
- **Mended.** The teeth go back, from the cellar, from Cass's barge, or bought back from her. The Mower closes the
  leak gate. If Hob closes his cuts too, the springs fail. Venn is ruined, and runs or is caught; Cass wants her
  money from somebody; the mill turns slower. Merrow never learns how close it came, unless the company tells it.
- **Drawn down.** With Gammer Coller's rod, the company turns the master wheel. The lake drops, the wall holds even
  leaking, and the drowned Hesk town comes up into the air for the first time in a thousand years, with whatever
  is in it. The mill is wrecked for a day and the low field ruined for a season. Merrow learns what its fields are.
- **Warned.** The wall breaks, but Mother Ysolt rang the bell and Merrow was on the hill. The low town is gone.
- **Lost.** The wall breaks at night, with Merrow asleep in the low town.

## 9. Things a company might try that the world must answer

- Watching the crest at night instead of fighting what walks it.
- Following the Mower down to the Hall, and seeing what it carries back.
- Going in by the sinkhole with a rope, by the cellar with Dunnet's help, or by the grate with Venn's key, lifted,
  bought or borrowed.
- Fitting the teeth itself, closing the gate itself, closing the miller's cuts with a shovel.
- Showing Ysolt the gauge shaft, the miller his spade-cuts, Venn his own ledger, or Cass the Mower.
- Selling the teeth to Cass itself.
- Taking the rod without asking.
- Opening the sluice to flood the culvert behind someone.
- Leaving the dale, and coming back after the storm to whatever it did.

## 10. Voice

Merrow is grey stone, black soil, willow, wet grass and smoke. Nothing in it is ancient-looking. What is ancient is
smooth, grey and plain, and easy to miss. The prose keeps to what can be seen, heard and handled:
- The Mower is *tall, grey, and too smooth*.
- The weirstone *rings when struck*.
- The water is *cold enough to ache*.

People speak briefly and about themselves. Nobody explains the Hesk; the Hesk explain themselves, in notches, to
whoever can read them.

## 11. What the dale was missing

*Added after the first browser play, before any of it was written in Kyr.*

Played through, the dale had strong bones and thin flesh. The drowned town rewarded the long way to it with a
sentence. Pell, the man Dunnet went into the hill with, was a name in one line of talk. The barge-master came and
went like a cart on a timetable. Merrow stood at its well all day and all night. None of these needs more topics or
another ending. Each needs things in the world that were always there and that the company can find, handle, and
use, and people who act on their own reasons.

**The town under the lake.** When Hollow Water goes down a man's height, the top of the Hesk low town stands out of
it: one street of tall narrow doors, silted to the sills. Three doors are worth opening.
- *The street.* Over each lintel a short row of notches: the household's own last measure of the water.
- *The face-house.* Here the Hesk fired their keepers' faces. A cold kiln; shelves of clay moulds, each the hollow of
  a face; and in one mould a grey face, fired and never fitted. A keeper is woken by its face, and stopped by losing
  it. The company now holds a second face. Venn wants a mask to show for his forty crowns, and Ysolt wants one to
  bury, and neither of them can tell one grey face from another.
- *The last house.* Its door is barred on the outside. Inside, a table is laid for a meal nobody ate, and down the
  door-post runs a tally of days that stops at forty-one. On the sill sits a child's toy of weirstone, a little bell
  that rings at a touch. The Hesk meant to come back. The last notches in the keeper's niche are the same count.

**Pell.** Dunnet and Pell went into the wet gallery together for the teeth. The clay stood up, and Pell did not come
out. Merrow believes Pell went downriver on the Heron in the spring, because Venn told it so. Dunnet knows better and
drinks. Pell's lantern lies in the clay at the far end of the wet gallery, its glass packed with mud.
- Brought back to Dunnet, the lantern breaks him: he tells the company everything.
- After that, he will go and tell Mother Ysolt. The steward is exposed before his own parish without a ledger being
  read.

**Dunnet, hurt.** When the Mower turns the cart over at midnight, the flying shaft catches Dunnet in the yard and
breaks his arm. On the second day he sits in the lean-to with his arm in a sling of sacking and a temper to match. A
company that binds it properly has a friend: the cellar bar comes off for them at dark, and no wages need change
hands.

**Cass and the bronze.** Cass Rowell grew up on the river below a broken weir, and her father drowned when it went.
Told what the bronze was holding shut, she will not carry it. She sets the sack down on the landing and goes up the
lane to the Hall for her sixty crowns in silver. When Venn cannot find them, she takes the Hall's plate instead.
After that Venn is ruined whatever the company does.

**Merrow keeps its own hours.**
- By day its people are at the well. At dusk they go into the Ox, and the talk there is of the day.
- On the second day Cass drinks there for an hour before she sails.
- Every evening Hob Teller walks up to the springs with his spade over his shoulder and stands looking at the water.
  A company can find him there, guilty, before anyone has shown him anything.
