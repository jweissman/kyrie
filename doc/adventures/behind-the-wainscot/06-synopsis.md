# 06 — Synopsis: the story, its acts, its people, its ends

## The situation before anyone arrives

Three hundred and twelve years ago a giant's child, Ermengarde Whitlock, found a starving family in the coal-hole of
Hollins and hid them in her doll's house, which was exactly their size. Her mother found the doll's bed slept in.
The doctor said the child was ill; she was sent across the sea to an aunt; going out of the Hall she stopped the Long
Clock with her finger, so that it would still be today when she came home. She never came home. Her mother died, the
house was shut, and over three centuries six hundred of the hungry and the hunted came in by its letterbox: the
Wainscot. Ermengarde became the greatest cartographer in Imbar, and never drew her own house. Now she is three
hundred and twenty, nearly blind, and dying, and four days ago she came home to draw it.

## What happens if nobody does anything

She winds the Clock on the first evening. On the second morning her companion lights the drawing-room fire on top
of the colliers' town in the grate; eleven die. On the third morning the Jarl's son dies in the companion's rat-trap.
The Jarl gathers up the poisoned bread she lays along the skirting, and on the sixth afternoon his man climbs the
tea table and puts it in the Lady's sugar. She dies that night. **The Tea at Ten Past Four.**

This is the world's own history, and the first thing a company that waits in the inn hears about.

## The acts

**Bootscrape** (the morning of the first day). A salvagers' village on a doorstep. The notice on the inn door. Jory
Mudge, who decides to tell you what Bootscrape tells nobody. Mrs Pyecroft at her corner table with her ledger.

**I. The Ratcatcher** (from the first step through the Slot). The Letterdrift and the Post; the Skirting, the Button
Market, the Nail; Mab Tenter's black ribbon; the Hearth with a fire laid in it; the Readers walking an encyclopaedia;
the Lady at her chart table, with nothing drawn inside her walls. At six, **Ten Past Four**: she comes down the
Stair with her candle and winds the Clock, and it strikes for the first time since the Going.

**II. The Thaw** (from nine on the second morning). The fire is lit. The colliers are on the Skirting, alive or not.
The house is warm for the first time in three hundred years and the lettuces come up on the sill. The trap takes
Wat, or does not. The Moot sits in the doll's house: the Hearth, the Lady, the cat. The chart is written on in the
night, and found, and answered; or the Keys play the Little Ship to its end; or a letter that has waited two hundred
and ninety-one years is delivered; or Dame Honor Lacey walks out onto the tea table in her handkerchief gown.

**III. The Smoking** (from the day the sulphur is in the house, or the seventh). Mrs Pyecroft's candles; the Concern's
surveyor; the Jarl's man on the table leg; the deed in the vault under the cistern; the will, if there is a will.
The Wainscot packs without saying so.

**The Last Night** (the tenth). She dies, whatever anyone does. If she knows them, they come up the Stair in the dark,
all six hundred, and stand on her floor, and the Keys play from downstairs.

## The people who matter most

- **Ermengarde Whitlock**, the Lady. Exact, dry, frightened, formidable. Told at eight that she imagined the people
  she loved; believed it; spent three hundred years measuring coasts so that nobody could tell her so again. Her wish
  is to finish her chart. Her fear is that the noises in the walls are her mother's prophecy come true. Her change:
  from "make it stop" to "so you do".
- **Dorothea Pyecroft**, her companion: our size, sixty-one, a vicar's widow; eleven years unpaid, never once asked
  anything about herself; has sold the house for stone on commission to provide for her old age, and wants the
  noises to be rats. The protagonist's employer. She can be cowed, bought, understood, provided for, or killed by tin
  soldiers.
- **Dame Honor Lacey**, Chatelaine of the Small House, eleventh of her name: afraid that the Lady will look at her and
  not know who she is, and that the Court's three hundred years are a story it told itself.
- **Hasp Tallowby**, Jarl of the Jars: a hard man who knows the pantry he lives on is not his; whose son is the first
  to die of the Lady's return, and who will kill her for it unless he is given a reason not to, or asked to come and be
  seen.
- **Sorrel Haddow**, a young Reader, who has taught herself to write the Lady's hand at the Lady's size with a broom,
  and wants nothing but to write back. A companion; she leaves the company in the end to be the Lady's scribe.
- **Mab Tenter**, who keeps the Nail, whose son the cat played with until he died: "I want it to stop."
- **Faith Penny**, Postmistress, bound by an oath to deliver letters to people who never come home.
- **Bet and Nan Collier** of the Hearth; **Kit Lacey**, who writes to Nan along the bell-wires.
- **Prior Tobit Hale** and **Sister Verity Cogg** of the Winders; **Ludovic Flatt** of the Keys; **Gideon Lamb** of
  the Readers; **Blind Annet** of the Under-folk; **Lance-Corporal Fennick** and **Captain Ordinance** of the Tin
  Regiment.
- In Bootscrape: **Jory Mudge**, **Hob Ludd**, **Widow Arkle**, **Mr Saltash**; and from the fifth day **Mr Overy**.

## Themes, worked through circumstance

- **Sanctuary and title.** The Wainscot lives in a house that belongs to someone else, on things that were not made
  for it. The question of the last act is literal: who owns Hollins? A will, a deed and a county court answer it.
- **Being told you imagined it.** The Lady's whole life is shaped by a diagnosis; her mother's unsent letter, found in
  the vault, reverses it. The Winders, who kept "the same day" for two hundred years on a child's word, are the same
  theme as comedy.
- **Warmth that kills.** Every kindness the Lady does her house (a fire, a running tap, traps against vermin, a
  fumigation to calm her nerves) is a catastrophe for the people in it, because she does not know they are there.
  Nothing in the house is evil; almost everything in it is dangerous.
- **Scale as a moral optic.** Mrs Pyecroft is exactly the Wainscot's size and despises it; the Lady is twelve times
  its size and loved it once. Size is not the measure of who looks down on whom.
- **Making, not finding.** The central act is not to read an inheritance but to write on one: to draw yourself into
  the chart of a house that left you out.

## The ends

The end comes on the tenth night, or the sixth if she is poisoned. The book closes on the first of these that holds,
from the most particular (`endings.kyr`), after a line for each fate that applies (Kit and Nan, Mrs Pyecroft, the
Regiment, the Jarl, the Under-folk, the stone, Sorrel, the Clock).

| Ending | When | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **The Ratcatcher** | the company told Pyecroft where they nest, and she never knew | thorough, discreet and successful |
| **The Tea at Ten Past Four** | she was poisoned | the Wainscot knows what was in the sugar, and will never say |
| **The Smoking** | the sulphur was lit and the Wainscot was in the walls | the Post's register ends in mid-line |
| **The Freehold** | she knew; her will was lodged with the deed | the first freehold in Imbar held by people living inside a building that belongs to them |
| **The Last Game, and the Quarrymen** | she knew; the deed went to the Concern | the Small House carried out of the house on a cart |
| **The Last Game** | she knew; no will that would stand | they live as they lived, in a house that now knows it |
| **Sold for Stone** | she never knew; the deed went to the Concern | Hollins taken down for a corn exchange and a gaol |
| **Down the Ladder** | the sulphur was lit; the Wainscot was out | Bootscrape twice the size, with a Post and a Court in the Mat's back room |
| **The House Goes Cold** | she never knew; nothing was sold | the fires go out; the Wainscot puts its coats on again |

In a generated world the book does not close: the chapter does, and the house is found as it was left
(`after.kyr`): a Freehold Hollins with a notice on the Mat's door that says NO RATCATCHERS; a Hollins in
scaffolding; a Hollins dark and nailed shut; a Hollins cold, with people still going in and out of its letterbox.
