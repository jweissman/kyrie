## The premise in one paragraph

In the western hills there is a house twelve times the size of a house. The giants who built it, the Whitlocks, were
the last of the tall gentry west of the march; the house has stood shut for three hundred years, and in that time
some six hundred people have come in by its letterbox and made a life in it. Three days before the protagonist
arrives, the last Whitlock came home to die. She has lit the fires, and her companion has advertised for a
ratcatcher.

## Scale, the one law

Giants are twelve times our height. A giant's room is sixty yards across; her stair is a flight of eight-foot cliffs;
a coin from her purse is a copper disc a foot wide, worth a farm in metal; a pin is a spear; a teacup is a cistern.
Animals are their own size: a mouse in the house is a mouse, a rat a rat. Only the giant, her things and her cat are
large. **Her doll's house, made at a twelfth of her scale, is exactly the size of a house for us.** That fact is the
seed of the whole history.

## History (most of it never told outright)

- **The Hungry Winter, three hundred and twelve years ago.** A famine in the hills. A family of the Lacey name,
  father, mother and three children, sheltered from snow in the coal-hole of Hollins. The Whitlocks' daughter
  Ermengarde, then a child of a giant's eight years, found them. She hid them in her doll's house in the nursery, fed
  them crumbs of a giant's bread, which is to say whole loaves, and taught them her nursery song. She wrote about
  them in a diary in a round child's hand. She called them her little people.
- **The Sweeping.** Her mother, Lady Agathe Whitlock, found droppings and a doll's bed slept in, and had the nursery
  swept and the doll's house shut. The Laceys fled into the walls. Ermengarde insisted they were real. Her mother told
  her she was a liar or ill, and sent her across the sea to an aunt. The carriage left at ten past four on a winter
  afternoon. On her way out of the hall the child stopped the long clock with her finger, so that it would still be
  today when she came home.
- **The Shut House.** Lady Agathe died within a decade. The servants left. The house was shut, the clock stopped,
  the letters falling through the slot for three centuries.
- **The Wainscot.** The Laceys survived in the walls, and word went round the desperate of the hills that there was
  a house nobody came to: you got in by the letterbox. Debtors came, runaways, a heretic preacher who taught that the
  stopped clock was holding the Family's return in suspension and must be kept, colliers from a flooded pit who took
  the cold grate and the coal-scuttle, a family of dwarf glaziers who took the windowsills. The Laceys moved back into
  the doll's house, and became, by possession of the only furniture in the world made at their scale, an aristocracy.
- **Miss Whitlock's life.** Ermengarde never came home. She became the greatest cartographer in Imbar, a giant who
  walked the coasts with a chain and a theodolite; the Whitlock charts are in every harbour-master's office. She never
  married. A letter from a giant who loved her, posted to Hollins in the year she left, lies in the drift of mail
  inside the door. Her eyes have failed. Her heart is failing. She has come home.

## Geography

Outside, westward from a hill country of the generated world: the march road comes over a moor to the village of
**Bootscrape**, built round the house's iron boot-scraper (a thirty-foot arch of iron, now the smithy) at the foot of
its doorstep. Bootscrape lives by giant salvage: what the Lodgers sell out through the letterbox (a pin, a button, a
nail) is ore to the hill country. It knows about the Lodgers and does not say so. On the moor above it stands the
**Waiting Stone**, a standing stone that has a face if you look at it at dusk.

The house, at Lodger scale:

- **The Hall.** The **Slot**, the brass letterbox, is the Lodgers' port: a rope ladder hangs inside it. Below it lies
  the **Letterdrift**, a dune of three centuries of unopened mail, mined by the Post for news of the world. The
  **Long Clock**, stopped at ten past four, holds the Winders in its case. The **foot of the Stair**, where the Riser
  families keep the ladders and charge a toll. Along the skirting, the **Wainscot** proper: a vertical town of lath
  and plaster lanes inside the walls, lit by candle-ends.
- **The Drawing Room.** The **Carpet**, a plain. The **Hearth**, a colliers' town in the cold grate and the flue, now
  about to be lit. The **Piano**, where the Guild of the Keys has practised for a century to play one tune, a note a
  minute, by teams jumping on the keys. The **Mantel**, the portraits of the Family, icons. The **tea table**.
- **The Library.** Books as cliffs. The **Readers** read a giant's books by walking along the lines with lanterns,
  a page a day; they have been on Volume M of an encyclopaedia for forty years. Her childhood atlas is here.
- **The Kitchen.** The cold **Range**; the **Sink**, where a dripping tap is the house's only running water and the
  **Tapwardens** ration it by the thimble; the **Pantry**, the Jarmen's country, with its jars of honey that have not
  spoiled in three hundred years, its sacks, its rats; the **dumbwaiter**, a lift worked by counterweights; the
  **bell-board**, whose wires run through every wall.
- **The Cellar.** Wine-bins; a cistern with something in it; the **Under-folk**, who went down a century ago and do
  not come up; the Whitlock **vault**, and in it the house's deed.
- **The Nursery.** The **Small House**, capital of the Wainscot and seat of the Little Court of the Laceys. The **Fort**
  and the **Tin Regiment**, clockwork soldiers half a man's height, wound by the child three centuries ago with orders
  to keep the little people safe from Mother, still at war with the mice. The **toy box**.
- **The Bedroom.** Her bed, her bedside table, her spectacles, her medicine. Mrs Pye lives in a hatbox on the
  dressing table, furnished as a room.
- **The Attic.** Trunks, dust, a barn owl through a broken slate, snow that has never melted.
- **The Garden.** The glasshouse, the vinery gone wild, robins.

## People

- **Miss Ermengarde Whitlock**, the Lady. Exact, dry, proud, frightened. She hears the house at night and thinks it
  is her mind going, as her mother said it would.
- **Mrs Dorothea Pye**, her companion of twenty-two years, of our size. She rides in a pocket, writes the Lady's
  letters with a quill as long as a spear, and has had no wages in eleven years. She has arranged to sell the house
  for stone after the Lady's death, on commission.
- **The Bishop**, the Lady's cat.
- **Dame Honor Lacey**, Chatelaine of the Small House, and her nephew **Kit**.
- **Prior Tobit Hale** of the Winders, and **Sister Verity Cogg**, who believes the Clock must never strike.
- **Sorrel Ames**, a young Reader, who has taught herself the giant hand and wants more than anything to speak to
  the giant.
- **Hasp Tallowby**, Jarl of the Jars, and his son **Wat**.
- **Bet Collier**, forewoman of the Hearth.
- **Faith Penny**, Postmistress of the Letterdrift.
- **Ludovic Flatt**, Cantor of the Keys.
- **Captain Ordinance** and **Lance-Corporal Fennick** of the Tin Regiment.
- **Drewe**, Tapwarden of the Sink. **Granny Mote** of the windowsill gardens.
- **Blind Annet** of the Under-folk.
- In Bootscrape: **Jory Mudge** of the Mat, **Hob Tallis** the salvager, **Widow Arkle** the chandler,
  **Mr Saltash** the notary and carrier, **Mr Overy** the surveyor of the Hollins Stone Concern.

## The principal story, in three acts

**Act I, The Ratcatcher.** Bootscrape; Pye's interview; the Slot; the discovery that the vermin are people; the
Wainscot; at dusk the Lady winds the Clock, and it strikes for the first time in three hundred years.

**Act II, The Thaw.** She lights the drawing-room fire; the Hearth burns; refugees fill the Wainscot. Traps in the
pantry; a boy is killed. The Moot in the Small House. The sink floods. Warmth, and the windowsill gardens flower for
the first time. The factions: the Court wants recognition, the Winders want to serve, the Jarmen want her dead.
The Readers find the diary.

**Act III, The Smoking.** Pye buys sulphur candles to fumigate the house on the eighth night, and brings the
surveyor; the Jarl's poison is in the teacup at ten past four on the sixth day; the Lady is dying. Contact, and a
will, or not.

## Endings (first version)

The Freehold (a will witnessed, the deed found: the house is the Lodgers' in law); the Last Game (she knew them, but
the sale goes through); Sold; Poisoned; the Smoking; the House Goes Cold (she never knew, and nothing changed);
the Ratcatcher (the protagonist did what they were hired for).

## Smaller adventures (first version)

The Undelivered (the giant's letter in the drift); the Slow Concert; Ten Past Four (the Winders' schism); the Drip
(the Tapwardens' missing ration); Fennick's desertion; the Jarl's son; the Hearth refugees; Volume M and the diary;
the Owl in the attic; the Salvager's commission; the Thaw-feast on the sill; Belling the Cat; Mrs Pye's ledger; the
vault.

## Why a player would want to be here

Because every ordinary thing has become a country. Because a lit fire is the most frightening event in a town's
history. Because the people have made something beautiful and absurd out of what was left lying around, and know
it. Because there is a dying woman upstairs who once loved them, and does not remember whether she did.
