# 03 — Severe editorial criticism, and the design as revised

*Stage 2. The treatment read as a demanding editor and an RPG designer would read it, before any Kyr. Each finding
says what is wrong and what was changed.*

## E1. "Little people" whimsy is the gravest danger

The treatment says the Lodgers are not little people, and then furnishes them with a Thaw-feast, a Granny Mote, and
thimbles. Every scale-joke is a joke the reader has heard (a thimble cup, a button coin). The cure is not to remove
the scale but to make every use of a giant's thing **cost something or settle something**.

*Revised:* buttons are coin because they are the one thing in the house that is counted and cannot be made; the
Button Market is where the Wainscot's economy is visibly scarce. Pins are spears because the Jarmen hold the
pincushion. The doll's furniture is an aristocracy's title. Nothing scale-funny appears without a social or practical
consequence, and the narrator never winks.

## E2. Fourteen minor stories, half of them thin

Several are encounters dressed as stories: the Owl in the attic (an animal, no person, no question), the Drip (a
missing-ration investigation with an obvious answer), the Thaw-feast (a mood), the garden (a biome). Fourteen spread
over a house is fourteen sketches.

*Revised:* **cut** the Attic, the Owl, the Garden glasshouse and the Tapwardens' investigation. The Drip survives as
a place with a warden who matters in the Hearth story (refugees need water; Drewe will not ration for strangers; the
Lady's turning the tap is a flood). The Sill survives as livelihood (work for pay in the first sun in three hundred
years), not a quest. The remaining stories each get a person, a question, two or more approaches, a turn and a
consequence, written into the grid in `07-adventures.md` before building.

## E3. The diary is the misread inheritance, again

The treatment's Act II has the Readers *find the diary*, which tells the truth about the founding, which is then shown
to people. That is the collection's one story, the seventh time.

*Revised:* the Lodgers have always known their founding (the Little Court tells it at every Moot; it is why they
are a court). Nothing in the house is misread. **The mystery is in the Lady, not in an artifact**: she was told
she imagined them and believed it. What she does not know cannot be shown to her by an exhibit, because she has spent
three hundred years distrusting exactly that kind of evidence. Contact is a relationship to be risked, not a proof to
be delivered.

## E4. The Lady is passive; she needs a want of her own

In the treatment she lights fires and dies. She is an object of the plot.

*Revised:* **she is making her last chart.** Her charts are famous; her map of the world has one blank, Hollins.
She has come home to survey her own house from memory before she dies, and works at it every morning at a table in
the Library by the window, with Pye pacing out rooms for her. That gives her a daily routine with real consequences
(where she walks, what she lights, what she asks Pye to measure), an inner life (exactness as a defence against the
mother who called her a liar), and **the adventure's central contact: someone can correct her chart in the night.**
A mapmaker who wakes to find the walls of her house drawn in, in a hand an eighth the size of hers, has to decide
what she believes. That is a scene the engine can play, and that no exhibit replaces.

The diary survives as one of several gifts, not the key.

## E5. The ratcatcher must have teeth on both sides

If the job is simply evil, the choice is trivial: nobody will kill the Lodgers.

*Revised:* the pantry has real rats, which really eat the Jarmen's stores; killing them is real work the Wainscot
would pay for too. Pye pays by the tail, two pence each. Hob Tallis in Bootscrape sells rat-tails by the barrel to
every ratcatcher who has ever come, and so the protagonist can cheat Pye honestly. Pye's traps are real and maim
people: springing or carrying them off is the first good act available, and Pye notices her traps empty. The one
thing the protagonist can do that is wholly wrong, **show Pye where the Wainscot is**, is offered, and pays well.

## E6. Too many factions

Court, Winders, Readers, Jarmen, Colliers, Tapwardens, Post, Guild, Regiment, Under-folk, Risers. A player cannot hold
eleven.

*Revised:* four that **want something of the Lady**: the Little Court (recognition), the Winders (to serve), the
Jarmen (her death), the Colliers (to survive her fires). The rest are places with a keeper, a livelihood and a story
of their own (the Post, the Keys, the Regiment, the Under-folk), and the Risers are one woman with a ladder.

## E7. The endings are a menu

Seven named endings with a condition each is the table the survey complained of.

*Revised:* the end comes on the tenth night, when she dies, whatever anyone does; or earlier, if she is poisoned.
What it says is composed from what has happened: whether she knew them, whether a will was made and witnessed,
whether the deed was found, whether the Smoking happened and who got out, whether the protagonist sold them. The
named endings remain (the engine closes on a title), but each is a different *state of the house*, and they are
written in order from the most particular.

## E8. There is no romance with a person in it

A letter from a dead love, delivered to a dying woman, is beautiful, and it is an elegy, not a romance.

*Revised:* Conception A comes in as a minor story. Kit Lacey, the Chatelaine's nephew, and Nan Collier, the
forewoman's daughter in the grate, have courted for two years by notes passed along the servants' bell-wires and have
never been in a room together. The fire brings Nan up as a refugee to the house Kit's aunt rules. The letter is kept
as the Lady's own story, and the giant who wrote it is left on the moor, perhaps a stone.

## E9. The cat must be a fear, not a set-piece

*Revised:* the Bishop hunts the ground floor at night, every night. Anyone on the open floors after dark meets it
unless it is belled or elsewhere. It cannot sensibly be fought; it can be avoided, distracted (catmint from the
kitchen garden), or belled, which makes the house safer at night for everyone thereafter, and requires climbing onto
the Lady's bed while it sleeps there.

## E10. Where does combat belong?

*Revised:* the pantry rats (an honest fight, and the job); the Jarl's bravos (fight, face down, or talk down); the
Tin Regiment, whose old orders make them attack whoever they take for Mother's people (dangerous in numbers;
avoidable by countersign; ended by new orders); the cistern eel in the cellar (optional, guards the way to the vault
by water); the Bishop (not a fight).

## E11. The Moot must decide something

*Revised:* the Moot on the third day is an Occasion in the Small House with three matters: the refugees, the Lady,
the cat. What the company says, and whom it has brought (Bet, Sorrel, the Prior, the Jarl), shifts the votes, and
the decisions change what the Wainscot does: whether the Court opens the Small House to the Colliers; whether the
Wainscot hides, flees or tries to make itself known; whether anyone is told off to bell the cat.

## E12. Integration with Imbar

Giants are established (Thrume's fells, the Long Man's king under the ridge), but a giant gentry with pianos and
portraits is new. *Kept, and flagged in friction.md* as a canon question: I place the Whitlocks as the last of an
old giant gentry of the western hills, now nearly gone, and make no claim about giants elsewhere. The Whitlock charts
are a claim about the wider world, kept vague (harbour-masters have them) so that nothing contradicts them.

## E13. Tone

The treatment is in danger of being wistful throughout. *Revised:* the Little Court is comedy (protocol for a
meeting that never came; a herald who has rehearsed for forty years); the Winders are theological farce with a
saboteur in it; Pye is satire of the servant who identifies upward, and then is not a villain; the pantry is
violence; the cat is horror; the Sill is plain happiness; the Lady's bedroom is tragedy.

## The design as revised (what will be built)

1. **Bootscrape**, outside: the inn, the salvager, the chandler, the notary; the moor and the Waiting Stone.
2. **The house**: Hall (Slot, Letterdrift, Clock, Stair, Wainscot), Drawing Room (Carpet, Hearth, Piano, tea
   table), Library (Readers, the Lady's chart table), Kitchen (Range, Sink, Pantry), Cellar (Under-folk, cistern,
   vault), Nursery (Small House, Fort), Bedroom (bed, bedside table, Pye's hatbox), and the kitchen garden by the
   coal-hole.
3. **Acts**: I The Ratcatcher (days 1–2), II The Thaw (days 2–6), III The Smoking (days 6–10).
4. **The Lady's routine** on her own clock: chart in the morning, fires, tea at ten past four (the hour the
   carriage left), the Clock wound at dusk, bed. Pye's on hers.
5. **Contact** by several routes, each with a price: the corrected chart; the Slow Concert; the letter from the
   drift; the diary; being seen, by daring; through Pye.
6. **Minor stories** (each with person, question, approaches, turn, consequence): the Ratcatcher's wages; the
   Hearth; Kit and Nan; Ten Past Four; the Slow Concert; the Undelivered; Belling the Cat; the Tin Regiment; the
   Jarl's son; Pye's ledger; the Vault; Sorrel; the Salvager's sovereign.
7. **Endings** composed from state, closing on the tenth night.
