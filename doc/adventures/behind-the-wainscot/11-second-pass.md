# 11 — The second pass: read as a finished work, and what it changed

*Stage 6. The adventure read again, after it played end to end in every ending, as a book and as a game rather than
as a build; then revised where it most deserved it. What is still wrong is at the end, plainly.*

## What I was not satisfied with

**1. Sorrel wanted one thing, and never got it.** Her whole character is "I want to write back". The first build used
her as an instrument (she wrote the chart, she wrote the will) and never let her speak to the Lady. A companion whose
wish the plot satisfies only on her behalf is furniture.
*Revised:* once the Lady knows, Sorrel can be let speak to her, and she does not ask any of the great questions she has
carried for twenty years: she asks what comes after Mandragora in Volume M. The Lady laughs and tells her why the
article on Manners is wrong. Asked afterwards, Sorrel leaves the company to stay at the chart table with her broom and
write down what the Lady says. The epilogue: the first book in Hollins written by anyone who lived in its walls. She
changes; the Readers change with her.

**2. After the reversal, the Lady was a menu.** Once she knew, she did what topics told her: lift the traps, forbid the
sulphur, bell the cat. She did nothing in front of the Wainscot, and the house did not change to look at.
*Revised:* the first dusk after she knows, she winds the Clock and does not go back to the fire: she stands in the Hall
with her candle and speaks to the skirting. If she has been told the name of the boy her cat killed she says it, and
the first person to come out onto the flagstones is his mother, alone, with her fists shut; and the Lady kneels down on
the floor of her own Hall to see her. From then on people cross the Hall in daylight. Mab Tenter, asked why she went
out first: "It doesn't stop it. I said I wanted it to stop. It doesn't stop. But it's been said." Her "I want it to
stop" and the Lady's "make it stop" (said when she glimpses someone on the floor) were already the same sentence; now
the two women meet over it.

**3. The doll's house was a court and never a doll's house.** The work's founding image (a toy that is exactly a house)
was never seen from the outside, from the size it was made at.
*Revised:* she can be asked to come up to the Nursery. The Court lights every candle in the Small House; she kneels
and puts her face to the parlour window, as she did at eight; Dame Honor, inside, raises a cup that fits her hand.
"You have kept it all just as it was." "No. We have mended everything. Twice." That line is, I think, the best in the
work, and the work's argument in four words: inheritance is not kept, it is mended.

**4. The Prior and Fennick asked for something and were never answered.** *Revised:* the Prior is told that she said it
is today ("That is not the same thing, you know. It is better.") and goes for a rope to make a winding team; Fennick,
told the war is over, says he had thought there was only winding and not winding, and asks to be wound as far as the
sea, not further.

**5. Replies that narrated both histories.** "If you warned them she says…, and if you did not…" is the author
apologising for not having written two replies. Nan on the Hearth, the Lady on the fire and on the traps are now each
two topics, one for each world; the fire's dead are named to the Lady one by one, and she stops at Bet.

**6. The Lady's tics, and mine.** "The voice of a woman who has given orders to harbour-masters" three times; "for a
long time" twenty-nine times; something "the size of" or "as big as" something seventy times. Scale is the work's one
law and its one figure, and repetition makes the reader stop seeing it. Trimmed by a third; the harbour-masters kept
once, where she first speaks to the walls of her Library.

**7. The cat was a dice-roll, then a death sentence.** As first built, the unbelled Bishop fell on anyone on the open
floor at night without a word and killed them in a blow; a failed attempt to bell him started a fight to the death. The
first is unfair (no choice); the second is untrue (cats play). *Revised:* he warns, once, with two green lamps opening
in the dark, and you have a moment; a failed belling is being batted across the counterpane like a cork and dropped off
the bed while he settles and begins, enormously, to purr. Belled, he still hunts, and the Wainscot steps into its
doorways as the bell goes by: the house is safe at night, which is what the fable promised.

**8. A player in the browser never saw the hook.** See friction F3 and F4: the notice on the inn door, the colliers in
the grate, and "Talk to the Lady" before she could be talked to. Revised in the module.

**9. A charmless protagonist could not save the Hearth.** The only argument to Bet without the job was a stranger's
nerves. *Revised:* asking Jory why Bootscrape is not glad gives you the Lady's bedroom fire, every night; tell Bet that
and it is an easy roll. The cold cleric's run then saved the Hearth.

## Is it distinctive?

I think so, and in the respect that mattered most when I chose it: nothing in Hollins is misread. The Wainscot knows
its founding; the diary is a fair copy on a shelf; the mechanism of the house is a giant's ordinary housekeeping. What
the player does is not decode but **write** (on her chart), **play** (her song), **carry** (her letters), **step out**
(in front of her), and **choose who to be to her**: her ratcatcher, her thief, her companion's confessor, her scribe.
Its catastrophes are kindnesses done by someone who does not know who is there.

## Does it have dramatic form?

Yes, in three movements that change the house physically: cold and dim with a stopped clock; warm and burning, with a
clock that strikes; and then either smoke, or open shutters, or the cold coming back into the walls. The reversal is
in the middle, where it should be, and it is a person's, not an artifact's: the morning she finds WE LIVE HERE and
sends her companion out of the room. The last night is fixed and known from the first (she came home to die), and the
question is only what she will know when it comes.

## Tone

Comedy (the Moot, Mr Ambry's forty-year Address, Faith Penny's postage due on a letter to oneself, the Winders'
theology, Ottoline Fogg's economics); horror (the cat's two lamps, the sulphur sinking like water, a scream before
light); satire (a servant our size who looks down on the vermin); plain happiness (lettuces on the sill, colliers
cheering in the spray from a giant's tap); and grief, which is the ground note.

## What tenfold ambition would mean, and the beginnings of it here

Not ten times the rooms. It would mean:
- **The house itself remembering**: every room of Hollins an archive of a giant family's life that the Wainscot has
  read as scripture and the Lady can read as memory. The two readings set side by side, in conversation, at every
  shelf. Begun with the Small House window and Volume M; not done for the portraits, the playing card at the Mat, the
  newspaper King Aldo.
- **The Wainscot as a polity that argues after she knows**: whether to accept a giant's will at all, since a freehold
  makes six hundred people owners and creditors and taxpayers overnight. Ottoline Fogg would have views. Not built.
- **Corwen**: whether the stone is a giant. I have kept it a stone, on purpose. A tenfold version would let the
  company walk up the moor on the last night and find something different from what it expects, and still not know.
- **Origins**: a hill-country origin whose grandmother sold pins out of the Slot, for whom Bootscrape's silence is
  family loyalty, and the Lady's house the thing they were raised never to name.

## What is still wrong

- **The middle week can be empty.** A player who reaches the Lady by day three has a week until she dies with the will,
  the vault and the Smoking to do, and a good deal of waiting. The house does not change enough on days five to nine.
  The Concern's surveyor measures nothing; Mr Overy should be seen on the flagstones with his chain.
- **Mrs Pyecroft's turning is too available.** Any of four ways turns her. Her being right about one thing (the Lady
  never asked her anything) is the most interesting fact in the work about service, and it is resolved in a topic.
- **The Jarl's arc depends on what the player did on day two.** Save Wat and Hasp is a man who pays for rats; the most
  dramatic character in the house becomes an innkeeper. That is the point (prevention prevents), but it means the
  "best" playthrough meets the least of him.
- **The Under-folk are a quest and not yet a people.** Forty-one in the dark, one elder, one request. They needed one
  more person and one more scene.
- **Kit and Nan are sweet and slight.** They meet, he stands up at the Moot, they marry in the epilogue. The romance
  never costs either of them anything.
- **The protagonist is not changed.** The Lady draws you, in the end ("There. Drawn."), and it is the only moment in
  which the protagonist is looked at. A protagonist with a past, an origin that the house could answer, would make it
  the Lady's story and theirs.
- **Tested is not enjoyed.** No one but the author has played it.
