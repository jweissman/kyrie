# 01 — Five competing conceptions

*Stage 1. Written before any Kyr, 31 October 2026, after reading the repertoire survey
(`doc/adventures/repertoire-2026-10-26.md`), the atlas, every country in `countries.json`, and the READMEs of the
adventure branches not yet on main (the Grand Tour, the Quiet in the Sky, Fair Weather, the Big Tent).*

## What the collection already has, and what it lacks

Already written: a dam read as a barrow; a titan read as fells; a dream-house that grows; a held star; a mountain
weighted by knowing; an oath and a war-loan; a hymn that is a program; a talking library; a city of three storeys and
the elemental courts; the warrior dead of Ysgard who have stopped fighting; a valley open one day in seven; a frozen
river fair; a honeycomb town; a penance market; a wreckers' reach; hot springs and banns; a circus.

The repertoire survey's diagnosis, which I accept: one story told seven times (an outsider reads a misread inheritance
and shows people the truth before a deadline); minds changed only by exhibits; endings that go down through the floor
into the planes; a protagonist who wants nothing, is guilty of nothing and is loved by no one. Missing: wanting; being
at fault; a person as an end; making something; comedy as a structure; fear that lasts; a story that stays small.

So my test for each conception below: does its central situation require a *decision about a person*, rather than a
*reading of an artifact*? Can the protagonist be at fault in it? Is it strange in its material, social or moral laws,
not only in its scenery?

---

## A. The Embassy of Hours (a city of two shifts)

A salt-mining city where, by a law three centuries old, half the people live by day and half by night, sharing the
same houses, beds, tools and offices, and never meeting. Day-folk and Night-folk communicate by notes left on
pillows; marriages are arranged between people who share a bed in shifts and have never seen each other. The law's
reason is lost; the mines must never stop. A Night clerk and a Day clerk who share a desk have been writing to each
other for nine years and have fallen in love, and the law of the shift says they may not meet. Meanwhile the mine is
failing and the city must decide whether to end the shifts.

*Promise:* romance through letters, a social law played seriously, a city with two faces of every place. *Weakness:*
the shift's lost reason is the misread inheritance again; the strangeness is a custom, not a world; the dramatic
engine collapses toward "end the law or keep it". Strong as a minor story, thin as a region.

## B. The Long Lien (a country owned by its dead)

Ghostwater: a fen town where the dead remain citizens, keeping their votes and their property for as long as they
are remembered. The living are a taxed minority who rent everything from their great-grandparents. Remembrance is the
economy (the rich dead pay the living to say their names), and the forgotten dead go hungry and dangerous. A young
woman wants to marry, and cannot, because her house belongs to a grandmother who will never pass it on.

*Promise:* sharp satire of property and gerontocracy; a moral order profoundly unlike Imbar's. *Weakness:* the
Quiet in the Sky has just made the dead its subject, and the Third Night and What Tessary Owes both turn on the dead
and debts. The satire would be legible in an afternoon and then repeat itself.

## C. The Lashings (a nation that is a raft)

A people of a thousand boats lashed together, drifting a slow gyre round a southern sea, landing nowhere. Districts
vote by lashing and secede by cutting loose; a quarrel can end with half a city drifting away at dusk. The Lashings
are being carried toward the doldrums and must decide whether to divide.

*Promise:* a geography that is politics; a city that moves. *Weakness:* the engine's places do not move, so the
central image would be told rather than played; and Skeel, Fair Weather and the Grand Tour have sea-cities enough.

## D. The Hush (a country where speaking aloud is dangerous)

A wold whose people speak by whistle, hand and knotted cord, because something in the hills hunts by voice. The
protagonist arrives talking. Conversation, the game's main verb, becomes the danger.

*Promise:* lasting fear; a culture formed by a material law; language learned from context. *Weakness:* it is a
mechanic before it is a story; nearly every exchange would need a bespoke rule, and the engine's conversation
model would fight it at every line. It also tends to horror only. I would rather keep it for a minor story elsewhere.

## E. Behind the Wainscot (a giant's house, and the people living in its walls)

Hollins is a house in the western hills built by the last of the giant gentry, twelve times the size of ours: a hall
whose flagstones are each a threshing-floor, a staircase of cliffs, a library whose books are walked rather than
read. It has stood empty for three hundred years with its door shut, and for three hundred years people have been
coming in through its letterbox: the famine-poor of the hills, debtors, runaways, colliers from a flooded pit, a
heretic who wanted a quiet place to wind a clock. Six hundred of them live in it now, in the space behind the
skirting, in the cold grate, in the pantry among jars of honey that have not spoiled, and in the doll's house in the
nursery, which, being made at a twelfth of a giant's size, is exactly the size of a house.

Three days ago the giantess came home. Miss Ermengarde Whitlock is three hundred and twenty, nearly blind, and dying,
and she has come back to the house she was sent away from as a child. She has lit the fires. Her companion, a
human-sized woman called Mrs Pye, has seen droppings and gnawed candles and has put up a notice in the village on the
doorstep: WANTED, A RATCATCHER.

The protagonist answers it.

*Promise:*
- **A strange material law that is entirely physical: scale.** Everything familiar becomes geography: a dripping
  tap is a river with wardens, a doormat a field, a lit fire a disaster, a giant's cat a monster that cannot be fought.
- **Warmth is apocalypse.** For three hundred years the house has been cold and dim. Her return is the first summer
  the Lodgers have known, and it is destroying them: the fire burns the colliers out of the grate, the tap floods the
  sink-folk, the traps maim children.
- **A person as an end.** At the centre is not a mechanism but a dying old woman who was told as a child that she
  had imagined the people she hid, and who believed it. She became the most exact cartographer in Imbar because she
  could no longer trust her own eyes. Her map of the world has one blank: her home.
- **The protagonist can be at fault from the first scene.** They were hired to kill the people they find. Pye pays
  by the tail.
- **Comedy as structure.** A court in a doll's house with three centuries of protocol for addressing a giant who
  never came; clock-priests whose theology turns on which hand of a stopped clock is which; a council that agrees
  unanimously to bell the cat, and then looks round for a volunteer.
- **Fear that lasts.** The Bishop, her cat, eighteen feet of grey fur, which hunts at night and plays with what it
  catches. Footfalls that shake the floor. A sulphur fumigation ordered for the eighth night.
- **A story that stays small and comes out into the world.** It ends in a bedroom, with a will or without one. Its
  persistent consequence is legal, not cosmic: whether the first freehold of the Lodgers is entered in the county
  court, or the house is quarried for stone.
- **Nothing to descend to.** There is a cellar, and a vault in it with a deed, but no mechanism under the floor and
  no plane behind the wall. The Kiritarun appear nowhere.

*Weakness to watch:* the Borrowers, Gulliver, and every story of little people. The difference must be held to
firmly: **the Lodgers are not little people**. They are ordinary Imbar folk, of the protagonist's own size, who have
made a society in an inheritance that is not theirs and to which they have no title. It is a story about sanctuary,
squatting and belonging, told through scale.

---

## Selection

E. It is the only conception whose central situation is a decision about a person; the only one in which the
protagonist's first act can be a wrong; the only one that turns the engine's most ordinary vocabulary (a room, a
door, a fire, a tap, a cat) into the extraordinary without adding a single new law of nature; and the one I would
most want to explore if I knew nothing of its plot. The house is a dungeon with reasons for every room, a city with
institutions shaped by its material circumstance, and a deathbed.

A survives as a minor thread in E (two Lodgers who have only ever met by letter). D survives as one custom (the
Stillness: when the house shakes, everyone freezes). B's satire of title and inheritance survives in E's last act,
turned the other way: here the living have no title, and the question is whether the dying owner will give it.
