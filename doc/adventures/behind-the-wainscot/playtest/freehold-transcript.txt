What she says. The Readers read; somebody should write.’ She hugs you, hard, and leaves ink on you. ‘Come and see me. I’ll be at the table. I’ll be the one with the broom.’ Sorrel Haddow goes her own way.
[Wait › An hour] At noon the Lady, at the kitchen table, gets up and goes to the sink and turns the tap; and the water comes down out of the brass like a river out of a cliff, white, roaring, and fills the Sink in the time it takes to say so. When she has gone back to her bread the Sink-folk come up out of it onto the draining board, coughing, and count each other, and come out two short.
[Wait › An hour] Now the house is warm all through, and the Lady sleeps more and works less, and Mrs Pyecroft goes about with her lips pressed together. In the Wainscot people have begun to pack, without saying so: a bundle by the door, a child’s shoes laced together. Everyone can feel that something is coming to an end, and nobody can say what.
[Wait › An hour] An hour passes. It is a long time since you slept. You are weary, and will fight the worse for it until you sleep.
[To the chart table] The top of the chart table: a plain of green baize, and spread across most of it, held down at the corners with books, a sheet of paper as big as a field: a chart, in ink, of a house. Under her question, in the big black hand: THE LACEYS’ PEOPLE, AND ALL WHO CAME AFTER. WE ARE STILL HERE. And under that, in hers, nothing yet. Here are Sorrel Haddow, a Reader of the Library; the Lady; and Mrs Pyecroft, the Lady’s companion.
[Talk to Mrs Pyecroft] Mrs Pyecroft looks at you as she would look at a bill she has already paid once.
[Ask Mrs Pyecroft what she will want of you next] ‘The night of the 8th,’ says Mrs Pyecroft. ‘When she has had her drops and is asleep. I have sulphur. I shall light it in the Hall, the drawing room and the kitchen, and shut the doors, and the smoke will go down into every crack in this house where they breed.’ She nods, briskly. ‘I shall want you to help with the doors. They are heavy.’
[Tell the Lady what Mrs Pyecroft means to do with the sulphur] The Lady goes grey. ‘Sulphur,’ she says. ‘In the walls.’ She sits with her hand flat on the table for a long time. ‘I told her to make it stop,’ she says. ‘I said, whatever you have to do.’ She closes her eyes. ‘Then I have been the monster under the bed, all this week, in my own house.’ And then, opening them: ‘There will be no sulphur. Not while I live, and I shall put it in writing for after.’
[Wait › An hour] An hour passes. Wren gains 150 experience: kept the Smoking from the Wainscot.
[Wait › An hour] On the tenth evening the Lady did not ring for her supper, and Mrs Pyecroft came down the ribbon from her hatbox and sat on the counterpane by her hand, and did not go back up. The house knows. The Clock goes on. The fire in her room has been let burn low.
[To the foot of the Stair] The foot of the Stair: the first step is a cliff of polished oak eight feet high, and the next one above it, and the next, going up into the dimness in a flight of twenty-two. Ma Riser, who keeps the ladders on the Stair, is here.
[To the first-floor landing by the Riser ladders up the Stair] You leave the foot of the Stair late, with the light already going yellow. The first-floor landing is a gallery of polished boards as wide as a road, with a banister along it like a fence of trees, and the Stair dropping away below into the dimness of the Hall.
[To the floor of the Lady's room] The Lady’s room. A fire burns in the grate, and has burned since she came; it has been the warmest room in Hollins since she came, and the warmth is extraordinary after the Hall, like walking into a loaf. The bed is a hill of white linen under a canopy, the bedside table beside it on tall legs, and on the dressing table a hatbox stands with a little door cut in its side and a curtain at a window. Along the mantel, in frames, maps: coasts, harbours, a whole sea, drawn so finely that the waves are lines of figures.
[To the counterpane] The counterpane is a white country of quilted hills and valleys, warm, smelling of lavender and old woman. At its head the pillows rise like snowfields. Across the foot of the bed, in a heap of grey fur as big as a haystack, the Bishop lies curled: eighteen feet of cat, his flank going up and down. A silver bell hangs from his collar now, and rings, faintly, whenever he breathes. Under the counterpane the Lady sleeps, a range of hills that rises and falls; her breath goes over you slow and warm and with a catch in it, every so often, like a door that sticks. Her face on the pillow is the size of a cottage, and very old, and in sleep it looks frightened. Here are the Bishop, the Lady’s cat, eighteen feet long; the Lady; and Mrs Pyecroft, the Lady’s companion.
[Wait › A while] Time passes. They come up the Stair in the dark, all of them who will: the Court in its good gloves, the colliers, the Readers, the Keys, Faith Penny in her green cap, the Winders with a candle each, Ma Riser carrying the old ones up her ladders one by one. They stand on the floor of the room, and on the bedside table, and along the foot of the bed, very quiet, six hundred people the size of the Lady’s thumb. She cannot see them. She can hear them breathing. ‘Oh,’ she says. ‘All of you.’
[Sit with her on the counterpane, by her hand, until the end] You sit on the counterpane by her hand, which is as big as a boat and as light on the linen as a leaf. She talks for a while, not much, in a voice you have to lean close to: about a coast in the north where the cliffs are white and the sea comes in green; about Honor Lacey, who could whistle through her teeth; about her mother. Then she asks you to stand where she can see you, in the candle, and you do; and she looks at you a long time, exactly, the way she looked at every coast in Imbar. ‘There,’ she says. ‘Drawn.’ And she closes her eyes.
[Wait › An hour] Sorrel Haddow sat at the chart table every day of the last week with her broom, and wrote down what the Lady said, a yard high; the Readers have bound it, and it is the first book in Hollins written by anyone who lived in its walls. The Tin Regiment stands in its Fort where it stopped, every soldier at his post, the Captain’s sword pointing at the door. Nobody winds them. Fennick sits in the ark. Forty-one names are entered in the Post’s register under a new heading, in Faith Penny’s hand: CAME IN BY THE COAL-HOLE. Drewe gives them their thimbles. The Long Clock goes on striking. The Winders wind it now, every Sunday, forty turns, with a team on the key, because she said it was today, and so it is. She died on the tenth night, a little before midnight, with six hundred people on her floor. Mr Saltash proved her will in the county court at Wendle in the spring, against a cousin, the Hollins Stone Concern, and a good deal of laughter, and won, with the deed on the table in front of the judge like a rolled carpet: the house and lands of Hollins, and all in it, to the people who live in its walls, in common, for ever. It is entered in the rolls of the county as the first freehold ever held in Imbar by people living inside a building that belongs to them. In Hollins the shutters are open. The Slot is a door. The Lady’s chart hangs in the Hall, framed, as big as a field, with the Wainscot drawn into it in a big black hand, every lane in its place; and in the margin, in hers, under WE LIVE HERE: SO YOU DO. E. W.
