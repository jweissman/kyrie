[To Bootscrape street] Bootscrape lives on a giant’s doorstep and makes a living from what falls out of the letterbox. It has done so for three hundred years without once telling anyone from outside what it knows. Four days ago a wagon the size of an inn came over the moor, and a woman as tall as a church tower got down from it and walked up the step and put a key in the door, and since then the village has been very quiet, and has watched the chimney. On the door of the inn, the Mat, a notice in a clear, pinched hand: WANTED, A RATCATCHER, FOR HOLLINS, TWO CROWNS THE DAY, APPLY WITHIN, TEN UNTIL TWO. Under that: THE HOUSE BEING OVERRUN; TWOPENCE THE TAIL. D. PYECROFT. Somebody has drawn a very small face in the corner of it, with its finger to its lips. Bootscrape street runs along the foot of the doorstep, under the scraper: an iron arch thirty feet high, rusted to the colour of a fox, with a forge built into one leg of it and washing hung from the crossbar. The houses are built of the ordinary stone of the hills, and roofed, a good many of them, with things that are not slates: a sheet of blackened brass, half a horn button the size of a millwheel, a shard of green glass that throws a green light into somebody’s kitchen. A rope ladder hangs down the face of the step to the lip, sixty feet up, where the brass of the letterbox shines.
[Into the Mat] The Mat is called after the thing it stands on, or stood on once: its floor is an edge of the giant’s doormat, coir as thick as a man’s wrist, grown over with moss and beaten flat by three centuries of boots. A fire, settles, the smell of beer and wet dog. Over the fireplace, framed, hangs a giant’s playing card, the six of cups, as big as a door. Jory Mudge, who keeps the Mat, is here.
[Talk to Jory Mudge] A big soft man with flour on his forearms looks up from the bread. ‘Come in off the moor. You’ll have seen it, then,’ he says, and nods at the wall, meaning what is beyond it. ‘Everybody stops on the road the first time. Sit down. Ale’s a penny and the bread’s my own.’
[Ask Jory Mudge about the house] ‘Hollins,’ says Jory. ‘The Whitlocks built it. Tall folk, giant gentry, the last of them west of the march, and the last of the last went away when my great-great-grandad’s grandad was a boy, and it’s stood shut since.’ He wipes his hands. ‘We live off its doorstep. Off what falls out of it, you might say.’ He does not say how a shut house lets things fall out of it. ‘And now she’s come home. Miss Whitlock. Four days back, in a wagon you could have put this inn in, with a cat on the seat beside her the size of a bull. Walked up the step, put a key the length of my arm in the door, and went in. The chimney’s smoked since.’ He looks at the wall again. ‘First smoke in three hundred years. You’d think we’d be glad.’
[Ask Jory Mudge why Bootscrape is not glad] Jory Mudge looks at you a long moment, deciding something. Then he leans on the bread. ‘You’re going in,’ he says. ‘Everybody who stops on the road goes in, sooner or later. So I’ll tell you what we tell nobody, and you’ll keep it like we keep it.’ He lowers his voice, though there is nobody near. ‘There’s folk in there. Our size. Living in it. Six hundred, they say. In the walls, and the grate, and the doll’s house in the nursery. Came in by the letterbox, starving, three hundred years of them. We trade with them, out of the Slot: their pins and buttons for our bread and needles.’ He straightens up. ‘And now she’s home, and she’s lit the fire, and her woman wants a ratcatcher.’
[Take your leave] ‘Off you go, then,’ says Jory Mudge, ‘and don’t do anything I’d be ashamed to hear about. Or do, and tell me.’

[Wait › An hour] At the corner table, with her back to the wall and a ledger open in front of her, sits a small upright woman in black with a pen behind her ear. A little line of men waits on the settle with sacks and terriers: ratcatchers, come down off the moor for two crowns a day. None of them looks as if he means to take it. An hour passes.
[Talk to Mrs Pyecroft] A small upright woman in black, with a pen behind her ear and a face that has been patient for a long time on purpose, looks at you as she would look at a bill. ‘Yes?’
[Ask Mrs Pyecroft about the position] Mrs Pyecroft looks you over, all of you, boots to hat, as if she were pricing you. ‘Hollins is infested,’ she says. ‘I have found droppings on the stair, candle-ends gnawed, and in the nursery a doll’s bed that has been slept in, which, if you have ever kept house, you will know means rats, and bold ones.’ She taps the ledger. ‘Two crowns a day, and found, and twopence a tail, which I shall count. I want the pantry cleared and the skirtings, and I want to know where they nest. Miss Whitlock is not well. She hears them in the walls at night, and she thinks,’ and here Mrs Pyecroft stops, and goes on in a different voice, ‘she thinks it is her mind. It is not her mind. It is rats. I want it to be rats.’
[Take Mrs Pyecroft's position as ratcatcher of Hollins] ‘Good,’ says Mrs Pyecroft, and writes your name in the ledger, spelt as she hears it. She pushes a canvas bag across the table: two spring-traps, a tin of paste, a pair of gauntlets. ‘The traps go where they run. The paste goes on bread. Don’t eat the bread.’ She counts four coins onto the table. ‘Two days in hand. I shall want tails, and I shall want the nest.’ She closes the ledger. ‘You come in by the Slot, like everybody. I’ll tell the Lady there’s a ratcatcher in the house, so she does not tread on you.’ You take a pair of spring-traps. You take a tin of arsenic paste.
[Ask Mrs Pyecroft about the drawing-room fire] ‘I laid it myself last night,’ says Mrs Pyecroft. ‘She sits in that room, and it is perishing; three hundred years of cold in the walls. I shall light it at nine, when she goes through to the Library.’ She looks at you. ‘Why?’
[Tell Mrs Pyecroft where the vermin nest: behind the Hall skirting, and in the doll's house] Mrs Pyecroft writes it down: behind the skirting of the Hall, along its whole length; the grate; the doll’s house. Her pen does not hesitate. ‘The doll’s house,’ she says. ‘I knew it. The bed slept in.’ She blots the page and counts ten coins onto the table. ‘That is worth more to me than any number of tails.’ Then she looks up, sharply, as if something in your face had told her more than you said. ‘Rats,’ she says. ‘Yes? Rats.’ And waits for you to say it.
[Take your leave] ‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Mrs Pyecroft, who was not trying to.

Bootscrape street runs along the foot of the giant’s doorstep, under the iron arch of the scraper, with the rope ladder hanging down the face of the step to the brass of the letterbox sixty feet up.
[To the top of the doorstep] The top of the doorstep is a plain of grey stone sixty feet above Bootscrape, worn into a hollow in the middle by the feet of giants. The door rises out of it, black oak, studded, going up into the sky; the keyhole above you is a cave. At the height of your head is the letterbox, a brass mouth a yard long with its flap wedged open by a chunk of wood, and a smell coming out of it of paper, dust and candles.
[Into the house by the Slot] Inside, the house is a country. It is cold, as it has been cold for three hundred years, except where she has lit fires, and dim, except where she has opened shutters; and everywhere, in the walls, in the grate, on the shelves, the people who came in by the letterbox are going about their lives without a sound, listening, as they have listened every day since Thursday, for the floor to shake. Inside the brass mouth of the letterbox, a ledge, and a rope ladder going down into a dimness that smells of old paper. Somebody has painted on the brass, small and neat, POST OFFICE OF THE WAINSCOT. ALL COMERS TO REPORT. And under it, smaller: MIND THE DROP.
[To the Letterdrift] Below the letterbox lies the Letterdrift: three hundred years of letters, fallen through the slot onto the hall floor and never picked up, heaped into a dune as high as a barn and as long as a street. The letters are a giant’s letters, each the size of a bedsheet, folded; their seals are cartwheels of red wax. Paths go over and into the drift, and in it people have dug rooms, roofed with envelopes, lit by candle-ends, with addresses for walls: MISS E. WHITLOCK, HOLLINS; THE LADY WHITLOCK, BY HAND; and a great many bills. In the face of the drift a counter has been built out of a giant’s postcard on trestles. Pigeonholes are cut into the paper behind it, labelled in a clear hand: NEWS, OLD; NEWS, OLDER; LOVE; MONEY; DEATHS; ILLEGIBLE. Faith Penny, the Postmistress of the Wainscot, is here.
[To the Skirting] Behind the skirting board, in the dark between the board and the wall, a street. The board is the street’s one side, a cliff of painted oak with its paint flaking in sheets; the wall is the other, plaster over lath, and into the plaster people have cut doorways and windows and stairs. Candle-ends burn in niches. Washing hangs on thread. There is a smell of soup and tallow and three hundred years of other people’s dust, and a great many people, all talking quietly, the way people talk in a house where somebody is ill. Somebody has chalked a skull on the board every few yards, and under each skull a heap of bread spread with grey paste has been swept into a sack. The sack has POISON on it, and DON’T, in a child’s capitals.
[To the Hearth] The Hearth: a town in a cold grate, where eighty colliers live in the ash of the Whitlocks’ last fire. The ash of the Whitlocks’ last fire, three hundred years old, has been dug into streets; the cinders are walls; on the iron fire-dogs, which are as tall as gallows, people have built galleries of slate and hung lanterns. The coal-heap at the back is a hill with pits sunk in it and a windlass over each. Up the black throat of the chimney you can see, far up, a square of sky, and ledges in the soot with doors. In the great grate, on the irons, somebody has laid logs as long as boats crosswise over a bed of kindling, with coal heaped round them: a fire, ready, waiting for a match. The colliers walk round the laid fire without looking at it, the way you walk round something in a dream. Here are Bet Collier, the forewoman of the Hearth; and Nan Collier, Bet Collier’s daughter.
[Talk to Bet Collier] A square black-faced dwarf-woman with a lamp on her cap straightens up from a windlass. ‘You’re not from the grate,’ she says. ‘You’re not from anywhere, by your boots. What do you want in the Hearth?’
[Warn Bet Collier that the fire will be lit in the morning] Bet Collier hears you out with her arms folded. ‘At nine,’ she says. ‘By her woman’s own mouth.’ She looks at the logs on the irons, as if for the first time. Then she puts two fingers in her mouth and whistles, and the whole grate goes still. ‘Everybody out,’ she says, not loud. ‘Children first, then the old, then what you can carry. Up the wall-way to the Skirting. Now.’ And as the Hearth begins to move round her, she turns back to you: ‘If you’re wrong, I’ll have your hide. If you’re right, I owe you eighty-three.’
[Take your leave] Bet Collier has gone back to work before you have turned round.
[To the Skirting] Behind the skirting board, in the dark between the board and the wall, a street.
[To the Button Market] Where the skirting board has warped away from the wall the street widens into a market: stalls under awnings of envelope paper, a well that is a giant’s thimble sunk in the floor and kept full from the Sink, and everywhere buttons. Buttons as tables, as wheels, as roofs, as shields; buttons in a bin, sorted by size, smallest first; and at the far end a long counter made of a single bone button, where Ottoline Fogg sits under a sign that says BUTTONS BOUGHT, OUTSIDE GOODS SOLD, NO CREDIT, NO CHEESE. Ottoline Fogg, the button-factor of the Wainscot, is here.
[To the Nail] The Nail is an inn built round a nail: a giant’s iron nail as thick as an oak, driven through the lath into a stud three hundred years ago and rusted to the colour of bread-crust. Rooms are hung off it on brackets, up and round, like nests. Below, a long table, a hearth made of a giant’s tinder-box, a smell of mutton broth. Over the hearth, a portrait cut out of a giant’s newspaper: a man in a periwig, captioned THE LATE KING ALDO, whom nobody living here has any idea about. Mab Tenter, who keeps the Nail, is here.
[Talk to Mab Tenter] A thin woman with a black ribbon on her sleeve looks up from ladling. ‘Bed’s a button a night, or a coin, the broth comes with it. Don’t go out on the flagstones after nine.’ She says it the way people say a thing they say to everyone. Then, differently: ‘Don’t.’
[Take a bed and supper] At six o’clock on the first evening the Lady came down the Stair, slowly, a step at a time, with Mrs Pyecroft on her shoulder and a candle in her hand like a burning tree, and the whole Hall went still. She crossed the flagstones to the Long Clock, opened its trunk, felt inside for the key, and wound it, forty turns, each turn a groan of brass through the house. Then she set the hands by her watch, and shut the trunk, and stood back; and the Clock struck six. It had not struck since the Going. The sound went into every wall in the house, and the Wainscot stood in its doorways and counted the strokes, six, and then stood there after, in the silence, as if waiting for a seventh. You eat what is put in front of you, and sleep long, under a roof.

The far wall is so far off it is blue. Up it climbs the Stair, a flight of cliffs going up into the dimness, and against the east wall stands a tall clock-case like a tower, its face high up, its hands stopped at a little after four. [To the kitchen floor] The kitchen of Hollins is a stone barn of a room, flagged, with a range along one wall like the front of a foundry, black and cold, its oven doors big enough to drive a cart into. A table runs down the middle that a village could shelter under, and does: there are tents under it. Along the far wall, the stone sink, high up, with a brass tap over it, and from the tap a drop forms, slowly, and falls, and forms. By the door a box of wood hangs in a shaft in the wall, on ropes: the dumbwaiter. Somebody has chalked on the flagstones, in a fair round hand: KEEP OFF THE OPEN AFTER NINE. The dumbwaiter’s box hangs in its shaft on ropes as thick as your arm. The Jarmen work the ropes from the kitchen end, and nobody rides it but by their leave.
[To the Pantry door] Four men behind the comb-gate bring their pins down level, eight feet of steel apiece. ‘Pantry’s the Jarl’s,’ says the biggest. ‘You’ve business with him, say so. Or you’ve honey to buy, there’s a hatch. Otherwise go back the way you came, and quick.’ The Jarl’s pin-men will wait 10 minutes more, no longer. The pantry door stands an inch ajar, which is a passage a cart could go through, and across the gap the Jarmen have built a gate out of a giant’s comb set on its back, its teeth for bars. Behind it, men with pins for spears. The comb-gate is shut across the pantry door. The Jarl’s pin-men, Jarmen, with giant’s pins for spears, are here.
[Tell the pin-men you have business with the Jarl] The biggest of them looks you over, and jerks his head. ‘He’ll see you. He sees everyone this week. Mind his boy.’ The comb-gate stands open.
[To the Jarl's hall in the Pantry] Something is lying in wait in the back of the Pantry: on the way in the ground is scuffed where something has lain a long while, and nothing there makes a sound. The Pantry is a cave of shelves going up into the dark, and on them the stores of Hollins, three hundred years old: crocks of honey as big as wine-vats, with doors cut in their sides; sacks of flour like grey hills; a ham hanging from a hook, black and hard as wood; jars of salt; a cheese that has turned to stone and been carved into a throne. The Jarmen live on the lowest shelf, among the jars, in rooms cut into the honey-crocks, and the whole place smells of honey, and mice, and men. The comb-gate stands open. [Wait › An hour] At noon the Lady, at the kitchen table, gets up and goes to the sink and turns the tap; and the water comes down out of the brass like a river out of a cliff, white, roaring, and fills the Sink in the time it takes to say so. [Wait › An hour] Now the house is warm all through, and the Lady sleeps more and works less, and Mrs Pyecroft goes about with her lips pressed together. In the Wainscot people have begun to pack, without saying so: a bundle by the door, a child’s shoes laced together. Everyone can feel that something is coming to an end, and nobody can say what.
[Wait › An hour] At ten o’clock Mrs Pyecroft goes down the Hall in her nightcap with a taper, and lights three fat yellow candles on three saucers, in the Hall, in the drawing room, in the kitchen, and goes out, and shuts the doors, which close like the end of the world. The smoke goes down. It goes down the way water goes down: along the flagstones, under the skirting, into the cracks, the grate, the pantry, the Nail. For a while there is coughing in the walls of the house, everywhere, all at once. Then there is less.
[Wait › An hour] The smoke finds you too. It comes along the floor yellow and slow, and where it touches it burns, eyes, throat, lungs; you are on your knees before you know it, and the way up is a long way off. Wren resists (constitution).
[Wait › An hour] On the tenth evening the Lady did not ring for her supper, and Mrs Pyecroft came down the ribbon from her hatbox and sat on the counterpane by her hand, and did not go back up. The house knows. The Clock goes on. The fire in her room has been let burn low.
[Wait › An hour] The Tin Regiment stands in its Fort where it stopped, every soldier at his post, the Captain’s sword pointing at the door. Nobody winds them. Fennick sits in the ark. The Smoking came on the night the ratcatcher’s word reached Mrs Pyecroft. The Post’s register ends that night, in mid-line. She died on the tenth night, in a quiet house. Mrs Pyecroft paid the ratcatcher’s account in full, with a bonus, and wrote a character for them in a fair hand: thorough, discreet, and successful. It is quite true. What the Wainscot calls them, those of it who are left, is not written anywhere; but it is said in Bootscrape, at the Mat, to children, as a warning.
