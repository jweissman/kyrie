[To Bootscrape street] Bootscrape lives on a giant’s doorstep and makes a living from what falls out of the letterbox. It has done so for three hundred years without once telling anyone from outside what it knows. Four days ago a wagon the size of an inn came over the moor, and a woman as tall as a church tower got down from it and walked up the step and put a key in the door, and since then the village has been very quiet, and has watched the chimney. On the door of the inn, the Mat, a notice in a clear, pinched hand: WANTED, A RATCATCHER, FOR HOLLINS, TWO CROWNS THE DAY, APPLY WITHIN, TEN UNTIL TWO. Under that: THE HOUSE BEING OVERRUN; TWOPENCE THE TAIL. D. PYECROFT. Somebody has drawn a very small face in the corner of it, with its finger to its lips. Bootscrape street runs along the foot of the doorstep, under the scraper: an iron arch thirty feet high, rusted to the colour of a fox, with a forge built into one leg of it and washing hung from the crossbar. The houses are built of the ordinary stone of the hills, and roofed, a good many of them, with things that are not slates: a sheet of blackened brass, half a horn button the size of a millwheel, a shard of green glass that throws a green light into somebody’s kitchen. A rope ladder hangs down the face of the step to the lip, sixty feet up, where the brass of the letterbox shines.
[To Ludd's yard, under the scraper] Under the leg of the scraper, where the iron goes into the ground, a salvager’s yard: a steel pin as long as a pike leaning on a rack with a dozen more, a horn button as wide as a cart wheel being sawn into combs, a copper rivet in a vice, and in a barrel by the gate, standing upright like arrows in a quiver, a great many rat-tails on strings. Hob Ludd, a salvager under the scraper, is here.
[Talk to Hob Ludd] A wiry man with a jeweller’s glass screwed into one eye is filing a pin. ‘Buying or selling? Or neither, which is the commonest,’ he says, without looking up. ‘Everything in this yard came out of the Slot, honest, for good bread and better needles.’
[Ask Hob Ludd how to get into the house] ‘By the Slot, like an honest body,’ says Hob. ‘Up the ladder, through the letterbox, and the Post’ll have you down the other side. The ladder’s mine and it’s a penny.’ He considers you. ‘Or round the north side to the kitchen garden, and down the coal-hole, if you don’t want the Post to know you’ve come. It comes out in the cellars, in the dark.’ He goes back to his pin. ‘I wouldn’t, myself. There’s people in the cellars that don’t come up.’
[Buy › A giant's pin, for a spear] You pay Hob Ludd, and take a giant’s pin, for a spear.
[Take your leave] ‘Don’t let me keep you,’ says Hob Ludd, who was not trying to.

[To the coal cellar] Inside, the house is a country. It is cold, as it has been cold for three hundred years, except where she has lit fires, and dim, except where she has opened shutters; and everywhere, in the walls, in the grate, on the shelves, the people who came in by the letterbox are going about their lives without a sound, listening, as they have listened every day since Thursday, for the floor to shake. The coal cellar: a heap of coal like a black hill under the hole in the roof, with grey light falling on it from above, and footprints across the dust where a good many people, over a long time, have come down the heap and gone on through the arch.

[To the foot of the cellar steps] At the foot of the cellar steps, which are each a fall of eight feet with a ladder lashed to it, the dark opens out under vaults of brick. It is cold, and still, and smells of earth and old wine. Somewhere water drips into water.
[To the wine-bins] Something is lying in wait in the edge of the cistern: on the way in the ground is scuffed where something has lain a long while, and nothing there makes a sound. The wine-bins: brick cells in rows, each as big as a chapel, each racked with bottles lying on their sides like great green cannons. In the cells people are living, in the dark, among pale mushrooms grown on the straw. Nobody carries a light. Voices come from everywhere, quiet. Blind Annet, the eldest of the Under-folk, is here.
[Talk to Blind Annet] A voice out of the dark, old and dry, very close: ‘You’ve a light. Put it down; it hurts the children.’ A hand finds your sleeve, light as a moth. ‘I’m Annet. They call me Blind, which is a joke, since there’s nothing down here to see. What brings an upstairs body to the bins?’
[Ask Blind Annet who the Under-folk are] ‘The Candle Riot,’ says Annet. ‘A hundred years. The Court put a tax on candle-ends, and my grandmother’s people went up the Stair and broke the Small House windows, and the Court sent them down here, and said stay.’ She laughs. ‘We stayed. We like it. It’s quiet.’ Then, not laughing: ‘But we came into Hollins by the coal-hole, my grandmother’s grandmother, in the Hungry Winter of Forty. The grating was open. So the Post never wrote us down. So we’re not in the book. So Drewe gives us no water, and we drink the cistern, and the children are sick of it every summer.’ Her hand tightens on your sleeve. ‘Six hundred in the house, they say. They don’t count us. Forty-one. We’re not anybody.’

[To the kitchen floor] The kitchen of Hollins is a stone barn of a room, flagged, with a range along one wall like the front of a foundry, black and cold, its oven doors big enough to drive a cart into. A table runs down the middle that a village could shelter under, and does: there are tents under it. Along the far wall, the stone sink, high up, with a brass tap over it, and from the tap a drop forms, slowly, and falls, and forms. By the door a box of wood hangs in a shaft in the wall, on ropes: the dumbwaiter. Somebody has chalked on the flagstones, in a fair round hand: KEEP OFF THE OPEN AFTER NINE. The dumbwaiter’s box hangs in its shaft on ropes as thick as your arm. The Jarmen work the ropes from the kitchen end, and nobody rides it but by their leave.
[To the Hall floor] The Hall of Hollins. The floor is flagstones of grey stone, each as wide as a threshing-floor, with grass-wide cracks between them where people have planted cress. The far wall is so far off it is blue. Up it climbs the Stair, a flight of cliffs going up into the dimness, and against the east wall stands a tall clock-case like a tower, its face high up, its hands stopped at a little after four. Along the foot of every wall runs the skirting, a moulded board as high as a cottage, with doors cut in it.
[To the Letterdrift] Below the letterbox lies the Letterdrift: three hundred years of letters, fallen through the slot onto the hall floor and never picked up, heaped into a dune as high as a barn and as long as a street. The letters are a giant’s letters, each the size of a bedsheet, folded; their seals are cartwheels of red wax. Paths go over and into the drift, and in it people have dug rooms, roofed with envelopes, lit by candle-ends, with addresses for walls: MISS E. WHITLOCK, HOLLINS; THE LADY WHITLOCK, BY HAND; and a great many bills. In the face of the drift a counter has been built out of a giant’s postcard on trestles. Pigeonholes are cut into the paper behind it, labelled in a clear hand: NEWS, OLD; NEWS, OLDER; LOVE; MONEY; DEATHS; ILLEGIBLE. Faith Penny, the Postmistress of the Wainscot, is here.
[Talk to Faith Penny] A halfling woman in a green cap with POST on it in brass letters looks up from a register the size of a cottage door, laid flat on trestles. ‘Name, place of origin, purpose of visit,’ she says, and dips a pen. ‘Everyone who comes in by the Slot is entered. We have entered everyone for two hundred and ninety years.’ She waits. ‘And you’ve no letters for anyone, I suppose. Nobody ever has letters.’
[Ask Faith Penny to enter the Under-folk in the register] Faith Penny’s pen stops. ‘They came in by the coal-hole,’ she says. ‘The register is of all who come in by the Slot.’ She looks at the register, at its two hundred and ninety years. Then she turns to the first page, the very first, where in a faded hand it says what the Post is for, and reads it under her breath, and stops. ‘All who come in,’ she says. ‘It says all who come in. It doesn’t say by the Slot.’ She sits very still. ‘Ninety years we’ve been saying by the Slot, and it doesn’t say it.’ She dips the pen. ‘Forty-one, you said. Give me the names, if she’ll give them.’ And then, fiercely, to the register: ‘The Post is not a gate.’ Tamsin gains 75 experience: had the Under-folk counted.

[Wait › An hour] At six o’clock on the first evening the Lady came down the Stair, slowly, a step at a time, with Mrs Pyecroft on her shoulder and a candle in her hand like a burning tree, and the whole Hall went still. She crossed the flagstones to the Long Clock, opened its trunk, felt inside for the key, and wound it, forty turns, each turn a groan of brass through the house. Then she set the hands by her watch, and shut the trunk, and stood back; and the Clock struck six. It had not struck since the Going. The sound went into every wall in the house, and the Wainscot stood in its doorways and counted the strokes, six, and then stood there after, in the silence, as if waiting for a seventh.
