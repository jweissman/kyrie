# The Sea Is Late: the story, before the code

*Pass 1. Fiction first; the Kyr that follows is a reading of it.*

## 1. The chalk

Every morning for nineteen years Hesper Aldous has climbed the harbour wall at Landfall with a stub of chalk and written one word on the tide-slate, which is the size of a door and older than she is.

OUT.

Below the wall lies the harbour: thirty feet of cracked grey mud, a slipway running down into nothing, and the ribs of a pilot-boat that the gulls gave up on in the second summer. The sea left Landfall in one night. It went out past the Tern Light at dusk, taking the fishing fleet's moorings with it like a cloth drawn off a table, and it has not been seen from the wall since.

"People ask what I'm waiting for," says Hesper. "I'm not waiting. I'm recording. There is a difference, and it is nineteen years wide."

She trims the harbour lamp every dusk, too. "A light is not hope. A light is a habit."

## 2. The bucket

Tamsin Lundy was born the night the sea left. She is nineteen and has never seen water that was not in a bucket.

She sits on the lip of the quay with her boots over thirty feet of nothing, and pours a cup from the bucket down the wall, and watches where it goes. "It goes *down*," she says. "Every time. My grandmother says the sea goes down as well, only further." Her grandmother, Samphire Gannel, keeps an alehouse in a stranded ship twelve miles out on the flat, and sent Tamsin ashore at six to be brought up among people who still believed in tomorrow. "I'd like to be disappointed by it properly," says Tamsin. "Everyone says it's bigger than they thought."

At the bottom of the slipway a dark stain has appeared overnight, a hand wide. She has put her finger in it. She holds the finger up to you, solemnly. It tastes of salt.

"Rain," says Hesper, from the wall, without turning.

"It's rained *in* it," says Tamsin. "It hasn't rained *up*."

## 3. The pilot who cried

Out on the old island, now only a rise in a plain of mud, Ketil Auger has told Landfall that the sea is coming back every spring for nineteen years. He is the Tern Light's last pilot. He lies on his belly each dusk with an ear on a copper bowl he has sunk in the floor, and he hears something that is not there yet.

"It's a draw," he says. "Like a long breath taken. Nineteen springs I've said it and nineteen springs I've been wrong, and I was a day early or a month late, and I'll be right the once. It will be the once that matters. You are not going to believe me. Good. Believe the clams."

The clams are the flat's one secret. Under the mud lie beds of barrel-sized shellfish that closed the night of the Ebb and have not stirred since. A fortnight ago one opened a finger's width. Then ten. Then the whole garden went *tick*, once, together, and shut.

## 4. The Fleet

Twelve miles out on the dry sea, five ships sit where the water left them. Nobody has made them leave. Over nineteen years they have become streets.

*Cormorant*, a Marchbank brig with a keel like a church beam, is an alehouse called the Wet Bar, which is the joke the Fleet has told itself since the second year. It has aged well. Samphire Gannel runs it with a ladle and a grievance. "It left," she says. "Left. I wasn't *finished* being angry."

*Brightwork* is a hulk stripped to its ribs. Grist Skellow's salvagers have taken forty wrecks to pieces for nails, brass, lead pigs, anchors and one marble bath, and the hoard lies in her hold under tarpaulin. Skellow is large, merry, and has knocked on every timber on the flat. He knows exactly which ships are sound. *Brightwork* is lace. He is not telling his crew.

*Pleasance*, a flat-bottomed pleasure-barge with a stage on her deck, is the home of the Pleasance Players, who have spent nineteen years rehearsing "The Return of the Waters," a masque of nineteen hundred couplets with a sea made of blue silk and ten boys. Philomel Thistlewood has written the Return nineteen times. "Never once the morning after," she says. "I do not know what people *say* afterwards."

## 5. The crossing

You can walk the Shell Road, laid by the Fleet in oyster-shell over nineteen years: four hours across, a mile of it under a sky with nothing in it to be afraid of, and then every mile is exactly the same.

Or you can go with Orlo Carrick, who sails a land-cutter called the *Hare* across the marl in forty minutes when the wind is up. The marl is a skin of dry mud over a bog, and it will hold a cutter at speed and nothing else. "You don't stop," says Orlo. "That's the whole instruction. People ask for more. There isn't more."

## 6. What the sea will do

It will come at dusk on the third day, and it will come at a gallop. Ketil knows this from the Draw; Skellow knows it from the clams. A man on foot on the flat is a man who will be found in the spring.

The sound ships will float. Nobody who lives in them has ever been on a floating ship.

*Pleasance* will roll. She is a theatre, top-heavy as a wedding cake. She wants ballast, and in the hold of the rotten *Brightwork*, under a tarpaulin, lie sixty tons of lead pigs, anchors and one marble bath.

Skellow is planning to take *Cormorant* at the last hour, with his crew and his hoard, by force. He has not told them. Sloe, who is eleven and carries his bucket, has not stopped telling everyone.

## 7. The Return

At dusk the flat begins to click. From the Light you can see it: a pale line along the edge of the world that is not a cloud, and below it the clam gardens opening in their thousands, white and wet, one after another toward you like a wave of applause. Then the sound, which is not surf. It is the sound a room makes when everyone stops talking at once.

The ships lift in the order they are sound. *Cormorant* goes first, rolling, her whole alehouse shrieking and a small woman at her taffrail screaming at the sea for being *late*. *Pleasance* goes level, ballasted with lead and a marble bath, and from her deck a voice and a trumpet go out across the water: the masque, with a new last couplet. *Brightwork* goes down by the stern, slowly, like a gentleman, while her crew stand in the shallows with their hoard on their backs, laughing.

At Landfall the harbour fills from the sea end. Hesper Aldous is on the wall with the chalk. She wipes the slate with her sleeve and writes one word.

## What I want the player to carry away

That a long wait does not end as expected and the wait was part of the people it made. That it is possible to be *angry* at a thing for nineteen years and still run to meet it. That heavy things which seemed worthless can keep a stage afloat. The reaction of a nineteen-year-old to something enormous that is, honestly, only wet.
